In Japan a menu, a shop window or a convenience-store shelf will often tell you what season it is before the weather does. Behind that sits one word, *shun* (旬), the time when an ingredient is at its best. This page is about what the word means in the official sources, how it has survived greenhouses and year-round supply, and how chains turn the season into a limited-edition product with a start date and a price. The two lineage pages go through the year: spring and summer, then autumn and winter.

Food tied to a date rather than a season — osechi at New Year, beans at Setsubun, the dishes of Hinamatsuri, pumpkin at the winter solstice — is a different subject, and has its own overview of food for annual events.

## What it is

The farm ministry's *washoku* learning pages say *shun* is used mainly for ingredients such as fish, shellfish and vegetables, and describe it as the peak period of an ingredient, when it is at its tastiest and most nutritious. The same page says that feeling the change of the four seasons through these ingredients is part of the appeal of Japanese cooking.

The ministry's food-education page for children puts it more simply: shun is the season when vegetables and fruit grown normally in nature are harvested, or when a fish is caught in large numbers. Its recipe page adds that food taken in its season is relatively cheap and high in nutrients.

**Not one moment but three.** The washoku page describes shun as a sequence:

- ***Hashiri*** — the first of the season, eaten to get ahead of it.
- ***Shun*** — the peak.
- ***Nagori*** — the time after the peak, eaten with regret that the season is ending.

The ministry presents this as the Japanese way of thinking about shun: enjoying different flavours from the same ingredient across the year. For vegetables, it says, many are tender and good raw at the *hashiri* stage, and many have a deeper flavour and sweetness by *nagori*. Its example is autumn aubergine.

## Why it is the way it is

**Year-round supply did not abolish it.** The washoku page says plant breeding and better distribution mean more ingredients are now on sale all year, and that enjoying the changes of nature and feeling the season in food is still one of the pleasures of Japanese cooking. The children's page is more specific about what year-round means: many foods sold regardless of season are grown in heated greenhouses or are imported. The recipe page says the same about greenhouse growing.

**The calendar is a separate thing.** Japan's old calendar divides the year into 24 seasonal markers, the *nijūshi sekki*. The National Astronomical Observatory's calendar wiki describes them as divisions of the year based on the sun's position, arriving about every 15 days, with dates that move a little from year to year; it also notes that China's knowledge and practice of the 24 solar terms was inscribed as UNESCO intangible cultural heritage in 2016. There is a finer division into 72 *kō*. No official source links either the 24 markers or the 72 *kō* to what is in season. The ministry's pages on shun do not mention them. So this site treats the calendar as a marker of the season and shun as the peak of an ingredient, and does not connect them.

**The shops sell the season, and often early.** Here the official sources are thin. No public or company source explains why Japanese chains build so much around limited seasonal products. What can be checked is how the companies describe their own:

- **McDonald's Japan, *tsukimi* (moon-viewing).** Its news release of 26 August 2026 says the *Tsukimi* family of products went on sale nationwide (some shops excepted) on 2 September 2026 for a limited period. End dates differ by item, from late September to late October. The Tsukimi Burger dates from 1991, and the company marks 2026 as its 35th year. A 2017 company article presents it as an autumn standard and a traditional taste of the company. Where the name came from and how it was launched in 1991, the company pages do not say.
- **Starbucks Japan, cherry blossom.** A press release of 12 February 2026 announced three seasonal-limited sakura drinks from 18 February 2026. It gives no end date, warns of temporary shortages or an early end, and says the drinks were planned both for people who like the taste of sakura and for people who simply want the sakura mood.
- **Starbucks Japan, sweet potato.** Drinks and a cake made with *beni haruka* sweet potato went on sale on 2 September 2026. The release describes domestic beni haruka as coming into season, and says that because September is still hot it chose a chilled flavour rather than the hot, floury baked potato. Again, no end date.
- **Seven-Eleven Japan, autumn.** A release of 19 September 2025 announced an autumn fair of 30 products using in-season sweet potato, chestnut, mushrooms, pumpkin and more, in two waves from 23 and 30 September, describing it as a limited, now-only food experience. That is the 2025 fair; a 2026 version is not confirmed, so its prices are not given here.

Put together, these releases show autumn products arriving in early September and spring sakura drinks in mid-February. That they sell the season ahead of the calendar is this site's reading of the dates, not something the companies say.

## What it costs

Only prices that the companies state as tax included are given, as posted in the releases and checked on the date at the top of this page. Starbucks gives two prices for each item, takeaway and eat-in, which follows Japan's two consumption tax rates — 8% for takeaway, 10% for eating in, as of 2026-10-10.

- **McDonald's Japan, autumn 2026.** Tsukimi Burger from ¥460 (value set from ¥760), Cheese Tsukimi from ¥490, Tsukimi Muffin from ¥420. The release says all prices include tax and that prices in some shops may differ by location.
- **Starbucks Japan, autumn 2026.** *Torori mitsu-imo beni haruka* Frappuccino, Tall ¥687 takeaway / ¥700 eat-in; the matching mousse tea latte ¥628 / ¥640; a baked sweet-potato cake made with beni haruka and *annō-imo* ¥530 / ¥540.
- **Starbucks Japan, spring 2026.** *Sakura saku yo* white peach Frappuccino, Tall ¥687 / ¥700; *Sakura saku yo* latte ¥618 / ¥630; *Sakura saku yo* white peach soda ¥579 / ¥590. These were the spring 2026 range; the release gave a start date only.

Shun produce in markets and supermarkets has no single price, and no official source gives one.

## Where to eat it

There is no one place. The chain products above are sold nationwide (McDonald's says some shops are excepted), for a limited time. For produce, the lineage pages name the places the ministry's own sources mention — Kyushu for the first bamboo shoots, Kyoto for its autumn matsutake rice, Nagano for new-season soba.

## The same name, a different place

The ministry's sources show that shun moves with place and variety. Bamboo shoots start in Kyushu around March and the harvest moves north until around May, with early digging in southern Kagoshima from around December. Buckwheat, a Nagano page says, has two new-season crops a year, called *natsu-shin* and *aki-shin*.

Tokyo's side is shown by its central wholesale market. Its children's page (with figures from 2008) says the top three sources of vegetables for Tokyo's central wholesale markets were Hokkaido, Chiba and Ibaraki, and for fruit Ehime, Aomori and the Philippines. Produce comes in at night, when the roads are empty, and goes to auction from about 7 a.m. Apples harvested from September to November are stored until about June or July of the next year. Growers use greenhouses and other methods to shift their shipping time to when the market has little and prices are high. The market also publishes a "relay" of the main producing areas month by month for 13 vegetables, but as animations and PDFs, so this page does not give which area supplies which month; bamboo shoots are not among the 13.

Official sources do not give how Tokyo supermarkets handle out-of-season produce, how many weeks the season shifts between a producing area and Tokyo, or how prices differ between them. This page also does not say that produce tastes better where it is grown; the ministry's definition of shun is about the time of year, not about place.

## What people get wrong

- **"Shun is a single date."** The ministry describes three stages, *hashiri*, *shun* and *nagori*, and treats each as worth eating.
- **"Year-round food means shun no longer matters."** The ministry says year-round supply has grown through breeding, distribution, greenhouses and imports, and still describes feeling the season in food as one of the pleasures of Japanese cooking.
- **"The 24 seasonal markers tell you what to eat."** No official source links them to ingredients.
- **"A seasonal limited product is the same as shun."** Chains choose their own start and end dates; Starbucks put its autumn sweet-potato drinks on sale in early September and made them cold because it was still hot.
- **"Seasonal food means festival food."** Shun is about ingredients at their peak. Food for particular days of the year is a separate tradition with its own overview.
