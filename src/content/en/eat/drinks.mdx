Most of what confuses visitors about drinks in Japan is not taste. It is paperwork. Why is there "beer", "happoshu" and something called "new genre" on the same shelf? Why can a 19-year-old vote but not order a highball? How many bottles can you carry in? All three answers come from the law, and the law is clearer than the shelf. This page is the map: how Japan classifies alcohol, the age rule, what customs allows, and how the two lineage pages — shōchū, and beer with the highball — fit in. Sake has its own overview and is not repeated here; for how a night in an izakaya runs, start from the izakaya overview.

## What it is

In Japanese tax law, *sake-rui* — "alcoholic drinks" — means any drink of 1% alcohol or more. The National Tax Agency adds that this includes things you can dilute with water, or powders you can dissolve, to reach 1% or more. Industrial alcohol and licensed medicines that contain alcohol are excluded.

The Liquor Tax Act then sorts every drink into **four kinds**, by how it is made and what it is like, and taxes each kind differently. The four are split again into **17 items**. In the agency's table:

- **Sparkling** — beer, happoshu, and "other sparkling drinks" (sparkling drinks that are neither beer nor happoshu, under 11% alcohol; the limit was under 10% until 30 September 2026).
- **Brewed** — *seishu* (sake), fruit wine, and other brewed drinks.
- **Distilled** — continuous-still shōchū, single-still shōchū, whisky, brandy, raw alcohol, and spirits.
- **Mixed** — synthetic sake, *mirin*, sweet fruit wine, liqueurs, powdered alcohol, and miscellaneous.

Two things are worth knowing before you read a Japanese label.

**There are two ways of sorting, and they do not match.** The national brewers' association points out that the words *jōzōshu* (brewed), *jōryūshu* (distilled) and *konseishu* (mixed) are used both for a classification by method and for the tax classification. A drink can be "brewed" by method and still sit in the "sparkling" tax kind — beer is the obvious case. When a Japanese source says "brewed drinks", check which list it means.

**One limit has just moved.** The tax agency's table gives the new 11% ceiling for "other sparkling drinks"; a finance ministry page still says under 10%. The documents were updated at different times. This page follows the tax agency, and does not try to place every canned chūhai on one side of the line.

## Why it is the way it is

**Tax sets the categories, and the government says the categories shaped the products.** Each kind has a basic rate and each item its own rate. That is why the beer shelf has three names on it: beer, happoshu, and the drinks the industry calls "new genre". That last name is not a legal term. The tax agency's leaflet calls it "happoshu ② (the so-called new genre)".

In its 2017 reform the government said that tax gaps between similar drinks were affecting product development and sales volumes, and set out to restore fairness in the tax burden without changing total revenue. It did so in steps from October 2020. The finance ministry's summary:

- **Beer-type drinks** — beer, happoshu and the new genre — were brought to a single rate in **October 2026**: ¥155,000 per kilolitre, which the ministry gives as ¥54.25 per 350 ml.
- **Brewed drinks** such as sake and fruit wine were brought to a single rate of ¥100,000 per kilolitre in October 2023.
- **Other sparkling drinks**, including chūhai, went up to ¥100,000 per kilolitre (¥35 per 350 ml) in October 2026.

In practice, the 1 October 2026 change lowered the tax on beer and raised it on happoshu and the new genre. What that did to shelf prices is up to each maker and shop, and I have not seen the makers' own price announcements, so I give no figures.

## Age and ID

**The age is 20.** A law of 1922 bans drinking by anyone under 20. It tells parents and guardians to stop it when they know of it, bars businesses from selling or serving alcohol to anyone they know will drink it under 20, and requires them to check age or take other necessary steps. The Tokyo Regional Taxation Bureau publishes the text.

**Adulthood moved to 18; drinking did not.** The Civil Code age of adulthood became 18 on 1 April 2022. In March 2022 the National Police Agency, the health ministry and the tax agency renewed their request to the retail trade: the drinking age is still 20. The request asks shops to check the age of anyone who seems under 20 by asking for a document showing age — the examples given are a driving licence or a My Number card — to check at self-checkouts by staff sight, to display posters, to train staff, and to manage alcohol vending machines.

**What that means for a visitor, I could not confirm.** None of the official pages I read says whether a foreign visitor will be asked for a passport, or how often a convenience store, izakaya or bar actually checks. The national tourism organisation, JNTO, gives the legal drinking age as 20, and on a separate page advises carrying your passport at all times; it says you are unlikely to be asked for it, but may be.

## Bringing alcohol into Japan

Japan Customs allows each traveller, for goods judged to be for personal use, **three bottles of alcohol, counted as 760 ml each, free of duty**. If you have both hand luggage and goods sent separately, they are added together. Travellers **under 20 get no duty-free allowance** for alcohol or tobacco.

What tax applies from the fourth bottle, and whether strength changes the count, was not on the customs page I read. Ask customs if you are carrying more.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Shōchū is two drinks in law.** Continuous-still shōchū (under 36%) and single-still shōchū (45% or less) are separate tax items. Only some single-still shōchū may be called *honkaku*, "authentic". The official sources describe the difference as one of distillation and how much of the ingredient’s aroma survives — not of quality.
- **Region names on shōchū are protected.** Iki, Kuma, Ryūkyū and Satsuma are geographical indications for distilled spirits, and since 2024 so is Tokyo Shimazake, the shōchū of the Izu Islands.
- **"Japanese whisky" is an industry standard, not a law.** The Japan Spirits and Liqueurs Makers Association set its own labelling rule in 2021. The tax law defines whisky by ingredients and distillation only. There is no Japanese whisky GI on the tax agency’s list.
- **The highball came back through izakaya.** By Suntory’s own account, its 2008 revival worked through bars and restaurants, not distilleries.

## Where to drink it

- **Izakaya** — the everyday place for all of it; see the izakaya overview.
- **Shōchū country** — Kagoshima and southern Miyazaki for sweet potato, Iki and Ōita for barley, Kuma for rice, the Amami Islands for brown sugar, Okinawa for awamori, the Izu Islands for Tokyo’s own. Kirishima Shuzo in Miyakonojō, Miyazaki, runs a factory tour by reservation.
- **Distilleries and breweries** — Suntory’s Yamazaki Distillery in Osaka prefecture, and Asahi’s museum at its Suita brewery, both by reservation. The lineage pages give the rules.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## The same name, a different place

Drinking at the source and drinking in a Tokyo izakaya differ in ways the official sources do show, though none of them says one is better.

- **At the source, you book.** Suntory’s tours need a reservation except for walk-in courses, some by lottery; tours with tasting cannot be combined on the same day. Asahi’s Suita tour is by reservation. Kirishima’s tour is booked by the day before. An izakaya you just walk into.
- **At the source, tasting has conditions.** Asahi gives no alcohol to drivers (on a motorbike, the passenger too), cyclists, or anyone under 20; they get soft drinks. Suntory’s tours are guided in Japanese only, with a free audio-guide app at Yamazaki and translation help on request at other sites.
- **Some habits travel, some stay home.** The shōchū makers’ information site says *maewari* — shōchū diluted with water and rested for a night to a week — is popular in Kyushu as a drink for guests and celebrations. Kirishima Shuzo says 20% shōchū is widely drunk in Miyazaki; that is a company’s statement. The highball went the other way: Suntory says it spread from izakaya outward.
- **What Tokyo pours, I could not document.** I found no official source on what Tokyo izakaya stock, so I make no claim that you will find every region’s shōchū there.

## What people get wrong

- **"Adults can drink at 18 now."** Adulthood moved to 18 in 2022. Drinking stayed at 20.
- **"New genre is a kind of beer."** It is an industry name; in law it is a kind of happoshu, and beer is a separate item.
- **"Brewed means not sparkling."** Beer is brewed by method and "sparkling" for tax. Japanese uses the same words for both ways of sorting.
- **"Kōrui shōchū is the cheap, worse kind."** The official sources describe a different still and a lighter carry-over of aroma, nothing more.
- **"Japanese whisky is defined by law."** It is defined by a makers’ association’s voluntary standard.
- **"You can bring in as much as you like for yourself."** Duty-free is three 760 ml bottles, and none for anyone under 20.
