Chanko nabe is the hotpot of sumo: a big pot of chicken stock, seasoned with soy sauce or miso, cooked for a room of large men who need to eat a lot. That much is simple. The word is not. Inside sumo, *chanko* is not the name of a pot at all, and where the word came from is still open. This page sets out what the farm ministry and the Japan Sumo Association say, side by side.

For soups and hotpots in general, start from the [soups and hotpots overview](/en/eat/nabe/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry says chanko nabe is said to have begun in the Meiji era. It places the dish in Sumida ward in Tokyo.

The word has two families of theories, and the Japan Sumo Association, in a quiz on its own site, says none of them is settled:

- **A pot.** *Chanko* comes from a Chinese sheet-metal pot, the *sha-ko* (written with the characters for "sand pot"), which reached Nagasaki.
- **A father.** *Chanko* comes from *chan*, an affectionate word for father. The association gives three versions of why: the stable master and his apprentices ate together like a family; the older man who did the cooking was fondly called that; or the 19th *yokozuna*, Hitachiyama, called the veteran wrestler in charge of the kitchen *chankō*, written with the character for father.

This page does not choose between them, and neither does the association.

## What makes it what it is

**Chanko is whatever a wrestler cooks.** The association is plain about it: everything a sumo wrestler makes is chanko, not only hotpot. Curry made by a wrestler is chanko too. *Chanko nabe* is the hotpot form, the one people outside sumo know.

**Built to eat in quantity.** The farm ministry says the pot lets wrestlers take in a large amount of food at one sitting, which suits the work of building a body for sumo.

**Chicken stock, soy or miso.** The ministry describes a chicken stock, seasoned with either soy sauce or miso. It does not fix one; which one depends on who is cooking.

**No four-legged meat, traditionally.** Wrestlers avoided the meat of four-legged animals such as beef and pork, the ministry says, because a wrestler who puts his hands on the ground has lost.

**A hotpot that is also an ordinary one.** The ministry adds a detail that keeps the dish in proportion: cooked at home with the same ingredients, it becomes an everyday *yose-nabe*, a mixed hotpot. What makes it chanko is who makes it and why, not a fixed recipe.

## How to eat it

The farm ministry notes that former wrestlers run chanko shops across Tokyo. For a visitor, those shops are where the dish is on offer.

What the sources describe about the pot itself is short:

- **The stock** is chicken.
- **The seasoning** is soy sauce or miso, depending on the shop or the home.
- **The meat**, by tradition, is not beef or pork.

How a sumo stable serves its own meals, and which vegetables go in, are not set out in the sources used for this page, so this page leaves them out.

## Not all chanko is this

The point the association makes is worth taking literally. A wrestler's grilled fish, rice or curry is chanko in the sumo sense. A hotpot of the same ingredients cooked at home, the ministry says, is a *yose-nabe*. So the word stretches one way inside sumo, to cover every meal, and shrinks the other way outside it, to mean one kind of hotpot.

Japan's other hotpots are seasoned in their own ways. Ishikari nabe in Hokkaido is kombu stock with miso and salmon; botan nabe in Tamba-Sasayama is wild boar in miso. Both are on the [soups and hotpots overview](/en/eat/nabe/).

## Chanko away from its town

The ministry places the dish in Sumida ward, and says shops run by former wrestlers are found across Tokyo. The sources used here do not tie chanko to any one neighbourhood beyond that, and no public source compares eating chanko in one part of Tokyo, or outside it, with eating it elsewhere on the same terms. This page does not say where it is better.

## What people get wrong

- **"Chanko means hotpot."** In sumo it means anything a wrestler cooks; chanko nabe is the hotpot.
- **"Chanko has one recipe."** The ministry gives chicken stock with soy sauce or miso, and says it varies.
- **"The word's origin is known."** The association lists a Chinese pot and the word for father, and says there is no settled answer.
- **"Any meat goes in."** By tradition wrestlers avoided beef and pork, the four-legged meats.
- **"You need to visit a stable to eat it."** The ministry says former wrestlers run chanko shops across Tokyo.
