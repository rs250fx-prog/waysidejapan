Japan’s best-known spaghetti dish is named after Naples and, on the only record I could reach, was made up in Yokohama. Pasta itself arrived much earlier, through a missionary in Nagasaki, and stayed a restaurant food for decades. Pizza, on the record, enters through a delivery chain. This page sets out what each source actually says, and where the record runs out.

For the wider group of Western foods made Japanese, start from the Western foods overview.

## Where it came from

**Pasta, from Nagasaki.** The Japan Pasta Association says pasta was first made in Japan around 1883 (Meiji 16), when a French missionary, Father de Rotz (マリク・マリ・ド・ロ in the association’s Japanese), built a single-storey brick macaroni works in Sotome, in what is now Nagasaki city. The association says this "is said to be" the first. The first pasta made by Japanese makers, it says, came in the Taishō period, when a Yokohama trader asked a noodle maker in what is now Kamo, Niigata, to make macaroni.

**Still rare before the war.** Domestic production grew from the early Shōwa period, the association says, but the machines were small and worked in batches, so pasta was something you could eat at a few restaurants.

**Ordinary from 1955.** Pasta became common only after fully automatic machines were imported, from the Shōwa 30s onward. Shōwa 30, that is 1955, is called the "first year of pasta". The domestic pasta of that time blended several kinds of flour to suit Japanese taste and texture. From about 1986, the association says, pasta made only from durum semolina came into home use in Japan, as travel abroad grew and Italian restaurants boomed.

**Napolitan, from Yokohama — by the hotel’s account.** The Hotel New Grand’s page on dishes that began there says its second head chef, Irie Shigetada, devised spaghetti Napolitan. While the hotel was requisitioned by the occupation, from 1945 to 1952, staff learned that American soldiers ate boiled spaghetti with salt, pepper and ketchup; the hotel says the chef arranged that into a dish of his own after the requisition ended. The page does not give a year.

Yokohama city’s tourism site calls Napolitan a pasta born in Yokohama, not Naples, and says it "is told" that the hotel’s head chef devised it and that Irie named it "spaghetti Napolitan". Note the wording: the city reports the story rather than vouching for it.

The pasta association’s history page does not mention Napolitan at all.

**Pizza, from a delivery chain.** Domino’s Pizza Japan’s timeline says that on 30 September 1985 it opened Japan’s first pizza delivery shop in Ebisu, Tokyo, founded by Ernest M. Higa. That "first" is the company’s own claim; I did not check it against an industry body.

## What makes it Japanese

**The hotel’s sauce.** The Hotel New Grand describes its Napolitan as made with garlic and onion cooked slowly, and a sauce of fresh tomatoes, canned tomatoes, tomato paste, bay leaf and olive oil. The page also says the hotel had spaghetti dishes such as Caruso and Bolognese, handed down from Sally Weil, the first head chef it hired when it opened in 1927. It does not say those were the direct ancestors of Napolitan, and neither does this page.

**The blend.** The early domestic pasta was not durum-only; it blended several flours for Japanese taste and texture. Durum-only pasta in home kitchens dates, on the association’s account, from about 1986.

**The delivery menu.** Domino’s timeline is the clearest record of pizza being adapted:

- **1986** — "Tuna Special", developed in Japan.
- **1992** — charcoal-grilled teriyaki chicken, which the company calls its first Japanese-style pizza.
- **1994** — "Mayo-jaga", mayonnaise and potato.

The company says it reached 600 shops in 2019 and, in 2023, 1,000 shops, which it calls a first in the industry.

**The delivery bike.** Domino’s says that because Tokyo’s roads were narrow and congested, it developed a delivery bike from a converted three-wheeled scooter, and started a 30-minute guarantee.

## How to order it

- **The hotel’s Napolitan.** Yokohama city’s tourism site says the original can be eaten at the hotel’s café, The Café.
- **Delivery pizza.** Look for the Japan-developed toppings on the chain menus; the three named above are the ones the company’s own timeline records.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Not all of it is this

- **Pizza-La.** Another delivery name, Pizza-La, is run by a company called Four Seeds (フォーシーズ), founded on 1 April 1980 according to its company profile. When Pizza-La itself began and how its menu developed, I could not confirm from its official pages.
- **The wood-fired pizzeria.** I found no official or industry source describing the pizzeria in Japan, so this page says nothing about it.
- **The coffee-house Napolitan.** The page on coffee houses and shaved ice mentions the café Napolitan. How ketchup Napolitan spread to coffee houses across Japan, I could not find in any primary source.

## Napolitan away from Yokohama

What can be said is narrow. The hotel’s own sauce is built on fresh tomatoes, canned tomatoes and tomato paste, and the hotel says the idea came from soldiers’ ketchup. Those are the two facts on record. I found no official source comparing the hotel dish with the Napolitan of ordinary coffee houses elsewhere, so this page does not describe the difference.

For pizza, I found no official source comparing Japanese pizza with Italian pizza. What is on record is that Japan-only toppings came out of a delivery chain.

## What people get wrong

- **"Napolitan is from Naples."** The city of Yokohama calls it a Yokohama pasta, and the hotel credits its own chef.
- **"The original Napolitan has no ketchup."** The hotel describes a tomato sauce and also says the idea came from soldiers’ ketchup. I found no source for the "no ketchup" claim.
- **"There is only one origin story."** Other shops and places are named in circulation, but I could not confirm any from an official or shop source. This site does not choose; it reports the one account it could check.
- **"Pasta came with the occupation."** Pasta was made in Nagasaki around 1883 and by Japanese makers in the Taishō period. What changed after the war was mass production.
- **"Domino’s was definitely the first pizza delivery in Japan."** That is the company’s claim.
