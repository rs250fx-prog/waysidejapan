If Kobe is defined by bloodline and grade, Matsusaka is defined by sex, place and time. The cattle are heifers that have never calved; the place is a valley in Mie prefecture, inland from Ise; and the time is how long they are fed there. It is the brand most Japanese people would name first for sukiyaki, and the brand whose name is most freely borrowed, because — unlike Kobe — it has no bronze statue at the door.

## Where it came from

The council that defines it is young: the Matsusaka Beef Council was formed in November 2004 by the city, surrounding towns and about 110 fattening farms, and it fixed the production area as the 22 municipalities that existed on 1 November that year, before mergers redrew the map. The practice is older. Farmers in the Matsusaka basin have long bought young heifers from elsewhere — Tajima in Hyōgo above all — and fattened them slowly on the local water and feed; the sukiyaki house Wadakin, founded in 1878, built the name over a century of serving the result. The council's registration system, certificates and seals exist to protect a name that the restaurant made first.

## What makes it Matsusaka beef

**The animal.** Japanese Black heifers, never calved. The council's explanation is the one every brand gives for heifers: finer fat.

**The place and the clock.** The animal must enter the production area by twelve months of age, be registered in the council's individual-ID system on arrival, and be fattened there for the longest and final period of its life. Each animal's history can be checked by the consumer online; qualifying meat is sold with a certificate and seal.

**No grade floor.** This is the point that surprises people who know Kobe's rules. Matsusaka beef does not have to reach a particular grade to carry the name. Quality is sorted by the market and the price, and by the second tier.

**Tokusan — "special production".** The stricter grade, a registered GI since March 2017: calves from Hyōgo brought in at around eight months and fed in the area for 900 days or more, roughly two and a half years. The GI file records 239 Tokusan animals shipped in one year against 6,951 Matsusaka beef overall, with 83% of Tokusan grading 4 or 5. Only Tokusan is a GI; "Matsusaka beef" itself is not registered.

## Why Matsusaka

A basin with good water and a tradition of buying in calves and finishing them slowly, next to the pilgrim road to Ise and a merchant town with the money to eat well. The council's own account stresses the long fattening and the heifers; the restaurant history explains the rest. Matsusaka is a case of a dish — sukiyaki at one house — making a brand that the farmers then organised to defend.

- **Wadakin**, founded 1878, does not publish restaurant prices. Its online shop (tax included) lists sukiyaki beef at ¥4,500 for 300 g of the middle grade, ¥7,050 for 300 g of the upper grade, ¥16,800 for 400 g of the top grade, and ¥25,300 for 400 g of sirloin — a usable guide to the spread within one certified name.
- The prefecture's tourism body lists the council's four conditions and the certificate as the thing to look for at a shop or restaurant.

Compare Ōmi, where a certified shop sells 500 g of sukiyaki beef for ¥4,320–10,800, and Kobe, where a lunch steak is ¥8,020–21,780: the three great names overlap in price far more than their reputations suggest, and the grade and cut matter more than the prefecture.

## How to eat it

Sukiyaki. This is the brand that made its name in a shallow iron pan with sugar and soy, and the Matsusaka houses serve it that way, the meat seared first in the Kansai manner and eaten through raw egg. Shabu-shabu and steak are on the menus too, but if you have come to Matsusaka for one meal, it is this one.

## Matsusaka beef away from Matsusaka

Matsusaka's name is on more menus than Kobe's and is less often real, for a structural reason: the council certifies the animal with a paper certificate and a seal, and has no registered-shop system with a statue at the door. A restaurant anywhere can buy certified Matsusaka beef and serve it honestly; a restaurant anywhere can also write "Matsusaka" on a menu, and the only check is to ask for the certificate and the ten-digit ID. The second thing that changes is the tier: outside Mie, almost all "Matsusaka" is the basic grade, which has no quality floor, and the 239-a-year Tokusan animals rarely leave the area. If a Tokyo menu says Tokusan Matsusaka, it should be able to prove it.

## Getting there

Matsusaka is on the Kintetsu line from Nagoya (about an hour by limited express) and from Osaka-Namba, and on the JR Kisei line; it is a convenient stop between Nagoya and Ise. Wadakin is in the town centre.

## What people get wrong

- **"Matsusaka cattle are born in Matsusaka."** They are brought in as calves, mostly from Hyōgo, and fattened there.
- **"Matsusaka beef is A5."** The basic name has no grade requirement. Tokusan is the tier with a record.
- **"The name on the menu is the proof."** The certificate and the ID number are. There is no bronze cow.
- **"Matsusaka is a steak."** It is a sukiyaki house's name, first and still.
