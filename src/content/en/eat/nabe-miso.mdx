Miso soup is the most ordinary bowl in Japan, and it is not the same bowl everywhere. The miso in it can be made with rice, barley or soybeans, and which one a region leans on has reasons the farm ministry and prefectures spell out: climate, what farmers grew, what went to the lord as tax. This page follows four of them — Aichi’s soybean miso, Kyoto’s white miso, Nagano’s Shinshu miso and the barley miso of Ehime and Kagoshima.

For soups and hotpots in general, start from the soups and hotpots overview.

## Where it came from

The research behind this page did not find an official account of when miso soup began, so this page does not give one. What the sources do describe is how each kind of miso came to belong to its region.

**Soybean miso, Aichi, Gifu and Mie.** The farm ministry explains that summers there are hot and humid, and that miso made with rice or barley koji spoiled easily. The answer was to shape steamed soybeans into balls and grow the koji mould on them directly. One of the two Hatcho miso specifications says this miso-ball method became established around 1830–1844; the other says production began in the village of Hatcho in the early Edo period, and that the name comes from its distance from Okazaki castle, eight *chō*, about 870 m. They are two documents describing the same name, and this site does not choose between them.

**White miso, Kyoto.** The farm ministry says white miso is thought to have begun in Kyoto and has been made since the Heian period, when it was eaten mainly by the nobility.

**Barley miso, Ehime.** Ehime grew barley as a second crop in its rice paddies, the ministry says, and the rice went to the lord as tax. So households made barley miso for themselves, which is why it was also called *inaka miso*, country miso.

## What makes each one what it is

**The definitions.** Japan’s product standard for miso (JAS 0022:2025) defines rice miso as steamed soybeans with rice koji and salt, barley miso the same with barley koji, and soybean miso as steamed soybeans grown with the koji mould and mixed with salt. The farm ministry says rice miso is about 80 percent of national production.

**Aichi red.** The ministry describes soybean miso, typified by Hatcho miso, as indispensable in Aichi cooking, matured for a year and sometimes two or three, with a deep richness, sourness and astringency. The two Hatcho miso specifications differ in detail:

- **Aichi prefectural miso and tamari cooperative.** Production area all of Aichi. Soybeans and salt. Miso balls at least 20 mm across and 50 mm long. Fermented in tanks under weights for at least a year, or at least ten months if temperature-controlled at 25°C or above. Reddish brown, pH about 4.5–5.1. It names the dish family it seasons: miso katsu, miso oden, miso simmered dishes and miso hotpots, as typical "Nagoya meshi".
- **Hatcho Miso cooperative** (added in January 2025). Production area Hatcho-chō, Okazaki. Soybeans, salt and water. Fist-sized balls, in wooden vats holding about six tonnes, under about three tonnes of natural stones piled into a cone, for at least two summers and two winters, without heating. Around 40 percent water, pH about 4.5–4.9. Shipments of 688, 761 and 791 tonnes in the three years Reiwa 3 to 5.

**Kyoto white.** Fermented for only one to ten days or so, low in salt, mild and sweet, according to the ministry.

**Shinshu.** Nagano prefecture classes Shinshu miso as a light-coloured, salty rice miso. "Shinshu miso" is a registered trademark of the prefecture’s miso cooperative.

**Barley, Ehime and Kagoshima.** Ehime’s barley miso is soybeans, barley koji and salt, with a lot of koji, a strong aroma and a faint sweetness. Kagoshima’s, the ministry says, has a lot of koji and rather less salt, and is markedly sweet. The ministry also notes that barley miso from Kanto is the opposite — salty, red and long-matured — while Kyushu’s is sweet and light.

## How it is eaten

**Aichi.** Soybean miso is cooked, not only dissolved. The ministry lists seven Aichi local dishes with miso in the name. Three stand out:

- ***Nimiso*** is root vegetables and other things simmered in soybean miso. How much liquid is up to each home, from a dry simmer to something close to soup or hotpot. Leftover broth becomes rice porridge or udon, and the flavour is said to deepen when it is reheated over several days.
- **Miso-simmered udon** (*miso nikomi udon*) is udon cooked in a Hatcho miso broth and served in an earthenware pot. A home dish, it is said to have been served by a restaurant in Ichinomiya in the Meiji era and then spread, centred on Nagoya. The noodles are flour and water with no salt, so they stay firm after long cooking.
- **Miso oden** is dipped in miso sauce — see the oden page.

**Kyoto.** White miso goes into the New Year *zōni* of the first three days of January. Many households make their own in advance, but it is not eaten much day to day. Some primary schools teach children to make it from soybeans.

**Nagano.** The full-of-ingredients miso soup is everyday food, more so in the cold winter, sometimes with *suiton* dumplings in place of rice. The farm ministry’s Nagano page says the prefecture makes more than 40 percent of Japan’s miso and consumes the most. That figure is the page’s own statement; it gives no year or statistic behind it.

**Ehime and Kagoshima.** Ehime eats barley-miso soup all year and serves it in school lunches. The ministry’s page does not tie it to breakfast. In Kagoshima, *tonjiru* (pork miso soup) made with barley miso is eaten all year, more in winter, in a region with many pork dishes; the ministry notes that the Satsuma domain ate pork, wild boar and venison in the Edo period.

## Not all miso soup is this

The ministry’s 2023 breakfast feature lists local miso soups beyond these four: crab *teppō-jiru* from Nemuro, saury dumpling soup from Iwate, *gojiru* from Gunma, *metta-jiru* from Ishikawa, clam soup from Shimane and dumpling soup from Oita. In Yamanashi a gourd miso soup is a summer dish. The same feature suggests regional additions — corn and butter in Hokkaido, whole natto in Akita, Iwate and Yamagata, sakura shrimp in Shizuoka, aosa seaweed in Mie, tomato in Kumamoto. I could not find a public source saying each region has a fixed breakfast miso soup.

## Regional miso away from home

I found no public source that compares eating these misos at home with eating them elsewhere on the same terms, and this page does not say which is better. Two facts are fixed:

- **Hatcho miso has a defined production area.** Under its GI registration, the name belongs to miso made in Aichi, or in Hatcho-chō, Okazaki, depending on the specification. How that plays out in shops elsewhere, or in price, I did not find.
- **Shinshu miso is a trademark.** The name is registered to Nagano’s miso cooperative.

## What people get wrong

- **"Red miso and white miso mean the same thing everywhere."** I could not find a public source that sets out how the names are used in Kansai, Nagoya and Kanto, so I would not assume.
- **"White miso is Kyoto’s daily miso."** The ministry says it is mainly a New Year food at home.
- **"Hatcho miso is one product."** Two specifications, two production areas, one name.
- **"Barley miso is always sweet."** The Kyushu kind is; the Kanto kind is salty and red.
- **"Miso soup is breakfast food."** The ministry recommends it at breakfast, but its Ehime, Nagano and Kagoshima pages describe it as eaten all year, without tying it to a meal.
