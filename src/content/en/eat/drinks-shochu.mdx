Shōchū is the drink most visitors pass over on an izakaya menu, and the one with the most regional variety in Japan. It is a distilled spirit, but it is usually drunk cut with water — cold, hot, or rested for days in advance. What it is made from changes by island and prefecture, and several of those places protect their names in law. This page is about what the label means, where each kind comes from and why, and how it is drunk.

For drinks in Japan in general — the tax categories, the drinking age, customs — start from the drinks overview.

## Where it came from

The national research institute for brewing (NRIB) calls shōchū Japan’s own distilled drink. How the technique arrived is told in two ways, and this site does not choose between them:

- **Okinawa.** NRIB says distilling is *thought* to have been brought to Okinawa directly through trade with China and South-East Asia in the 15th century. The Tokyo Regional Taxation Bureau states it more firmly: that it came to Okinawa from Siam (now Thailand) in the mid-15th century is, in its words, the accepted view.
- **Satsuma.** In its section on sweet-potato shōchū, NRIB says shōchū seems to have been made by around the 16th century, but probably from millet, barnyard millet and sake lees rather than the ingredients used now.

The ingredients came later, and each has a date:

- **Sweet potato.** The sweet potato reached Tanegashima from Ryūkyū in 1698 and Yamakawa (now Ibusuki, Kagoshima) in 1705. Satsuma had made rice shōchū before; around 1850 the lord Shimazu Nariakira encouraged mass production of sweet-potato shōchū.
- **Barley.** Iki island in Nagasaki is said to be its birthplace. In Ōita, the end of barley rationing in 1951 is what started serious production.
- **Rice.** Made all over Japan; the old centre is Kuma (Kuma district and Hitoyoshi, Kumamoto). When and how the technique reached Kuma is not clear; NRIB gives the mid-Edo period as a view drawn from several theories.
- **Brown sugar.** Made only in the Amami Islands (see below).
- **Buckwheat (soba).** Mainly the Takachiho area of Miyazaki, also Nagano and Hokkaido. NRIB dates its start to Gokase, Miyazaki, in 1973.

**The 1973 still.** NRIB also dates a change in style. In 1973 a vacuum still for shōchū was developed in Fukuoka prefecture and taken up in Kuma, where it was called the "vacuum revolution". It spread to Ōita barley and Miyazaki buckwheat shōchū and produced lighter products. In NRIB’s description, ordinary-pressure distillation gives a fuller flavour, vacuum distillation a lighter one.

## What makes it shōchū

**Two stills, two legal items.** The Liquor Tax Act splits shōchū by how it is distilled:

- **Continuous-still shōchū** — under 36% alcohol. Also called *kōrui* (A-type), and sometimes labelled "white liquor".
- **Single-still shōchū** — 45% or less. Also called *otsurui* (B-type), and also sometimes labelled "white liquor".

The national brewers’ association explains that a single still is simple in structure, so more of the ingredient’s aroma and flavour dissolves into the spirit. NRIB adds the comparison with whisky: whisky is usually distilled two or more times, single-still shōchū often only once, which is why more aroma remains. The official sources describe this as a difference in distillation. They do not call either type better, and neither does this page.

**What *honkaku* means.** Only single-still shōchū that meets the law’s list of ingredients may be labelled *honkaku shōchū*, "authentic shōchū". The association gives examples: grains or sweet potatoes with their kōji and water; grain kōji and water only; shōchū using sake lees; brown sugar (sugar) with rice kōji and water.

**Two more labels.** Single-still shōchū made only from rice kōji grown with black kōji mould and water is labelled "shōchū otsurui" or "awamori". A blend of the two still types is *konwa* shōchū, labelled "kōrui-otsurui" or "otsurui-kōrui" blend according to which is the larger part.

**How it is made.** In NRIB’s outline, kōji turns the starch into sugar and yeast turns sugar into alcohol, both in the same vessel at once. A first mash of kōji, water and yeast is followed by a second mash where the main ingredient goes in, then a single distillation. The second mash reaches 14–20% alcohol; the spirit comes off the still at 37–43%.

## The regions, and what their names promise

The tax agency’s list of geographical indications for distilled spirits:

- **Iki** (Iki city, Nagasaki), **Kuma** (Kuma district and Hitoyoshi, Kumamoto) and **Ryūkyū** (Okinawa) — designated 30 June 1995.
- **Satsuma** (Kagoshima, except Amami city and Ōshima district) — designated 22 December 2005.
- **Tokyo Shimazake** (eight towns and villages of the Izu Islands, Tokyo) — designated 13 March 2024.

Miyazaki, a sweet-potato and buckwheat region, has no shōchū GI on that list.

**Awamori.** Okinawa’s traditional shōchū, made from rice kōji grown with black kōji mould and water only, using Indica rice, single-distilled. Awamori stored three years or more is called *kūsu* (aged). Since 1 August 2015 a bottle may be labelled aged only if all of it has been stored three years or more.

**Amami brown-sugar shōchū.** Brown sugar with rice kōji is allowed as a shōchū ingredient only in the Amami Islands. NRIB gives two reasons side by side. One is wartime: during the Pacific War and the American occupation after it, brown sugar could not be shipped off the islands. The other is tax: when Amami returned to Japan in 1953 the gap between the rates for shōchū and for spirits was large, and the Liquor Tax Act was amended to count brown-sugar spirit as shōchū, on condition that rice kōji was used.

**Tokyo’s island shōchū.** Tokyo Shimazake is honkaku shōchū characterised by barley kōji, in three kinds: sweet potato, barley, and a blend. In the Tokyo Regional Taxation Bureau’s description, only shōchū using domestic sweet potatoes and water from the Izu Islands, fermented, distilled, stored and bottled on the islands, may use the name. NRIB tells how it began: in the mid-19th century a Satsuma trader, Tansō Shōemon, exiled to Hachijōjima, is said to have brought sweet-potato shōchū to Miyakejima and Hachijōjima. Brewing with grain was banned on the islands then, so a spirit from potatoes was welcomed.

## How to drink it

These are the habits the makers’ association and NRIB describe, with their reasons. Neither presents them as rules.

- **With water (*mizuwari*).** The association calls it the most common way: it keeps the aroma while softening the spirit. NRIB suggests diluting to 10–15% alcohol.
- **With hot water (*oyuwari*).** The association suggests *roku-yon*, six parts 25% shōchū to four of hot water. That gives about 15% alcohol at roughly the temperature of warmed sake, which it says brings out savouriness and sweetness. NRIB says 4:6, 5:5 and 6:4 are all common, and that warming lifts the aroma.
- **On the rocks or straight.** For the full flavour, with a chaser of water.
- **With soda, or in a cocktail.** The association says the aroma lifts and it tastes fresher.
- **With soba-yu.** Buckwheat shōchū is sometimes cut with the water noodles were cooked in, at soba shops.

NRIB adds pairings: sweet potato gains aroma and sweetness when warmed; aged awamori is drunk straight, other awamori on the rocks or with water, and in Okinawa often with turmeric tea (*ucchin-cha*).

**Maewari, the drink made the day before.** The makers’ association’s information site describes *maewari*: shōchū mixed with water and left from a night to a week, so that it blends with the water and becomes rounder and gentler. It calls it popular in Kyushu and a drink of hospitality, served to guests and at weddings and other celebrations. Some distillers sell it already diluted, as *kura-wari*. No prefectural or tourism-board source checked for this page describes maewari.

## Not all shōchū is this

A continuous-still kōrui shōchū and an aged awamori are both shōchū in law; vacuum-distilled Ōita barley and ordinary-pressure Satsuma sweet potato are both honkaku. Read the ingredient and the still before the name.

## Shōchū away from its islands

**At the source, you book.** Kirishima Shuzo, a sweet-potato shōchū maker in Miyakonojō, Miyazaki, runs a factory tour, KIRISHIMA WALK FACTORY, five times a day (10:00, 11:00, 13:30, 14:30 and 15:30), about 60 minutes, booked online or by phone by the day before. It says tasting is offered on the tour, but to ask about current arrangements. Its lees-recycling facility needs no booking.

**The strength may differ.** Kirishima Shuzo says 20% shōchū is widely drunk in Miyazaki. That is a company’s statement.

**Maewari is a home habit.** The association places it in Kyushu as a drink for guests.

**Tokyo has its own.** Tokyo’s shōchū GI belongs to the Izu Islands, not the city, and works on the same system as Satsuma or Iki.

No official source checked for this page gives the tour fee, says whether under-20s may join, gives a statistic on the strength drunk in Miyazaki, or says what Tokyo izakaya actually stock, so this page makes no claim about these. Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kōrui is the cheap, bad shōchū."** The sources describe a different still and less carry-over of aroma, not a grade.
- **"Awamori is not shōchū."** It is single-still shōchū in law, with its own rules.
- **"Brown-sugar shōchū can be made anywhere."** Brown sugar with rice kōji counts as a shōchū ingredient only in the Amami Islands.
- **"There is a correct ratio."** The association suggests 6:4 for hot water; NRIB lists 4:6 to 6:4 as all common.
