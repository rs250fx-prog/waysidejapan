Mochi is steamed glutinous rice pounded until it holds together, and in Japan it is food for occasions, New Year among them. Which shape you are given, what soup it sits in, and whether the red rice beside it is salty or sweet all depend on where you are. This page covers mochi, the New Year soup *zōni*, and *sekihan*, the bean-coloured rice of celebrations. It also covers the Consumer Affairs Agency’s New Year warning that mochi is a choking risk.

For rice and the things around it in general, start from the rice and sides overview.

## Where it came from

**Mochi.** The farm ministry’s guide to traditional foods lists many forms across Japan — cut mochi, *noshi-mochi* (rolled sheets), *namako-mochi*, *hishi-mochi*, freeze-dried *kōri-mochi*, *arare*, Akita’s *kiritanpo*, *kibi-dango* and more — and calls them food for occasions, closely tied to daily life. It describes the method: glutinous rice is polished, washed, soaked and drained, then steamed and pounded, cooled and left to set.

**Zōni.** The ministry’s Kyoto entry says there was a custom in the Heian period of eating a soup with mochi at New Year, and that in the Muromachi period it spread to many regions as a celebratory meal of the warrior class.

**Sekihan.** The ministry’s Akita entry gives two explanations for the red colour and does not choose between them: that it is a remnant of offering ancient red-grained rice (*akagome*) to the gods, or that red drives away evil. It says white rice coloured with red beans spread as sekihan around the middle of the Edo period. I could not find an official explanation of what sekihan means across all of Japan, so this page stays with Akita’s two.

## What makes it mochi, zōni and sekihan

**Square or round.** The ministry’s magazine *aff* puts the rough dividing line near Sekigahara in Gifu. To the east square mochi (*kaku-mochi*) is usual; to the west, round (*maru-mochi*). Five prefectures — Gifu, Ishikawa, Fukui, Mie and Wakayama — have areas that use both.

The exceptions are where the history shows:

- **In the east, round.** The Shōnai area of Yamagata, which the ministry links to Kyoto culture carried by the *kitamaebune* coastal ships, and Ichinoseki in Iwate, where there is a custom of eating fresh-pounded mochi on more than 60 days a year.
- **In the west, square.** Parts of Kōchi (Tosa) and Kagoshima (Satsuma). The ministry says this is thought to be connected to their lords, the Yamauchi and Shimazu, spending long periods in Edo.

Why square at all? The same page says mochi was originally round, and that in the Edo period a method of rolling it flat and cutting it into pieces appeared, and spread from Edo because the pieces were easy to carry. The ministry marks this as one of several views. I could not open the Agency for Cultural Affairs’ material on this, so I have nothing to add from it.

**Two zōni, officially.** The ministry’s database describes them as different dishes:

- **Tokyo (Edo zōni).** Square mochi, grilled until fragrant, in a clear soup of kombu and bonito. The ministry ties the dark-soy Edo zōni to soy production growing in Noda and Chōshi in Shimōsa during the Genroku era.
- **Kyoto (white miso zōni).** Round mochi, said to stand for harmony and long life, in white miso with kombu stock. The head taro (*kashira-imo*) stands for descendants and success; daikon is cut in rounds for harmony or in a hexagonal "tortoise shell" for long life. The ministry says white miso is held to have started in Kyoto and was made from the Heian period, mainly for nobles. Families eat it in the first three days of the year.

**More zōni.** The *aff* page lists others: square mochi in Sapporo, Miyako, Tokyo and Nagoya; round mochi in the red-bean zōni of San’in and in Hakata; both shapes mixed in parts of Kagoshima. Nagoya avoids grilling white mochi and simmers it soft instead. Sapporo’s clear soup uses chicken bones and is on the sweet side, and Hakata’s uses flying-fish stock (*ago-dashi*). There are sweet zōni too, including one with mochi filled with red-bean paste, said to come from people in the old Takamatsu domain hiding the fine sugar *wasanbon* inside it. Okinawa, the page says, is held to have had no zōni custom.

**Two sekihan.**

- **Akita** adds sugar to its sekihan of *tenko-azuki* beans. It is made for celebrations and Buddhist rites, Obon, New Year, weddings and funerals, and for giving thanks for the harvest, following the custom of offering it at the Niiname-sai harvest festival on 23 November. For someone who has lived a full life, there is also a custom of calling it "black rice".
- **Hokkaido** mixes in sweet *amanattō* beans and colours the rice with food colouring. It is made for festivals, Obon, weddings and birth celebrations, handed round to neighbours, and served in school lunches. The ministry says there are several stories of its origin, and that it is said to have been devised in the early 1950s by Nanbu Akiko in Sapporo as a sekihan easy to make at home. It also notes that 23 November is called "sekihan day", with supermarket campaigns.

## How to eat it

The Consumer Affairs Agency’s New Year warning, published on 23 December 2020, gives these figures: of deaths from choking on mochi, 43% happened in January; the number of men who died was 2.6 times the number of women; and deaths cluster in the first three days of the year. Among people aged 65 and over, food choking caused more than 3,500 deaths a year, more than 2,500 of them aged 80 and over (the agency cites the health ministry’s vital statistics for a two-year analysis period).

Its advice:

- Cut mochi into small pieces.
- Moisten your throat with tea or soup first — but do not wash pieces down without chewing.
- Take a bite of a manageable size.
- Chew slowly and well, then swallow.
- When older people eat mochi, have someone watch.

The page does not set out first-aid steps itself; it links to the Japan Medical Association’s resuscitation guidance.

## Not all of Japan’s mochi is this

The ministry’s list of mochi forms runs from cut and rolled mochi to *kiritanpo* and *kibi-dango*. And Ichinoseki’s custom of fresh mochi on more than 60 days a year shows that, there at least, mochi is not only a New Year food.

## Mochi away from home

I could not find a source that compares zōni or sekihan at home with what is served in Tokyo or abroad. What the ministry shows is a difference between places: Tokyo and Kyoto zōni differ in mochi shape, soup and seasoning, and Akita and Hokkaido sekihan differ in sweetness. Neither is described as the original of the other.

## What people get wrong

- **"Japanese mochi is round."** East of Sekigahara it is usually square.
- **"Zōni is one recipe."** The ministry presents Tokyo’s clear soup and Kyoto’s white miso as separate dishes, and lists many more.
- **"Sekihan is red because of the beans, end of story."** The beans colour it, but Akita’s page gives two explanations of why red, and does not choose.
- **"Sekihan is salty."** In Akita and Hokkaido it is sweet.
- **"Choking is rare."** The agency’s figures put 43% of mochi choking deaths in January, mostly in the first three days of the year.
