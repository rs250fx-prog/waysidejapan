Most Japanese food tastes the way it does because of a stock you never see. *Dashi* — usually kelp (*kombu*) and dried, smoked bonito (*katsuobushi*), sometimes dried sardines, flying fish or shiitake — is the base of miso soup, of the broth under udon and soba, of simmered dishes, and of a great many sauces. It is also why so much Japanese food that looks vegetarian is not. This page explains what dashi is, what umami is, where the ingredients come from, and what to ask if you do not eat fish. The lineage pages cover the regional split: kombu in Kansai and bonito in Kantō, and the sardine and flying-fish stocks of the west.

## What it is

The farm ministry's booklet on *washoku* calls dashi the key to every dish. It is made from kombu or katsuobushi, or by simmering ingredients for a set time, and the booklet calls it the most important element of Japanese flavour: the base of miso soup and clear soup, and the foundation of simmered dishes and dressed greens.

**Umami.** Umami is the fifth basic taste, after sweet, sour, salty and bitter. The Umami Information Center names its main sources: the amino acid glutamate, found widely in meat, fish and vegetables; inosinate, rich in meat and fish and the umami of katsuobushi; and guanylate, rich in dried mushrooms such as shiitake. It distinguishes *umami*, the basic taste, from the everyday Japanese word for deliciousness.

**Why kombu and bonito together.** Glutamate combined with inosinate or guanylate tastes stronger than either alone. The center measured the effect as strongest at a 1-to-1 ratio of glutamate to inosinate, about seven to eight times either alone, and reports that the first dashi of one long-established restaurant, when analysed, came out at exactly 1-to-1. Kombu and katsuobushi together is the classic first dashi (*ichiban dashi*); kombu and dried shiitake works the same way.

**Experience came first.** The National Diet Library's essay on dashi notes a combined bonito-and-kombu stock in a cookbook of 1668, and suggests cooks were beginning to recognise the effect. A Kyoto restaurateur interviewed by the city's tourism site says the same: the cooks knew before the chemists.

## Who discovered umami

**Ikeda Kikunae.** The National Diet Library says the chemist began studying the taste of kombu in 1907, resumed in 1908 after reading a paper arguing that umami helps digestion, identified glutamate as kombu's taste with his assistant, and in 1909 launched it commercially as *Ajinomoto*. The Umami Information Center dates the identification and the name *umami* to 1908, with a patent filed in April and registered on 25 July that year. The farm ministry's booklet says 1907. The difference is between starting the research and identifying the compound; this site gives both years.

**What he did not discover.** The library calls it a misunderstanding that Ikeda discovered glutamic acid: a German chemist, Ritthausen, did that in 1866. Ikeda's contribution was realising that sodium glutamate has a strong umami taste and devising a way to make it industrially.

**How he started** is told two ways. The long-standing story, from Ikeda's own article, is that he was prompted by kombu his wife had bought; the Umami Information Center tells it that way. The library notes that this is being reconsidered, and that some suggest Ikeda, who grew up with kombu dashi in Kyoto, had been interested in kombu's taste since his youth.

**After him.** In 1913 Kodama Shintarō identified inosinate as the umami of katsuobushi, and in 1957 Kuninaka Akira of the Yamasa soy-sauce laboratory identified guanylate, later confirmed as the umami of dried shiitake.

## The ingredients

- **Kombu.** The Umami Information Center calls it the food richest in glutamate. Most is harvested in Hokkaido, from July to September; Hakodate city says Hokkaido produces over 90% of Japan's kombu. Varieties differ: *ma-kombu* is used mainly in Kansai around Osaka; *Rishiri* gives a clear stock suited to Kyoto's *kaiseki*; *Rausu* is thin and soft; *Hidaka* is soft enough to eat simmered.
- **Katsuobushi.** Bonito simmered, then smoked and dried repeatedly until its water content is 26% or less. Kagoshima produces the most, and with Shizuoka is one of the two main producing areas. The types: *arabushi* (simmered and smoked), *hadakabushi* (arabushi with the surface shaved), *karebushi* (cured with mould) and *honkarebushi* (mould-cured repeatedly, the finest). *Honkarebushi* takes about four months of moulding and sun-drying; the farm ministry says the mould breaks down fat, so the stock stays clear.
- **Other dried fish.** The farm ministry's guide lists *sōdabushi* (rich, used for soba), mackerel flakes (soba and udon broth, miso soup), *urumebushi* (Kansai udon) and sardine flakes (ramen).
- **Niboshi / iriko.** Boiled and dried anchovy. The National Diet Library says there is little clear record before the 18th century, and that from about the Kyōhō era (1716–36), when katsuobushi was too expensive except for festivals, dried sardines and cheaper fish flakes became the everyday stock, especially in western Japan.
- **Ago.** Flying fish, the stock of northern Kyushu (see the lineage page).
- **Dried shiitake.** The center gives fresh shiitake 70 mg of glutamate per 100 g, dried shiitake 1,060 mg of glutamate and 150 mg of guanylate — the guanylate forms in drying. The soaking water is used as stock.

**A long history.** The farm ministry records bonito bones at Jōmon sites, and *katsuo* products in law codes of 701 and 905; it says the near-modern method began in Kii in 1674 and is said to have reached Makurazaki in 1707, and on another page that mould-curing was devised in Tosa in the mid-1600s. The National Diet Library says fishermen from Kii devised the improved, mould-cured bonito in Tosa in the late 17th century. These accounts overlap without agreeing, so I give them side by side. The library dates the first source for something like modern dashi to the late Muromachi period, and says bonito and kombu stocks reached ordinary households in the Meiji era, as home cookbooks spread.

## Instant dashi

- **Granules.** Ajinomoto launched Hondashi in November 1970, selling it to busy households in the high-growth years as a way to make "authentic" miso soup by adding granules; by the 1980s it calls it indispensable. Yamaki launched a bonito stock base in October 1969, and single-use packs of bonito flakes in 1972.
- **What the standard says.** Japan's agricultural standard for flavour seasonings (the 2015 version I read) defines them as amino-acid seasoning and flavour ingredients — fish flakes, dried sardines, kombu, scallop, dried shiitake — with sugar and salt, dried into powder or granules. So granulated dashi can contain fish. Check the label.

## If you do not eat fish

This is where dashi matters most to visitors.

- **Fish is the default.** The usual ingredients — katsuobushi, dried sardines, fish flakes — are fish. I could not find an official figure for how many Japanese dishes use bonito stock.
- **JNTO's warning.** Japan's national tourism organisation says soups and sauces often contain meat or fish ingredients, that bonito flakes are a hidden ingredient, and that the sauce packet with nattō is often fish-based. It advises saying clearly that you cannot eat meat, seafood, or any animal product including dashi and soup stock, carrying a card in Japanese, and stating dietary needs when you book. A rice ball with kombu is one fish-free example it gives.
- **"Vegetable" is not "vegetarian."** The Japan Tourism Agency's guide for restaurants gives an example: one restaurant serves a fully plant-based *shōjin kaiseki*, and separately a "vegetable *kaiseki*" that includes bonito stock, eggs and dairy.
- **Fish is not "not meat".** The same guide's table of dietary types shows that vegans and lacto-, ovo- and lacto-ovo vegetarians all avoid seafood; only pescatarians eat fish.
- **The visitor experience.** In the agency's survey of 71 vegetarian and vegan visitors, many said they had given up ramen, sushi, soba and tempura they had hoped to eat, and more relaxed their rules in Japan than on other trips abroad.
- **A fish-free stock exists.** Kombu (glutamate) and dried shiitake (guanylate) give umami without fish. I have not found an official source calling it the standard vegan dashi, and whether a given miso soup or noodle broth uses it can only be answered by the shop.

## Where to taste it

- **Osaka and Kyoto** — kombu-led stock, light soy, as in Osaka's kitsune udon.
- **Tokyo** — bonito-led broth with dark soy.
- **Kagawa** — Sanuki udon with *iriko* stock, or bonito, shop by shop.
- **Gotō and Fukuoka** — *ago* stock.

## The same stock, a different place

There is no official comparison of a Tokyo broth with a Kansai or regional one, so this page does not say one is better. What the sources show:

- **The home of a stock is not one taste.** Sanuki udon shops use *iriko* or bonito; Osaka's udon stock is mainly ma-kombu and fish flakes; Hakata's New Year soup mixes flying fish, kombu and bonito.
- **The ingredients travel.** Kombu is over 90% from Hokkaido, katsuobushi mostly from Kagoshima and Shizuoka. A Tokyo or Osaka stock is made from ingredients shipped in, as it has been since the Edo period.
- **The soy differs.** Kansai uses light soy, Kantō dark.

## What people get wrong

- **"Umami was discovered by Ajinomoto."** It was identified by Ikeda Kikunae in 1907–08, who then helped create Ajinomoto.
- **"Ikeda discovered glutamate."** Ritthausen did, in 1866. Ikeda found its taste.
- **"Miso soup is vegetarian."** Usually the stock is fish. Ask.
- **"Instant dashi is just kombu."** By the standard, it can contain fish, scallop and amino-acid seasoning. Read the label.
- **"Dashi is listed in the UNESCO inscription."** *Washoku* was inscribed on 4 December 2013; the farm ministry's page on it mentions umami, not dashi, though its booklet calls dashi the base of Japanese flavour.
