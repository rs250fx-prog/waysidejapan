Shōjin ryōri is the food of Zen temples: cooking without meat or fish, built on vegetables, tofu and the processed foods made from soy and wheat. Two things are worth knowing before you book it. The meal a visitor eats is not the meal monks eat in training. And "shōjin" on a menu does not settle whether the stock is fish-free or the food is vegan; the official sources say to ask.

For kaiseki in general, start from the kaiseki overview.

## Where it came from

The sources give the arrival in two ways, and this site does not choose:

- The farm ministry's booklet *Washoku no Ryūgi*: shōjin ryōri was the Zen meal practice and cooking of temples in Song-dynasty China, brought to Japan by Zen monks about 700 years ago.
- Kyoto's official tourism site: it arrived with Buddhism in the Kamakura period (1185–1333), and was cooked and eaten by monks in their monasteries. Because meat and fish were not used, vegetables became central.

Kyoto city's education board describes it as developed mainly at Zen temples, using no meat or fish and often vegetables, seaweed, soy products and konnyaku.

## Two meals: the monks’ and the guests’

The farm ministry's booklet describes both at Daitokuji:

- **The monks' meal.** Lunch for monks in training is barley rice and simmered vegetables — one soup, one dish — and all three meals are frugal, enough to hold off hunger. Preparing and eating food are both part of training.
- **The guests' meal.** From the late Muromachi into the Edo period, lords, tea masters and wealthy merchants who came to practise at Daitokuji were served a hospitality shōjin in the honzen style, enriched with processed foods such as tofu, yuba, fresh fu and natto. The booklet says it was different from the monks' meal but shared the spirit of wasting nothing and using every ingredient fully. It is said, the booklet adds, to have influenced the tea kaiseki that tea masters later developed. The booklet notes that this kind can still be eaten at restaurants serving Daitokuji.

So the shōjin a visitor eats around a temple is the hospitality kind, not the monks' daily food.

## What makes it shōjin

- **No meat or fish.** That is the line Kyoto city and the farm ministry draw. Neither says how eggs, dairy or the pungent vegetables are treated.
- **Kōya's description.** The Kōya temple lodging association describes its shōjin as valuing the five methods, five tastes and five colours — cooking method, seasoning and colour balance — and as evolved from the temples’ own hospitality food, served, it says, without any *namagusa-mono* or the like.
- **Stock.** The farm ministry's booklet calls dried shiitake indispensable to shōjin, and lists kombu as a stock as well. Stock in general is covered on the dashi overview.
- **Yuba and fu.** Kyoto's tourism site explains yuba as the skin formed by heating soy milk in a shallow pan, eaten as it is, in hot pots or in kaiseki; and fu as made from wheat gluten, in fresh and baked kinds.
- **Sesame tofu.** The farm ministry lists *goma-dōfu* as a Wakayama local dish: said to be one of the shōjin foods of monks on Kōya, the mountain opened by Kūkai about 1,200 years ago, with sesame thought to have supplied protein lacking in a diet without meat or fish. It is made of white sesame and Yoshino kudzu. Another account traces it to *asa-dōfu* in a shōjin cookbook of 1697. The ministry gives both; so does this page.
## Where to eat it, and how the temples run it

**Kōya: temple lodgings.** The Kōya temple lodging association (checked 2026-10-11) says fires and the Meiji reforms reduced the temples; there are now 117, of which 52 take overnight guests. It says a survey of 1832 seems to have counted 812. Each lodging temple has its own food, rooms, bath and garden. Sake and beer, called *hannya-tō*, may be drunk freely, and guests are encouraged to join the early-morning service in the main hall the next day. The association takes bookings for lodging, and for shōjin lunches at lodging temples, through a form on its site.

Its lunch comes in three tiers: a basic and a standard type with a first and second tray, and a fuller type with a third tray as well; prices may differ by temple. On 2026-10-11 the page said lunch bookings were suspended.

Individual temples also book directly: Ekōin's site takes lodging bookings from 11 months ahead; Fukuchiin by phone and fax, 9:00 to 20:00. I could not find tax-included lodging prices on the association's, Ekōin's or Fukuchiin's pages.

**Eiheiji: a Zen training stay.** Eiheiji's site (checked 2026-10-11) describes an overnight stay as a Zen practice experience, booked in advance, in shared rooms separated by sex. Supper is called *yakuseki* and breakfast *shōjiki*. On the second day you rise at 4:00 and leave for the Dharma hall at 5:05 for the morning service. Applications go to the temple's office by phone or fax. The page listed arrival dates of 5 October and 3 November; I could not see which were full, and no fee was given. The morning service is held every day of the year and anyone may join, individually or in a group; the guide for the incense offering is ¥1,000 a person.

**Kyoto: a temple restaurant.** Tenryūji's shōjin restaurant, Shigetsu, inside the temple grounds, needs a booking for two or more people by three days ahead (two days for one set, *Yuki*), checked 2026-10-11. It takes no service charge, but a garden entry fee is needed on top of the meal. Its page lists prices but does not say whether they include tax, so I do not give them. Kyoto's tourism site recommends booking shōjin restaurants in advance.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Shōjin is not the same as vegan

- **Different lines.** Shōjin, in the official definitions, means no meat or fish — a religious rule. Vegan, in the tourism agency's guide, also excludes eggs and dairy. They overlap, but they are drawn on different axes.
- **The five pungent vegetables.** The Japan Tourism Agency's vegetarian and vegan guide (April 2020) notes that "Oriental vegans and vegetarians", common among visitors from Taiwan, follow Buddhist thinking and also avoid five strong-smelling plants (*gokun*): garlic, Chinese chives, rakkyō, green onion and asatsuki chives. I found no temple or farm ministry source defining *gokun*. The Kōya association’s description speaks of *namagusa-mono* and does not mention gokun. I cannot say temples officially exclude them.
- **One Tokyo example.** The same guide, from interviews in February 2020, describes a shōjin restaurant in Roppongi, opened in 2015, where about 40% of guests were from abroad. It served a fully plant-based *shōjin kaiseki* (labelled Complete Vegan) and a separate vegetable kaiseki that included bonito stock, eggs and dairy. The kitchen and utensils were not separated: guests sensitive to gokun were told the utensils might have been used with them, and served only if they accepted.
- **Fish stock.** Kyoto's tourism site warns that some restaurants use fish stock, and to check in advance if you want to avoid it.

## Shōjin away from the temple

The sources show a difference of structure, not of quality. On Kōya the temple that feeds you is where you sleep, with the morning service next day; at Eiheiji the meals are part of a training stay; in Kyoto the restaurant is inside temple grounds. A city restaurant lacks that setting — my reading of the sources, not a survey of city restaurants. I found no official comparison of price, quantity or ingredients, and Kyoto's fish-stock warning applies in Kyoto itself: check the restaurant, not the city.

## What people get wrong

- **"Shōjin means vegan."** The official definition is no meat or fish; ask about stock, eggs and dairy.
- **"Visitors eat what monks eat."** The monks' meal is one soup and one dish; visitors get the hospitality kind.
- **"Every temple serves lunch to walk-ins."** Kōya's lunches were suspended on 2026-10-11, and Tenryūji's restaurant needs a booking days ahead.
- **"The arrival date is settled."** The farm ministry says about 700 years ago; Kyoto's tourism site says the Kamakura period.
