The farm ministry, in a footnote to its food-education white paper, describes the basic Japanese meal, *ichijū sansai*, as rice with a soup, such as miso soup or a clear soup, and three dishes, combined with pickles. Beyond those two, the ministry's database of local dishes holds a family of regional soups, each tied to its area. This page covers the ones the sources describe most fully: pork soup in Kagoshima, *kenchin-jiru* in Kanagawa, Iwate and Ibaraki, Niigata's *noppe*, and clam soup at two lakes. For rice and the things around it in general, start from the [rice and sides overview](/en/eat/rice-sides/). Miso itself is on the [miso page](/en/eat/nabe-miso/), and stock on the [dashi overview](/en/eat/dashi/).

## Where it came from

The sources give origins for single soups, not for the family. The pages opened for this one cover pork soup only for Kagoshima, so where pork soup began nationally is not given here.

**Kenchin-jiru** has two stories, and this site does not choose. The ministry's Kanagawa page gives both: that the name comes from *kenchan*, a dish in *fucha ryōri*, the Chinese-style vegetarian cooking, or that the *Kenchō-jiru* of Kenchōji temple in Kamakura came to be called kenchin-jiru. The Iwate page says the soup is held to have begun in Kanagawa.

**Satsuma-jiru**, Kagoshima's chicken miso soup, is said to have begun with Edo-period Satsuma samurai who held cockfights and cooked the losing birds on the spot with vegetables.

## What makes each one

**Tonjiru, Kagoshima.** Pork, daikon, carrot and taro simmered in stock with miso, finished with spring onion and shredded ginger. The ministry says Kagoshima has many pork and miso dishes, among them *tonkotsu* and *buta-miso*, and that in the Satsuma domain pork and wild boar were eaten from the Edo period and barley miso was widely made. It is eaten all year, and more in winter.

**Kenchin-jiru, Kanagawa.** Vegetables are fried in oil before simmering. It is eaten all year but preferred in the cold months, and in parts of Kantō it is eaten at *setsubun* and at the *Ebisu-kō* festival.

**Kenchin-jiru, Iwate.** Vegetables and tofu fried in oil and seasoned with soy. There is no meat; the tofu is fried in oil until it crumbles. The ministry says kenchin-jiru is also a local dish in Iwate, Ōita and Ibaraki, with different ingredients in each. It was once eaten mostly at the end of the harvest work, at New Year, and above all at *Koshōgatsu*, the "little New Year"; now it is eaten any time.

**Tsuke-kenchin, Ibaraki.** Kenchin-jiru of taro, daikon and carrot, served with a separate plate of cold *zaru* soba to dip in it, using thick soba. The ministry notes that Ibaraki has grown buckwheat since the Edo period and today harvests the most after Hokkaido, Nagano and Tochigi.

**Noppe, Niigata.** The ministry is careful to say that Niigata's *noppe* differs from the *noppei-jiru* found around Japan and is more a simmered dish than a soup. Taro is the main ingredient, with vegetables, mushrooms and dried scallop, lightly seasoned and thickened; in winter salmon roe is scattered on top. In deep snow, people made a large pot and kept it in the snow, which is why it is sometimes eaten cold. It is a New Year standard, eaten all year and served at festivals, memorial services and weddings, and its name changes from area to area.

**Shijimi-jiru, Shimane.** Lake Shinji is Japan's seventh-largest brackish lake, and its large *yamato-shijimi* clams are among the biggest catches in the country. About 300 fishers work it, each limited to about 100 kg. In the Izumo area the soup is everyday food: miso soup in winter, a clear soup in summer, and the soup eaten in the *doyō* days was held to be special. The ministry passes on a local saying, "shijimi, from cold water, one boil", meaning do not overcook them; the clams are left overnight in water of 1% salt, like the lake, to purge the sand, and the miso goes in once they open.

**Shijimi-jiru, Shiga.** Made with *seta-shijimi*, a clam found only in Lake Biwa. The ministry says it was everyday food until about 1965, but the catch has fallen sharply with changes in the environment. The season is December to April and the fishing season March to April; without seta-shijimi, the soup can be made with *yamato-shijimi*, which is caught all year.

## Not all of Japan's soups are these

- **Senba-jiru, Osaka.** A clear soup of salted mackerel trimmings and daikon. The ministry says the servants of Senba's merchant houses ate plainly and were given fish such as salted mackerel only twice a month, and the bones went into stock — a dish of *shimatsu*, making use of everything. The bones and daikon are simmered in kombu stock and seasoned with salt.
- **Mamebu-jiru, Iwate.** From the old Yamagata village in Kuji: soy broth of dried sardines and kombu with vegetables, grilled tofu and wheat dumplings filled with walnut. Its name is explained more than one way. It is served at ceremonies and New Year, and it is said the dumplings are made large for happy occasions and small for funerals.
- **Dumpling soups.** Ōita's *dangojiru* and Kumamoto's *dagojiru* have their own [page](/en/eat/udon-dagojiru/), and Hachinohe's cracker soup is on the [senbei-jiru page](/en/eat/bkyu-senbei-jiru/).

## Soups away from home

No official source compares these soups where they are made with the same soups in Tokyo. What the sources show is a difference inside the name itself: kenchin-jiru is a different soup in Kanagawa, Iwate and Ibaraki; *noppe* in Niigata is not the *noppei-jiru* of elsewhere; and shijimi soup at Lake Shinji and in Shiga is made from two different clams.

## What people get wrong

- **"The soup in a Japanese meal is always miso soup."** Senba-jiru and summer shijimi soup are clear soups, and kenchin-jiru in Iwate is seasoned with soy.
- **"Kenchin-jiru has one recipe."** The ministry describes different ingredients in each prefecture that claims it.
- **"Noppe is a soup."** In Niigata, the ministry says, it is closer to a simmered dish.
