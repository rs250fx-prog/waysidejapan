Japan has more pork brands than anyone can keep track of, and most of them are a name on a label. Kagoshima’s black pork is different: it has written standards, a history that nearly ended in the 1970s, and a claim to be the first pork brand in the country. This page covers it, then two regional pork dishes with official write-ups — Obihiro’s grilled pork bowl and Nagasaki’s simmered pork belly.

For Japan’s other meats in general, start from the meat overview.

## Where it came from

**Brand pork.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* (April 2026) says more than 400 pork brands are said to exist in Japan; a Kagoshima research report published in 2024 gives the same figure, citing a 2018 handbook of brand pork. The *aff* article does not give a common definition. It says brands differ by bloodline, feed and so on, and gives one example of how a brand polices itself: for Ibaraki’s *Hitachi no Kagayaki*, distributors in the brand council check the meat and sell only what meets a set grade. I could not confirm, from the original documents, a legal definition of brand pork or the legal conditions for calling pork *kurobuta*, so neither is stated here.

**Black pork in Kagoshima.** Kagoshima prefecture gives two strands of history, side by side, and this site does not choose between them:

- black pigs are said to have come from Ryūkyū about 400 years ago and been introduced by the lords of the Shimazu domain;
- in the Meiji era they were crossed with the Berkshire breed from England.

The farm ministry’s children’s Q&A gives the breed’s background: in Japan "black pork" means the Berkshire, which originated in the county of Berkshire in England and came to Japan in the Meiji era. It is black all over except the tip of the nose, the feet and the tip of the tail. It grows more slowly than other breeds and has smaller litters.

**How it became a brand.** Kagoshima’s research report tells the story. In the Shōwa 30s (1955–64), black pigs fed on sweet potatoes from the volcanic *shirasu* plateau were shipped alive to Tokyo, won over the city, and became Japan’s first pork brand. When a trading standard for pork carcasses was set in 1961 and carcass grading began, the pigs went instead to slaughterhouses near the farms, and the meat was distributed nationally.

**How it nearly disappeared.** According to the prefecture, black pigs were the core of Kagoshima pig farming until the Shōwa 30s. From the late Shōwa 40s farms moved to white pigs, and by 1975 black pigs were 1.6 percent of shipments. Producers kept the breed going, and it became a brand known across Japan.

## What makes it kagoshima kurobuta

The farm ministry’s *aff* lists the two standards of the Kagoshima Kurobuta Producers’ Council:

- **Breed.** All Berkshire, and not raised together with other breeds.
- **Feed.** Late in fattening, a feed with 10 to 20 percent sweet potato added, for 60 days or more.

The prefecture’s research report describes kagoshima kurobuta as Berkshire pigs born and raised in Kagoshima, fed that sweet-potato ration for 60 days or more, and shipped at roughly 230 to 270 days old. The shipping age is in the report, not in the *aff* list.

**The six whites.** Because of white hair on the four legs, the nose and the tip of the tail, the breed is also called *roppaku*, "six whites" (*aff*).

**What the producers say about the meat.** *aff* and the prefecture describe fine muscle fibres, a clean bite and plenty of savoury flavour. The prefecture says the sweet-potato feed improves the fat, raising its melting point so that it is not greasy and tastes clean. That is the prefecture’s description of its own brand.

## Obihiro butadon

The grilled pork bowl of Obihiro, in the Tokachi district of Hokkaido, is on the donburi overview’s list; this is its background.

The farm ministry’s local-food page says pig farming began in Tokachi around the end of the Meiji era. In the early Shōwa era a diner in Obihiro served a bowl of charcoal-grilled pork coated in a sauce like the one used for grilled eel, *kabayaki*. It was meant as stamina food for farmers and settlers, and pork was used because eel was expensive and hard to get. Obihiro is held to be the birthplace, and 10 February is registered as Butadon Day.

The ministry’s Hokkaido page gives a further background: Yoda Benzō of the Banseisha, a group of Tokachi settlers, began keeping pigs for food, starting with four. In the early Shōwa era a popular diner in Obihiro served the bowl as "a menu that is truly Tokachi", and it caught on. The page describes the Tokachi way as pork grilled well with the sauce on it, laid over rice.

Obihiro city says simply that butadon was born in Obihiro, that the city has many specialist shops, and that a butadon map is published.

**Who and when.** Exactly who made the first bowl, and in which year, is not settled in public sources. Individual shops give founding stories; I could not confirm them, so I do not repeat them.

**At home.** The ministry’s home recipe uses 2 tablespoons soy sauce, 1 tablespoon sugar and half a tablespoon mirin for the sauce, and mostly loin or belly. Grilling over charcoal makes it more fragrant, it says.

## Nagasaki kakuni

The farm ministry’s Nagasaki page traces *kakuni*, simmered cubes of pork, from the Chinese dish *dongpo* pork (*tonpōrō*). During the period of national seclusion it was adapted into *tōbani*, a dish of *shippoku* banquet cooking, and later became pork kakuni eaten on its own. *Shippoku* is one of the "wakaran" foods — Japanese, Chinese and Dutch — that the ministry says grew out of Western cooking arriving through the Dutch on Dejima.

In Nagasaki the method stays close to the original: skin-on pork belly (*sanmai-niku*) simmered until sweet. The ministry calls it "similar but different" from the kakuni found across Japan.

## How to eat it

The sources say little about ordering. Butadon is pork grilled with the sauce on it and laid over rice, mostly loin or belly; kakuni in Nagasaki is a dish on its own; kurobuta is a breed and a brand, not a dish.

## Not all of Japan’s pork is this

- **Okinawa** eats more pork than any other meat, according to the farm ministry, which repeats the saying that everything is eaten except the squeal. Its braised pork belly, *rafute*, cooked with sugar, soy and awamori, is on the Okinawa local-food page. I have not found a source comparing rafute with kakuni, so I do not compare them.
- **Agu**, Okinawa’s native pig, does not appear on the ministry’s Okinawa page, and the prefectural figures I found could not be read from the original document. Nothing about agu is stated here.
- **Shōgayaki**, pork with ginger: I could not find an official source on its origin, so it is not covered.
- **Tonkatsu** has its own pages.

## Kurobuta away from Kagoshima

I could not find a source comparing kurobuta in Kagoshima with kurobuta elsewhere, or Obihiro’s butadon with butadon in Tokyo. The one thing the sources show is that kurobuta made its name outside Kagoshima: by the research report’s account, it was live pigs sold in Tokyo in 1955–64 that turned it into Japan’s first pork brand. After 1961 the meat itself travelled. For kakuni, the ministry is clear that the Nagasaki dish and the national one are not the same.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kurobuta is any black pig."** In Japan it means the Berkshire breed, and kagoshima kurobuta adds rules on breeding, feed and, in the prefecture’s description, shipping age.
- **"Brand pork meets a national standard."** The farm ministry’s article gives no common definition; each brand sets its own.
- **"Kurobuta has always been Kagoshima’s pig."** By 1975 it had fallen to 1.6 percent of the prefecture’s shipments.
- **"We know who invented butadon, and when."** Obihiro is held to be the birthplace, but the person and the year are not settled in public sources.
- **"Kakuni is the same everywhere."** The ministry treats Nagasaki’s as its own dish.
