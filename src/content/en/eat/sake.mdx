Sake is the one Japanese drink most visitors want to understand and the one whose label is hardest to read. The good news is that most of the label is regulated. The words *junmai*, *ginjō* and *daiginjō* are legal terms with fixed meanings; the percentage on the back is a measurement; and a place name on the front is a promise. This page is the map: what sake is in law, how to read the label, why it is served at temperatures from near-freezing to hot, and what the regions are known for. The lineage pages go deep on one region at a time.

## What it is

Japanese tax law defines sake (*seishu*) as a drink under 22% alcohol made by fermenting rice, rice *kōji* and water, then filtering it. Small amounts of certain other ingredients are allowed in some sake, within limits set by law, but rice must always be used and the filtering step is compulsory. By method it is a brewed drink, like beer and wine, not a spirit.

*Nihonshu* — "Japanese sake" — is a narrower name. Only sake made in Japan, from Japanese-grown rice and rice kōji, may be called nihonshu. Since December 2015 it has been a national geographical indication, protected in the EU, the UK, the US and India under trade agreements.

Two things make sake unusual as a brewed drink. **The fermentation is doubled.** Enzymes from the kōji turn starch into sugar while yeast turns sugar into alcohol, both in the same tank at once, over about 20 days. The national tax agency's standard for the nihonshu GI says this parallel process, with strong yeast, is why sake reaches an alcohol level rare for a brewed drink. **It follows the seasons.** It is brewed in winter, stored and matured from spring into summer, and shipped from there.

The same standard describes the taste: colourless to yellow (amber when aged), with more amino acids and peptides than beer or wine, a gentle acidity and sweetness, and aromas ranging from fruity *ginjō* scent to kōji and caramel. The brewers' association puts it more simply: less bitter than beer, less acidic and more savoury than wine.

## Reading the label

The national standard for sake labelling dates from 1989 and has applied since 1990. It sets eight "special designation" grades according to ingredients and how much the rice is polished.

| Grade | Ingredients | Rice polished to |
|---|---|---|
| Junmai daiginjō | Rice, kōji | 50% or less |
| Junmai ginjō | Rice, kōji | 60% or less |
| Tokubetsu junmai | Rice, kōji | 60% or less, or a special method (explained on the label) |
| Junmai | Rice, kōji | No requirement |
| Daiginjō | Rice, kōji, brewer's alcohol | 50% or less |
| Ginjō | Rice, kōji, brewer's alcohol | 60% or less |
| Tokubetsu honjōzō | Rice, kōji, brewer's alcohol | 60% or less, or a special method |
| Honjōzō | Rice, kōji, brewer's alcohol | 70% or less |

In every grade, kōji rice must make up at least 15% of the rice.

**The percentage.** *Seimai buai* is the weight of polished rice as a share of the brown rice. At 60%, the outer 40% has been ground away. The tax agency gives a comparison: table rice is polished to about 92%, and most rice for sake to 75% or less. Polishing more costs more — the Niigata brewers' association says so plainly — and *ginjō* brewing combines highly polished rice with slow, cold fermentation to bring out the fruity aroma called *ginka*.

**"Junmai" means no added alcohol, not a minimum polish.** Until 2003, junmai also had to be polished to 70% or less. That requirement was removed, and the 15% kōji rule added. Every special-designation sake must show its polishing ratio near the ingredients.

**Brewer's alcohol is a tool, not a filler.** In the tax agency's words, a suitable amount makes the aroma higher and the taste cleaner, and suppresses the lactic bacteria (*hiochi-kin*) that spoil sake. In ginjō and honjōzō it is capped at 10% of the weight of the rice.

**Other words you may see.** *Nama*: never heated. *Genshu*: not diluted with water. *Ki-ippon*: junmai brewed at a single brewery. A rice variety (e.g. "Yamada Nishiki 100%") may be named if it is more than half the rice. A region name may be used only if all of the sake was brewed there; a blend from different regions may not carry one. Superlatives such as "best" or "first" are banned.

**Sake that is not in the table.** Anything outside the eight grades is ordinary sake (*futsūshu*). The Niigata association says it has no polishing requirement and is usually around 73–75%. Words such as "rice-only sake" on a bottle that does not qualify as junmai must carry an explanation.

## Why it tastes the way it does

**Sweet and dry.** The *nihonshu-do* (sake meter value) on many labels measures density: the higher the plus number, the less sugar; the further into minus, the more. But the national research institute warns that acidity and alcohol change the impression too — at the same value, more acid tastes drier. *Acidity* makes the taste feel fuller and hides sweetness; *amino acidity* separates full-bodied sake from light.

**By grade.** The research institute's own summary, written in 2007 as a tendency rather than a rule: ginjō sake has an apple-or-banana aroma and a *tanrei* taste — not thin, but smooth with a clean finish; junmai is often richer, with more acidity and savouriness; honjōzō and ordinary sake are often finished to be gentle in aroma and taste.

**By drinking type.** The brewers' association sorts sake into four types for serving: fruity, aged, light and smooth, and rich.

## Hot, cold, or in between

Sake is, in the brewers' association's phrase, good warmed and good chilled — a rare thing among the world's drinks — and is drunk across about 5–55°C. Warming became common from the mid-Edo period.

The association's recommended ranges by type:

- **Fruity** — 10–16°C.
- **Aged** — 7–25°C, a wide band.
- **Light and smooth** — 6–10°C, or close to freezing. The narrowest band.
- **Rich** — 10–45°C. The widest band, and the type that changes most with temperature.

Japanese has a word for each temperature, describing what you feel rather than a number. In the association's descriptions: *yuki-bie* ("snow-cold", the bottle beads with water, aroma subdued); *hana-bie* (you feel the cold through the bottle; the aroma opens as you drink); *suzu-bie* (out of the fridge a while; a brighter aroma and a slight thickness); *jōon*, the temperature of an old Japanese earth-floored kitchen; *hinata-kan* (neither warm nor cold); *hitohada-kan* ("skin warm", rice and kōji aroma come up); *nuru-kan* (about body heat, the fullest aroma); *jō-kan* (steam rises, aroma tightens); *atsu-kan* (hot, sharper and drier); *tobikiri-kan* (very hot). The association says the range from *hitohada-kan* (35–40°C) to *jō-kan* (45–50°C) is generally considered the best for warm sake, and that warming makes sweetness and savouriness easier to taste.

Two more words: *hiyaoroshi*, sake brewed in winter, matured over summer and bottled in autumn after only one heating, and *yawaragi-mizu*, water drunk alongside sake. Because sake is fairly strong, the association recommends about as much water as sake, and Niigata's brewers add that it lets you keep track of how drunk you are.

## How it is served

- **Masu** — a square wooden box. It was originally a measure for 180 ml and is now used as a serving vessel, especially at events.
- **Tokkuri** — the narrow-necked flask, usually about 180 ml.
- **Kiki-choko** — the white tasting cup with two blue rings on the bottom: the white shows the colour, the rings show clarity.
- **Tasting flights** — sets of three are common at museums and tasting rooms (see below).

Tasting rooms publish their prices, and they are the cheapest way to compare:

- **Japan Sake and Shochu Information Center**, Toranomon, Tokyo — run by the national brewers' association. About 50 sakes, shōchū, awamori and fruit liqueurs from around the country, from ¥100 a glass, with sets of three. Weekdays 10:00–18:00. The page does not say whether prices include tax.
- **Ponshukan**, inside Niigata, Nagaoka and Echigo-Yuzawa stations — five tasting coins and a cup for ¥1,000 including tax since 1 July 2026 (it was ¥500 for 31 years). Over 20s only.
- **Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum**, Fushimi, Kyoto — ¥600 for over 20s, including three tastings. Cashless only.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Region names are legal promises.** The national tax agency lists 23 geographical indications for sake as of this page's date — some prefectures, some smaller districts. Each sets its own rules. Nada Gogō requires Japanese rice of grade 3 or better and water drawn within the district; Niigata and Kyoto require local water and local brewing, storage and bottling.
- **Fushimi is not its own GI.** It falls under "Kyoto", designated in October 2025 for the whole prefecture.
- **The words are official.** The GI documents describe each region's sake in their own words — Nada "balanced, with a clean finish", Kyoto "soft and round", Niigata *tanrei*, clean with few off-flavours. The famous phrase *tanrei karakuchi*, "clean and dry", is not in Niigata's official text.

## The same name, a different glass

What changes when sake leaves the region:

- **The bottle travels intact.** A GI sake has to be brewed, stored and bottled in its region, so a "Niigata" or "Nada Gogō" bottle in Tokyo is the same sake that is poured in Niigata or Kobe.
- **The comparison does not.** What the regions offer that a Tokyo bar usually does not is breadth from one place: Ponshukan's machines hold 111–129 sakes from Niigata's breweries; Gekkeikan's museum pours three of about ten seasonal sakes from one brewery.
- **Tokyo has its own tasting room.** The information center in Toranomon pours from all over the country, a glass at a time.
- **Some sake is seasonal.** Nada's *hiyaoroshi* has traditionally been shipped from September.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sake is rice wine."** It is a brewed drink like wine and beer, but its starch is turned into sugar and the sugar into alcohol in the same tank at the same time, which is how it reaches its strength.
- **"Daiginjō is the best sake."** It is the most polished, and the research institute describes ginjō as aromatic and smooth. Whether that is best is a matter of taste; the rich types have the widest serving range.
- **"Added alcohol means cheap sake."** In ginjō and honjōzō it is capped at 10% of the rice weight and used for aroma and stability, by the tax agency's account.
- **"Good sake is never warmed."** The brewers' association recommends warming for rich types and treats *nuru-kan* as the temperature with the fullest aroma.
- **"Niigata sake is dry."** The official word is *tanrei*: clean, with few off-flavours. The same text mentions a gentle sweetness from the rice.
- **"Any sake is nihonshu."** Only sake made in Japan from Japanese rice.
