A lot of what Japan eats is not cooked at home and not eaten in a restaurant. It is bought ready-made and carried somewhere else: a box of rice and side dishes from a supermarket, a tray of fried chicken from a department store basement, a bento from a station kiosk eaten on a train or, more often now, at home. Japanese calls this middle ground *nakashoku*, "middle food". This page is the map of where it is sold and what the official numbers say about it. The lineage pages go into the department store food floor and the station bento one at a time.

Convenience stores are the other half of this story, and they have their own overview on this site; start from the konbini overview for opening hours, the tax line for eating in, and how to pay.

## What it is

Three kinds of shop sell most of the ready-made food you will see outside a convenience store.

- **The supermarket** (*sūpā*). Food is its main business; the trade ministry’s monthly retail statistics say in as many words that a supermarket’s main product is food and drink. Its prepared-food section is counted as *sōzai*. In the supermarket associations’ survey for 2025 (a panel of 270 companies, figures before tax), *sōzai* was 11.3% of sales and grew 5.0% across all stores. That survey defines *sōzai* broadly: side dishes, boxed meals, fried food, bento, onigiri, sushi, in-store bakery and fast food all count.
- **The department store food floor** (*depachika*, from *depāto* and *chika*, basement). In August 2026, food was 25.2% of all department store sales in Japan, according to the Japan Department Stores Association (figures without consumption tax). Within that, sweets were 8.7% of sales, prepared dishes 6.2%, other food 6.2% and fresh food 4.0%. Sweets, not dinner, are the larger line.
- **The station.** The *ekiben*, the station bento, was once close to the only takeaway food a station sold. JR West’s 2026 research report on ekiben describes how station buildings, in-station shopping (*ekinaka*), department store food floors opening into stations and convenience stores turned what had been close to an ekiben monopoly on station takeaway into open competition. The report dates the loss of the monopoly to the breakup of Japanese National Railways in 1987.

For a sense of scale, the trade ministry’s preliminary figures for July 2026 put department store and supermarket sales together at ¥1.96 trillion for the month: ¥535 billion for department stores (up 4.3% on a year earlier) and ¥1.43 trillion for supermarkets (up 0.8%). Food in department stores was down 0.5%; food in supermarkets was up 0.8%.

I could not find an official statistic for sales or shop numbers inside stations.

## Why it is the way it is

**More people buy food than cook it.** The farm ministry’s 2024 white paper, citing the Japan Sozai Association’s survey, puts the *nakashoku* market at about ¥11 trillion in 2023, 118.6% of its size in 2014. By category, rice dishes were 43.9%, general side dishes 34.9% and prepared noodles 9.2%. The ministry links the growth to more single-person households and more households where both partners work, and calls it the "externalisation and simplification" of eating.

**A bento is cheaper than eating out.** JR West’s ekiben report says the boxed meal remains the core of the rice-dish category because it costs less than a restaurant meal and saves the work of cooking. The same report places the rise of *nakashoku* as an industry in the 1950s and 60s, alongside restaurant chains.

**Department stores sell gifts.** The department store association explains its August 2026 food figures by gift buying: food was steady thanks to demand for small gifts (*temiyage*) over the Obon holiday and the summer break, and sweets and prepared dishes held up because people were going home to family and gathering more often, even though everyday buying was held back by rising prices. Why Japanese people give food gifts the way they do, I could not find explained in an official source, so it is not explained here.

**Why prices fall into tiers, I could not find.** It is easy to see that a supermarket box and a department store box are priced differently. I did not find a ministry or trade-body source explaining why, so this page does not offer a reason.

## Closing-time discounts

This is the part visitors ask about most, and the official record is thin.

- **What JNTO says.** Japan’s national tourism organisation tells visitors that perishable food such as prepared dishes may be discounted in a food shop’s last two or three business hours, sometimes to a fraction of the original price, with signs such as *hangaku* (half price). It also says this does not always happen. Of department store food floors, it says a visit around closing time is an affordable way to try them.
- **Why shops do it, officially.** The farm ministry frames it as selling out to cut food waste. Its food-loss page lists buying discounted food close to its best-before date as one thing consumers can do. It estimates that food retailers produced 480,000 tonnes of food loss in fiscal 2024. A separate ministry release gives 4.64 million tonnes of total food loss for fiscal 2023, of which 2.31 million came from businesses; the two figures are different estimates for different years.
- **One chain’s sticker.** The ministry’s magazine *aff* (October 2025) describes the supermarket chain Life changing the stickers on items it discounts to sell out to green ones carrying a message asking shoppers to help reduce food loss, and encouraging *temaedori*, taking the item at the front of the shelf. A 2023 issue explains why: food past the shop’s own sell-by limit is taken off the shelf and becomes waste even when its best-before date has not passed.

What I could not find: any official rule on when discounts start, how much they are, or what sticker colours mean, and any official account of closing-time discounts on department store food floors. Treat the timing as each shop’s decision.

## Where to eat it

- **Department store food floors** are in the major department stores — JNTO’s 2011 article names Isetan, Odakyu, Seibu and Takashimaya — and every city has several. JNTO notes that they are often connected to stations.
- **Ekiben** are sold at large stations from many regions at once, and at small stations as the local speciality, according to JNTO, which gives prices from under ¥1,000 to around ¥3,000 for boxes with wagyu. JR West’s report names permanent ekiben shops at major terminals run by the JR groups: Ekibenya Matsuri at Gransta Tokyo (JR East), Tabi-bentō Ekiben Nigiwai (JR West), PLUSTABento (JR Central) and JR Kyushu’s own.
- **Ekiben fairs** at department stores. Keio Department Store’s fair in Shinjuku, held since 1966, reached its 61st edition from 6 to 21 January 2026, with about 40 makers cooking on site and more than 380 kinds of ekiben, according to the store’s announcement on a press-release site.

## The same name, a different place

For food bought to take away, "away from home" is the normal case, and the ekiben shows it most clearly. JR West’s report found:

- **Most ekiben are eaten at home.** In its 2026 web survey (19,891 people aged 18–79 screened, 6,388 who had bought an ekiben in the past year), 62.8% had eaten one at home, 36.0% on a train, 14.0% at their destination and 9.3% in the station (more than one answer allowed). The report concludes that the ekiben began as food for the journey and is now mainly *nakashoku*.
- **The fair is the station, moved.** Ekiben fairs began, the report says, so that people who had moved to the cities could taste home when chilled food could not easily be sent. Today makers also ship boxes made in their own kitchens (*yusō ekiben*, "transported ekiben") to fairs, supermarkets and the permanent shops at big stations, which the report describes as an ekiben fair held at the station every day.
- **Few buy at the home station.** For two well-known boxes, Kanagawa’s shūmai bento and *masu-zushi* (trout sushi), the report finds fewer than 40% of buyers bought at a station in the home prefecture. Asked why they bought, 17.8% said it was only on sale at a station or department store event.

What I could not find is any comparison, with prices, of the same ekiben bought at its home station and in Tokyo. The ekiben page has the details.

## What people get wrong

- **"Everything is half price at closing."** JNTO says discounts may come in the last two or three hours and not always. There is no official rule on time or rate.
- **"Department store food floors are mostly dinner."** Sweets were a bigger share of department store sales than prepared dishes in August 2026, and the association ties food sales to gift buying.
- **"Supermarket deli means side dishes."** In the industry survey it includes bento, onigiri, sushi and in-store bakery.
- **"Ekiben are eaten on trains."** In JR West’s survey, more recent buyers ate them at home than on a train.
- **"There is a correct way to shop the discounts."** Officially, discounting is a shop selling out its stock, and buying it is presented by the farm ministry as a way to waste less food. Nothing more is prescribed.
