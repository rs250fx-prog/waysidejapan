Hōtō looks like a thick, flat udon in a pot of vegetable stew, and in a sense it is. The difference is in how the noodle is treated: it goes into the pot raw and unrested, and its flour thickens the miso broth. It is Yamanashi's dish, and the farm ministry's explanation of why is a lesson in what land will grow.

For udon in general, start from the udon overview.

## What it is

The farm ministry's food guide: wheat flour kneaded with lukewarm water, rolled and cut into wide flat noodles, simmered with seasonal vegetables and meat in a miso broth with dried-sardine stock. At home it is made in a big pot; in restaurants it comes in an iron pot, one per person. Pumpkin is common, and the flour on the noodles gives the broth body.

**How it differs from udon.** The ministry's children's guide says the noodles are not rested after making and are simmered raw in miso, giving a mellow taste.

## Why Yamanashi

The ministry gives the history in steps:

- A dish written as *hakutaku* appears in the Heian period and is thought to have come from China.
- It was once eaten all over Japan, but as rice farming spread it remained mainly in Yamanashi.
- Yamanashi's mountainous, volcanic land suited wheat better than rice, so it became a staple.
- Its popularity in Yamanashi can be confirmed in records from the Edo period on.

## The Takeda Shingen story

Two name stories are told: that it was the field food of the warlord Takeda Shingen, and that the name comes from *hōtō*, "treasured sword", used to cut the ingredients. The ministry's food guide says the legend that Shingen devised it cannot be confirmed in records.

There is also a saying: *umai mon da yo, kabocha no hōtō* — "pumpkin hōtō is the good stuff".

## When it is eaten

The ministry gives no particular season; it is simmered with whatever vegetables are in season.

## Not all of Japan's flat noodles are this

Aichi's *kishimen* is a flat udon in a strong soy broth, eaten hot or cold. See the kishimen page.

## Hōtō away from Yamanashi

No official source compares hōtō in Yamanashi with hōtō elsewhere. The one difference the ministry records is within Yamanashi: a big pot at home, an iron pot per person at a restaurant.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hōtō is udon in miso."** The noodle is not rested and goes in raw, which changes the dish.
- **"Takeda Shingen invented it."** The records do not show that.
- **"It was always only Yamanashi's."** The ministry says it was once eaten widely.
