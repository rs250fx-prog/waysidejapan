Three seaweeds farmed in Japan show how different seaweed farming can be: *nori*, grown on nets over tidal flats and made into sheets; *wakame*, grown on the Sanriku coast and sold mostly blanched and salted; and *mozuku*, farmed mostly in Okinawa. Kombu has [its own page](/en/eat/seafood-kombu/). For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the [seafood overview](/en/eat/seafood/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods says the seas around Japan hold about 1,500 kinds of seaweed, of which about 50 are eaten. Seaweed was listed as a tax item under the Taihō Code. In the Edo period kombu, nori, wakame, *tengusa*, *hijiki* and *funori* spread across the country, helped by being light once dried. The main ways of processing are plain drying, ash drying, boil-drying, sheet drying (*suki-boshi*) and salting.

Nori farming, the same guide says, began in the Edo period, and processed nori was an expensive product.

## What makes each one

### Nori

The nori growers' association, an industry foundation, describes how it is farmed.

**Leased sea.** Japan's coastal waters are public, so nori is farmed on sea leased each year through the local fishing cooperative, between 1 September and 30 April. When nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus run low, nori loses its colour, softness and flavour, so some areas end the season by mid-March. Fertiliser and water temperature are hard to control, and the association's writer calls nori a crop "left to heaven".

**The Ariake Sea.** Seeding there begins on 16 October. Nets carrying nori spores, attached by way of oyster shells, are spread between poles over 2 m tall. The Ariake Sea has a tidal range of about 6 m; the association says that sunlight reaching the nori at low tide is said to raise its amino acids and improve the taste, and that the gentle currents are said to make it grow thin and soft. The farm ministry's page on Saga gives the range as 6–7 m and says Saga is first in Japan for sheet nori production.

**New nori.** From early November, new nori from Tohoku and Tokyo Bay starts to appear, the association says.

**How seeding became possible.** The association credits a British scientist, Drew-Baker, with finding the spore stage inside shells in 1949, and Ōta Fusao in Japan with succeeding, in 1953, in seeding nets on land from that stage.

### Wakame

The farm ministry's page on salted wakame says the main area is Sanriku, a rich fishing ground where the Oyashio and Kuroshio currents meet. Miyagi succeeded in mass-producing seedlings in 1956, and farming began around 1960. Wakame is in season in March and April; most of it is salt-preserved and shipped all year.

The usual form today is blanched and salted. The steps, as the ministry gives them:

1. Blanch in hot water.
2. Cool.
3. Rub with salt.
4. Leave salted for 24 hours or more.
5. Drain off the water.
6. Remove the central rib.

### Mozuku

A July 2007 newsletter from Okinawa's fisheries research centre is the source here, and its figures are from that time. It said Okinawa's farmed mozuku output since fiscal 1999 had been 13,000–21,000 tonnes a year, and that over 95% of the mozuku produced in Japan came from Okinawa. Two kinds are farmed, Okinawa-mozuku and mozuku. Output swings widely from year to year depending on how well the seedlings germinate in the nursery beds. Nets go out to sea around November and the harvest comes around the following April.

## How to eat it

**Wakame.** The ministry says to soak salted wakame back, changing the water, and lists salads, vinegared dishes, dressed dishes, miso soup, *shabu-shabu*, tempura and stir-fries.

**Mozuku.** The 2007 newsletter does not describe how it is eaten. The farm ministry's list of Okinawan local dishes includes *mozuku-don*, mozuku over rice; more Okinawan food is on the [Okinawa local-food page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-okinawa/).

**Nori.** The sources used here describe how it is grown, not how it is graded or toasted, so this page does not go into that.

## Not all of Japan's seaweed is this

The three on this page are the farmed ones the sources describe in most detail. The ministry's list of seaweeds that spread in the Edo period also includes *tengusa*, *hijiki* and *funori*. Dried seaweed belongs to the same family as dried fish; see [himono](/en/eat/seafood-himono/).

## Seaweed away from home

No official source compares these seaweeds eaten where they are grown with the same products in Tokyo. What the sources show is that each has a home coast — the Ariake Sea and Saga for nori, Sanriku for wakame, Okinawa for mozuku — and that much of the timing is set by the sea: nori's season by leased water and falling nutrients, wakame's by a spring harvest preserved in salt.

No tax-included price was confirmed, so none is given.

## What people get wrong

- **"Nori is gathered wild."** The sources describe it as farmed on nets, on sea leased each season.
- **"Fresh wakame is all there is."** Most is blanched and salted so it can be sold all year.
- **"Mozuku is grown all over Japan."** In 2007, over 95% of it came from Okinawa.
