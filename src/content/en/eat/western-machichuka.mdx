*Machi-chūka* (町中華) — "town Chinese" — is the small, privately run Chinese restaurant of a Japanese neighbourhood: fried rice, gyoza, ramen, a rice bowl with a thick sauce on top. Many of its standard dishes do not exist in China. This page covers how Chinese dishes were taken into Japanese eating, where the name came from, the long argument over tenshinhan, and what the chains that sell the same dishes charge.

For the wider group of foreign foods made Japanese, start from the Western foods overview. Ramen and gyoza have their own pages and are only mentioned here.

## Where it came from

**One dish at a time, for eating with rice.** Kikkoman’s research journal says Chinese cooking, like Western cooking, was not taken into Japan as a whole system but dish by dish, choosing dishes that worked as side dishes for rice. It says Chinese food had spread little before the Taishō period and became hugely popular after the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923, because it was cheap and tasty; popular dining halls and department-store restaurants began serving Japanese, Western and Chinese food side by side. Another issue says it reached home cooking after the earthquake too.

**The noodle’s names.** The Shin-Yokohama Ramen Museum traces the names from *Nankin soba* (an 1884 newspaper advertisement in Hakodate) to *shina soba* (around 1928) to *chūka soba* (around 1958) to *ramen*, which spread with instant noodles from 1958. It dates Asakusa’s Rairaiken, and Japan’s first big ramen boom, to 1910. Kikkoman’s journal says shina soba appeared in 1923; the two may describe different events, and this site gives both. The ramen page goes further.

## The name "machi-chūka"

The writer Kitao Toro (北尾トロ), interviewed by President Online in 2019, says his exploring began at the end of 2013, when a Chinese restaurant he had used since his student days closed. The word was not his coinage, he says; some people already used it, and it gathered into one genre the shops once filed as popular Chinese restaurants, ramen shops or *chūka-ya*. The interview gives no definition, only a portrait: elderly, quiet owners, worn fronts, a different taste at each shop. He dates his Machi-chūka Exploration Team to 2015 there; his publisher, Chikuma Shobō, says 2014. Both are his side’s accounts.

Chikuma lists his book on the postwar history of machi-chūka for 8 October 2026, describing machi-chūka as privately run popular Chinese restaurants that spread after the war. Its topics include cooking brought back by people returning from the continent, wheat policy, chemical seasonings, delivery, and ageing owners competing with chains.

The national federation of Chinese restaurant associations (全中連) describes the *chūka ryōri* restaurant as usually small, with the owner cooking cheap, everyday dishes centred on fried rice, gyoza and ramen, at square four-seat tables or a counter. I found no government or industry definition of machi-chūka.

## Chūka ryōri and chūgoku ryōri

Japan has two words for restaurants serving Chinese food. The federation says shops do not always keep to a strict line, but suggests a rough split:

- **Chūgoku ryōri** (中国料理) — in principle Chinese dishes served as they are or arranged; generally larger restaurants with relatively expensive dishes such as shark fin and Peking duck, and round tables where several dishes are shared.
- **Chūka ryōri** (中華料理) — dishes from China arranged for Japanese diners, or created from the idea alone; small, everyday shops. Gyoza, twice-cooked pork or mapo tofu are side dishes for rice, while fried rice, tenshinhan, chūkadon and ramen are each a whole meal.

## What makes it machi-chūka

The dishes, mostly. The federation says tenshinhan does not exist in China; that one theory holds fried rice began as yesterday’s leftovers fried with rice; that *lamian* in China means pulled noodles, quite unlike Japanese ramen; and that Chinese gyoza are boiled where Japanese gyoza are pan-fried.

A 2018 paper in the journal *Kokusai-Joho* by Masuko (増子保志) groups tenshinhan, chūkadon and Cantonese-style noodles as dishes that do not exist in China — the standard menu of the town’s popular Chinese restaurants, absent from high-end ones. It finds the name "chūkadon" in a cookbook of 1937. The city of Kobe’s page on its friendship city, Tianjin, also says tenshinhan is not found there.

## Tenshinhan: the stories, and why none wins

Tenshinhan is a crab omelette on rice under a thick sauce. The paper finds the crab omelette (芙蓉蟹) in prewar Chinese cookbooks dated 1913 to 1937, but not the version on rice, and concludes the dish took shape after the war. Then it weighs the stories:

- **Tokyo**, as told by Yokota (横田文良) of the Tsuji culinary school: after the war, at a Rairaiken shop opened by the third-generation owner at the Yaesu exit of Tokyo Station, a cook from the Ginza restaurant Manjuen put a crab omelette on rice for customers in a hurry and covered it with a sweet-sour sauce adapted from sweet-and-sour pork. Tianjin was then known as the port for ships bringing people home.
- **Osaka**, also from Yokota: soon after the war, Taishōken near Osaka Castle, run by an owner from Shandong, put a crab omelette on rice under sauce, taking the idea from a Tianjin habit of rice with toppings.
- **Other stories**: a returnee who named a crab-omelette bowl after his memories of Tianjin (Yin Qing, 殷晴); a name shortened from a dish made with Tianjin rice; a recipe passed on by a Tianjin cook.
- **The author’s guess**: Tianjin noodles appear in cookbooks from 1952 to 1966, so perhaps the noodle came first. Why "Tianjin", he cannot explain.

The paper says the Tokyo and Osaka stories rest on hearsay, with no clear date and no matching newspaper article or essay, and ends by saying nothing firm was found. I could not reach any official account from Rairaiken or Taishōken. This site does not say which shop, or which city, was first.

## The shops, and what they charge

I could not confirm an official, tax-included price for any individual machi-chūka. Chains that sell the same dishes publish theirs. Prices checked on 2026-10-11, tax included.

**Hidakaya**

- Chūka soba — ¥420
- Fried rice — ¥550; half fried rice — ¥310
- Tenshinhan — ¥690
- Chūkadon — ¥700 (only at some shops)
- Stir-fried vegetables — ¥470; as a set meal (*teishoku*) — ¥720

**Gyoza no Ohsho** (Hokkaido, Tōhoku, Kantō and Kōshin’etsu menu)

- Fried rice — ¥693 (¥680 to take away)
- Tenshinhan — ¥737 (¥723 to take away)
- Chūka-han — ¥726 (¥712 to take away)

Gyoza prices are on the gyoza page.

## How to order it

Read the menu as the federation describes it: some dishes go with rice, others are a meal on their own. At Hidakaya, stir-fried vegetables come alone or as a set meal. On Gyoza no Ohsho’s eastern menu, the tenshinhan page offers a choice of sweet-sour, salt or Kyoto-style sauce; the western and southern pages do not mention it, and whether those shops offer it I could not confirm.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## The same dish, a different price

Gyoza no Ohsho says it prepares a menu for each area to suit local character and customers’ needs; it does not explain the price differences. Its east covers Hokkaido, Tōhoku, Kantō and Kōshin’etsu; west covers Hokuriku, Tōkai, Kansai, Shikoku and part of Chūgoku; south covers the rest of Chūgoku and Kyushu. On 2026-10-11, tax included:

- **Fried rice** — east ¥693, west and south ¥638
- **Tenshinhan** — east ¥737, west and south ¥693
- **Chūka-han** — east ¥726, west and south ¥682

A common belief holds that tenshinhan sauce differs between east and west Japan. I could not confirm it from any source I opened, and I found no official source comparing the dish at one shop with another.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tenshinhan is a Tianjin dish."** Kobe city, the restaurant federation and the 2018 paper all say it is not found there.
- **"The origin of tenshinhan is known."** The paper finds none of the stories firmly documented.
- **"Machi-chūka has an official definition."** It is a name one writer says was already in use and his group spread.
- **"Ramen is Chinese lamian."** The federation says lamian means pulled noodles, quite unlike Japanese ramen.
