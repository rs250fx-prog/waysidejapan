*Yōkan* is a dense bar of sweet bean paste, cut into slices, sold in a narrow box. It is among the plainest-looking wagashi and has one of the strangest histories: its name means "mutton soup", it reached Japan as a dish for monks, and in the Meiji era it became army rations. It also keeps for a year, which is one reason it is sold at airport shops.

For wagashi in general, start from the wagashi overview.

## Where it came from

**A soup.** The Japan Wagashi Association says the Chinese *yōkan* was a soup containing mutton; in Japan, without a habit of eating meat, pieces shaped from wheat or bean flour imitated the mutton, and that part separated from the soup to become a sweet. Toraya puts it this way: Zen monks who studied in China in the Kamakura and Muromachi periods brought it back as one of the *tenshin* light dishes; meat being forbidden, it was probably a soup with azuki and other plant ingredients standing in for mutton. It spread from temples to nobles and warriors, the soup and its solid part separated, sweetness was added, it was used at tea gatherings, and by the Edo period it was established as a sweet. The association also says simply that it is said to derive from *kan*, a *tenshin* brought with tea.

**Steamed, then set.** Toraya distinguishes:

- **Mushi-yōkan** — bean paste with wheat flour or kuzu, steamed. Springy.
- **Neri-yōkan** — bean paste with dissolved agar, worked and set. Smoother, keeps better, and the main type today.

**When neri-yōkan began, the sources disagree.** Toraya: water yōkan and neri-yōkan with agar were made in Edo from the mid-1700s. The association: neri-yōkan appeared after agar was discovered, around the Kansei era (about 1800). The Ogi tourism association: the present neri-yōkan was devised in the early Edo period. This site gives all three.

**Toraya** itself was founded in Kyoto in the late Muromachi period, by its own account.

## Ogi yōkan, Saga

The Ogi city tourism association tells how a town in Saga became a yōkan centre:

- **The sugar road.** In the Edo period, sugar came from Nagasaki along the Nagasaki Kaidō — the "Sugar Road" — and in Ogi it went into yōkan.
- **The army.** Yōkan makers multiplied after the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95. Yōkan kept well and was wanted as portable, preserved food for soldiers; during that war it was sent to the front as canteen goods. Ogi lay between the navy at Sasebo and the army at Kurume. By the Taishō era there were 29 makers.
- **Wider sales.** Quoting the town history, the association says sales were local in the Meiji era and later reached Nagasaki, Fukuoka, Tokyo, Korea and Manchuria.

**Who started it** is not settled. One Ogi maker, Tenzan Honpo, says the originator is held to be Morinaga Sōkichi, with a start given as 1872 or 1875, and that it was called *sakura-yōkan* after being sold at the entrance to the Sakuraoka park; it adds that it won a prize at a national confectionery show in Kyoto in 1902 and that the Russo-Japanese War brought a boom. It words all of this as "said" and "thought". I have not found an official date, so I say the start is disputed.

## How to keep it and eat it

- **Unopened**, Toraya gives one year from manufacture, and says it can still be eaten for a year after that date. Its date includes a safety factor of 0.8–0.9.
- **Opened**, seal the cut end and keep it at room temperature; eat soon. Exposed to air, the sugar may crystallise or mould may appear within a week.
- **With drink.** The association suggests about 1 cm cubes if serving it with spirits.

## Yōkan away from home

- **What you find depends on the shop.** Toraya's company-run shops handle its fresh sweets; its 71 counters in shopping centres and airports (eight at airports) sell long-keeping goods, and products differ by shop. The Kyoto-only and airport-only bars exist only where their names say.
- **Shelf life made it travel.** Ogi's yōkan reached Tokyo and beyond because it kept. The same is why it is an airport gift today.
- **No price comparison.** I found no official comparison of yōkan prices at the source and in Tokyo.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yōkan has mutton in it."** The name does; the sweet never did in Japan.
- **"Yōkan is a jelly."** Neri-yōkan is bean paste set with agar; mushi-yōkan is steamed with flour.
- **"Yōkan goes off quickly."** Unopened, a year from manufacture at Toraya.
- **"The date it was invented is known."** Three sources give three different eras for neri-yōkan.
