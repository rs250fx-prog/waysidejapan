*Ōmisoka*, 31 December, is the last day of the year, and its food is a bowl of buckwheat noodles: *toshikoshi soba*, "year-crossing soba". It sits between the solstice pumpkin and the New Year box of *osechi*, and like both it comes with reasons attached. The farm ministry gives three, side by side. It also records that the custom is far from uniform. The name, the day, the noodles and the broth all change from place to place.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the [overview of food for annual events](/en/eat/seasonal/). For the noodle itself, see the [soba overview](/en/eat/soba/).

## Where it came from

The ministry’s *aff* magazine says the habit of eating soba on New Year’s Eve is said to have spread among ordinary people in the Edo period. It does not name a year, a city or a founding shop, and this page does not add one.

New Year’s Eve is not one of the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*. It is the day before the first of the New Year days, and its food leads into the [New Year food](/en/eat/seasonal-new-year/) that follows.

## What makes it toshikoshi soba

The *aff* magazine gives three meanings, and does not choose between them:

- **Long and thin.** The shape of the noodles stands for a long life and for the family’s fortunes stretching out.
- **Easy to break.** Soba noodles break easily, so eating them is said to cut off the hardships and misfortunes of the year just ending.
- **Tough in the field.** Buckwheat is a crop that stands up to wind and rain, so following its example is a wish to stay free of illness.

The first is about what lies ahead, the second about what is left behind, the third about the plant rather than the noodle. The three sit together in the same article, and none is given as the original.

## Not all of Japan does this the same way

The *aff* magazine lists four ways the custom varies.

**The name.** Besides *toshikoshi soba*, it is called *ōmisoka soba* (New Year’s Eve soba), *toshitori soba* (soba for taking on a year), *jumyō soba* (life-span soba) and *shian soba*. The magazine gives the names without explaining each, and this page does not explain them either.

**The day.** In Aizu it is eaten on New Year’s Day, as *gantan soba*. In Niigata it is eaten on New Year’s Day, or on 14 January as *jūyokka soba*, fourteenth-day soba.

**The kind of soba.** Kyoto has *nishin soba*, topped with herring. Niigata has *hegi soba*.

**The broth.** In Kanto the broth is made with dried bonito and dark soy sauce. In Kansai it is made with kelp and dried bonito, and light soy sauce.

The ministry’s regional pages add more:

- **Fukui.** The ministry’s local-food page for *oroshi soba*, soba served with cold broth and grated daikon radish, says it is eaten as toshikoshi soba on New Year’s Eve, as well as to close wedding feasts and Buddhist services. It says the dish is traced to buckwheat grown as wartime emergency food when Asakura Takakage made his seat at Ichijōdani from 1473, and to the lord Honda Tomimasa, who came to Fuchū from 1601 with soba makers and spread thin soba topped with grated daikon. The [regional food overview for Hokuriku](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokuriku/) has more on it.
- **Kagoshima.** The Kyushu office introduces *soba-zui*, a soba porridge. It describes it as a home dish eaten through the cold months, not only on New Year’s Eve.
- **Taketa, Ōita.** Not soba, but a New Year’s Eve dish all the same. The ministry’s page on *atama-ryōri* says that in this inland town, far from the sea, salt-water fish were precious in the Edo period, so large fish were blanched and eaten down to the gills, jaw and innards. It names celebrations, New Year’s Eve and New Year guests as the occasions, and says some places still serve it if booked ahead.

These are differences within Japan, not a "real" version and copies. The ministry records them side by side.

## How to eat it

The *aff* magazine describes meanings, names, days and broths, not a set way of eating. It gives no hour and no rule about finishing the bowl, and this page does not add one. Where the custom has its own day, as in Aizu and Niigata, that is the time to eat it there.

For how soba is ordered and eaten in a shop, the [soba overview](/en/eat/soba/) covers the rest.

## What people get wrong

- **"Toshikoshi soba has one meaning."** The ministry gives three: a long life, cutting off the year’s troubles, and good health.
- **"Everyone eats it on 31 December."** Aizu eats it on New Year’s Day, and Niigata on New Year’s Day or 14 January.
- **"It is always the same bowl."** Kyoto tops it with herring, Niigata uses hegi soba, Fukui serves it with grated daikon, and the broth differs between Kanto and Kansai.
- **"It is an ancient court custom."** The ministry says it spread among ordinary people in the Edo period.
