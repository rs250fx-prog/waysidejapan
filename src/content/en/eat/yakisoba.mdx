*Yakisoba* means "fried soba", but there is no buckwheat in it. It is a wheat noodle of the Chinese kind, fried on a griddle or in a pan, and — in the version this page is about — seasoned with Worcestershire sauce. It is festival-stall food, and it is also the food of a handful of towns that turned it into their name. This page is the map: where the sauce version is thought to come from, what the towns changed, and what I could not confirm. The lineage pages go deeper on one town at a time.

## What it is

Chinese-style wheat noodles, fried, and seasoned with sauce. What goes in with them depends on the town and the shop. The noodles are cooked on a hot surface rather than served in soup — the same idea as Nagasaki's thick-noodle *sara udon*, which is fried until no soup is left, and the opposite of a noodle stewed in its soup, like champon.

## Why it is the way it is

The best account I have of its origin comes from a sauce company, and it is worth reading as one.

**From Western food to stall food.** Otafuku, a sauce maker, traces a chain on its own site. In late Edo-period Tokyo there was *mojiyaki*, batter drawn on a griddle. In the Meiji 30s (the late 1890s and early 1900s) street stalls imitating Western food (*yōshoku*) used Worcestershire sauce in what the company calls "parody dishes", the start of okonomiyaki. Then, from the end of Meiji into early Taishō, noodle makers for Chinese noodles appeared, centred on Asakusa, and a dish imitating Chinese fried noodles (*chāmen*) with Worcestershire sauce added is thought to be the prototype. Sauce yakisoba, the company says, is thought to have been born and established in Tokyo in the Taishō era. Every step is written as "is thought to"; I have kept that.

**After the war.** Otafuku's page says nothing about the postwar years, and I could not find a public or company primary source for the familiar story that sauce yakisoba spread through the black markets. This page does not tell it.

**It suits a stall.** Otafuku calls sauce yakisoba a standard of festival stalls. The towns tell the same kind of story: Ota says that in the Shōwa 20s and 30s (roughly 1945–65) there were many yakisoba shops, stalls among them, on the approach to the temple Daikōin; Yokote's yakisoba is said to have started just after the Second World War with a man who ran an okonomiyaki stall and was looking for a new dish to make on his griddle.

**It travels.** Ota gives a reason its yakisoba took hold that is about the dish, not the town: it was cheap and filling, and because it has no soup, it does not go soggy.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Three towns, one exchange.** Ota's official page says that at the time it was formed, Yokote, Fujinomiya and Ota were the only three "yakisoba towns" in Japan, and they joined in an exchange they named the *Sankoku Dōmen*. This is where the phrase "Japan's three great yakisoba" comes from in practice; I found no official definition of the phrase itself.
- **A contest made two of them famous.** On the B-1 Grand Prix's own record, Fujinomiya won gold at the first contest (2006, Hachinohe) and the second (2007, Fujinomiya). At the fourth, held in Yokote in 2009, a Yokote yakisoba group won gold and Fujinomiya placed tenth. The same Yokote group had taken silver at the first.
- **Ota defines no taste.** Ota's shop association, formed in 2002, counts 44 shops including supporting members and says each shop's taste is its own. Neither the city nor the association describes a common noodle, topping or sauce.

## Where to eat it

- **Fujinomiya**, Shizuoka — firm, oil-coated steamed noodles from the town's own noodle makers, with pork crackling and sardine powder.
- **Yokote**, Akita — thick, straight boiled noodles, a soft fried egg on top and *fukujinzuke* pickles on the side.
- **Ota**, Gunma — 44 association shops, each with its own sauce.
- **A festival.** Otafuku calls sauce yakisoba a standard of festival stalls.

## The yakisoba bun

*Yakisoba-pan* — yakisoba in a soft bread roll — has a story told by two organisations, both of which say there is more than one theory.

The Morinaga Angel Foundation says it may have begun at Nozawaya, a shop that stood in Minami-Senju, Arakawa ward, Tokyo. In 1952 the shop sold bread rolls (*koppe-pan*) and yakisoba separately, and a customer said it was a bother and asked for the noodles to be put in the bread. The bakery company Camelia calls Nozawaya the leading theory, gives the price as ¥15 a roll in 1952, says it spread across Tokyo, and says Nozawaya closed in 2010.

Both are a foundation's and a company's articles. I found no record from 1952 itself, so this is a theory, and this site presents it as one.

## The same name, a different place

What changes when yakisoba leaves home is mostly the ingredients you can get.

- **Yokote's noodles and sauce stay mostly in Akita.** The Patent Office's page on the Yokote yakisoba trademark says the dedicated noodles and sauce were sold mainly within Yokote and were hard to buy elsewhere, and are now carried by supermarkets in Akita prefecture. How easy they are to find in Tokyo or elsewhere, I could not find.
- **Fujinomiya's noodles come from Fujinomiya.** The town's yakisoba society names four noodle makers in the city. I found no comparison of Fujinomiya yakisoba made in the town and outside it.
- **The town names are brands.** "Yokote yakisoba" is a regional collective trademark held by the town's yakisoba cooperative.

I found no source that compares the taste of any of these at home and away, and this page does not.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yakisoba is made with soba."** It is made with Chinese-style wheat noodles. Otafuku traces it to an imitation of Chinese fried noodles.
- **"Yakisoba is a postwar black-market food."** You may hear its postwar spread told that way, but I could not find a primary source for it. The sauce company's account puts its birth in Taishō-era Tokyo.
- **"There is an official top three."** Three towns made an exchange together. Nobody, as far as I could find, ranks them.
- **"Every regional yakisoba has a recipe."** Yokote's is described in its trademark. Fujinomiya's city page describes its noodle and toppings. Ota's association says only that each shop's taste is unique.
- **"The yakisoba bun has a known inventor."** It has a leading theory — a shop in Minami-Senju in 1952 — told by a foundation and a bakery, not a documented fact.
