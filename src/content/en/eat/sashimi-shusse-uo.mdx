On a Japanese menu the same fish can appear under several names, and not because of a translation problem. *Buri* (Japanese amberjack, often called yellowtail) and *suzuki* (Japanese sea bass) are *shusse-uo*, "promotion fish": like a man rising through the ranks, they take a new name at each size. The names differ by region, and so do the official accounts of when the fish is at its best.

For sashimi in general — cuts, labels, safety — start from the sashimi overview.

## The names

**Buri.** Chiba prefecture calls it a promotion fish, like suzuki:

- **Kantō**: wakashi → inada → warasa → buri.
- **Kansai**: tsubasu → hamachi → mejiro → buri.

Mie prefecture names it by age: *inada* at one year, *warasa* at two, *buri* at three.

**Toyama** has more. A 2015 column in the Sasakawa Peace Foundation's Ocean Newsletter by Nakai Seiichi, a professor at the University of Toyama, maps them: in eastern Toyama, tsubaiso → fukuragi → ganto → buri (or tsubaiso → fukuragi → hamachi → buri); in the west, kozukura → fukuragi → ganto → buri; at Nadaura in Himi, five stages — kozukura → fukuragi → nimaizuru → ganto → buri. Inland, three stages are common; in the mountains, one or two (fukuragi → buri, or just buri). The line between ganto and buri is given as 5 kg. That is a researcher's column, not an official source.

**Suzuki.** Chiba gives seigo → fukko → suzuki. It says Tokyo Bay leads Japan's landings at 2,000 tonnes a year, and that suzuki is famous in summer as *arai*.

## What hamachi means

In the Kansai sequence, *hamachi* is a young buri. The consumer agency's labelling Q&A uses "tuna (wild) and hamachi (farmed)" as an example of a mixed platter, and that example is sometimes read as "hamachi means farmed". I found no official source saying so, and the regional naming above shows hamachi is first a size name. Ask, or read the label.

## When it is best

The sources disagree, and this page leaves them side by side:

- **Chiba**: winter, as *kan-buri*, "cold-season buri", is when it is fattest; summer's inada has less fat but a lighter taste.
- **Mie**: in western Japan, including the Kumano coast, spring is the season; wild buri are caught from February to April, and farmed buri all year. Its main dishes are sashimi, teriyaki, buri with radish, and salt-grilled.

## Handling the fish

Hokkaido's research institute explains why handling matters for buri and mackerel. *Ikejime* is the general term for treating a live fish to keep its quality after death; its example cuts the spinal cord to kill at once and cuts the gills to bleed it, on board. *Nojime* is letting the catch die untreated, for example in iced holds. Killing at once avoids the muscle activity of a struggling death, saves the fish's energy compound and delays rigor, it says. Its freshness guide: a K value of 20% or less for eating raw, though the rate of change differs greatly between species.

## The same fish, a different name

- **Order by local name.** The same fish is *inada* in Tokyo and *hamachi* in Osaka at a similar size, and Toyama has names of its own.
- **Season depends on the source.** A winter fish in Chiba, a spring one on Mie's coast.
- **No official ranking.** No source compares buri eaten at Himi with buri eaten in Tokyo, so this page does not.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hamachi and buri are different fish."** Hamachi is a stage of buri in the Kansai names.
- **"Hamachi always means farmed."** No official source I found says so.
- **"Buri is only good in winter."** Chiba says winter; Mie says spring.
- **"The names are the same everywhere."** Toyama alone has several sequences.
