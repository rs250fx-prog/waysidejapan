A *sunakku* (スナック, "snack" or "snack bar") is, in JNTO's words to visitors, a bar led by a hostess or a "Mama" that offers conversation, karaoke and hospitality. The name tells you less than it seems. Under Japanese rules, what decides how a snack is regulated is not the sign but what the staff do, and in particular whether they sit with you and entertain you. This page sets out what the official classifications say, where the police draw the line, what is known about the bill, and the warnings Japanese authorities give visitors about bars.

For izakaya in general, start from the izakaya overview.

## Where it came from

I could not find the origin of the word or the history of the snack bar in Japan in any official or trade-body source, so this page does not tell it. What follows is how the business is classified today.

JNTO describes snacks as a hostess- or Mama-led bar with conversation, karaoke and hospitality (2024, in a piece on Hakodate), and elsewhere as an intimate, often smoky, late-night hideaway where the owner welcomes and looks after you (2021). Those are tourism descriptions, not legal ones.

## What makes it a snack

Official sources classify the snack in more than one way.

- **Japan Standard Industrial Classification** (revised July 2023): group 7661, "bars, cabarets and nightclubs", covers places that mainly serve Western liquor and food and let customers enjoy themselves. "Snack bar" is one of its examples. The "authentic bar" goes in a different group, 7651.
- **Japan Finance Corporation**, in its table of businesses eligible for hygiene-sector loans, puts the snack in three places. Under the "social" (*shakō*) business it is a restaurant holding an adult-entertainment business permit, such as a snack or bar. "Snack" also appears as an example of "other restaurants". And "snack (mainly a coffee shop)" appears under coffee shops. The same name, in other words, can fall into different categories depending on the permit and what the place actually does.
- **The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's** productivity manual for the social business groups "bars, snacks, pubs and drinking places" together in a usage survey. It mentions daytime snacks and "care snacks" appearing, and suggests keeping a ledger of customers' kept bottles to improve the business. The manual gives no year of publication; it refers to fiscal 2019.

## The line the police draw

The rules turn on *settai* (接待), usually translated as entertaining customers. The National Police Agency's guidance on the adult-entertainment business law, issued on 27 June 2024, defines it as entertaining customers in a way that creates a festive, pleasure-seeking atmosphere: conversation or service for a particular customer or group that goes beyond what normally comes with eating and drinking.

Its examples:

- **Counts as settai:** sitting close to a particular few customers and continuing to talk with them or serve them drinks. Sitting close, urging them to sing, clapping along and singing with them. Close bodily contact, holding hands, or feeding a customer by bringing food or drink to their mouth (a social handshake, or contact needed to look after a drunk customer, is excepted).
- **Does not count:** pouring a drink or making a whisky-and-water and then leaving; waiting behind customers, or serving drinks to order from behind the counter; greetings and a little small talk that go with these. Inviting customers in general to sing without being near them, clapping, or setting up the karaoke when asked.

Tokyo's Metropolitan Police list of business types gives the two categories this produces:

- **Adult-entertainment business, type 1** (entertaining customers while they eat and drink): cabarets, *machiai*, restaurants, cafés and others that have facilities to entertain customers while they enjoy themselves, eat or drink. This needs a permit.
- **Late-night liquor-serving restaurant business**: bars, drinking places and the like that serve alcohol between midnight and 6:00. The police agency's guidance defines these as restaurants that serve alcohol, excluding those that normally serve what is recognised as a staple meal, and says any drink of 1% alcohol or more counts, regardless of quantity. The Tokyo police page lists this separately, under a notification heading, but does not explain how a notification differs from a permit.

So two places with "snack" on the sign can fall under different rules: one where the Mama chats with you from behind the counter, and one where staff sit with you and sing along.

**What I did not confirm.** The law was amended in 2025, and a new version of the guidance has been issued, but I have not read the new text. This page therefore does not say what the current guidance contains, or give the hours rules for type-1 businesses after midnight.

**The 2025 amendment.** The police agency's summary, prompted by problems at host clubs, says the amendment added duties for businesses that entertain customers: no false explanations of charges, no demanding food or drink by playing on a customer's romantic feelings, and no serving food or drink the customer did not order. Intimidating customers into ordering or paying became a prohibited act with penalties. The penalty for running without a permit rose from up to two years to up to five years in prison, and the fine from up to ¥2 million to up to ¥10 million. The summary does not give the date it took effect, and I did not confirm it.

## The bill

Very little about how bars and snacks charge is set out in official sources.

- **Cover charges.** The National Consumer Affairs Center says an izakaya's *otoshi* can be thought of as a table charge. If you are billed a separate "charge" on top of the otoshi, or an amount seems wrong, it advises asking staff before you pay. I found no official source on how much bars or snacks charge for a seat or how that is decided.
- **Kept bottles.** The health ministry's manual suggests keeping a ledger of customers' bottles, so the practice of a bottle kept at the bar for you appears in trade guidance. How it is priced, and how long a bottle is kept, I could not confirm.
- **Time limits.** JNTO says hostess and host clubs tend to be expensive, and advises knowing all the charges and watching time limits. It also notes that all-you-can-drink and all-you-can-eat deals have time limits.
- **Payment.** I could not confirm whether bars or snacks commonly take cards.

The health ministry's manual also reports an internet survey from December 2018 of people who had used bars, snacks and pubs. Those responses are what customers said they spent, not any bar's prices, so I do not repeat them as figures. The same survey found that customers choosing a place weigh the prices and price system, and the atmosphere, most.

I could not find a tax-inclusive price from any snack or bar's own site, so this page gives none.

## Warnings for visitors

These come from the consumer agency and the Tokyo police. They concern bars in nightlife districts generally, not snacks in particular.

- **The pattern.** The National Consumer Affairs Center (January 2020) reported several cases of people who went into a bar in a nightlife district, paid by card, and later found a very high charge. Almost all had been taken there by a tout or by someone they had just met, and getting the money back is very hard. One case, received in English in October 2019: a visitor in Japan for the Rugby World Cup was led to a bar by a woman he had just met, was told his card had failed, paid in cash, and was then billed about ¥2 million by his card company.
- **How common.** Of 195 consultations from visitors to Japan in the centre's first year, "eating out and food delivery" was second with 24 (lodging was first with 49), with trouble over menu display and the amount billed. The report does not count bars separately.
- **Shinjuku police** say rip-offs by bar touts are increasing around Kabukichō, and that touting on the street is prohibited in Shinjuku ward. Do not follow touts. If you use an all-you-can-drink deal at a bar, check which drinks it covers, since some are excluded. Check reviews online first. If you get into trouble, call 110 or Shinjuku police station, or go to a nearby police box if you cannot call. The page links to English, Chinese and Korean versions in PDF, which I did not open.
- **Tokyo police** (December 2023) describe people invited through dating sites being told "let’s go to a place I know" and taken to a rip-off bar, where the woman disappears and the staff start charging large sums. They also describe customers whose cards were misused after they got drunk at bars touts had led them to, in Kinshichō, Shinbashi and Ueno among others. Their advice is the same: do not follow touts.
- **The Consumer Hotline for Tourists**, run by the National Consumer Affairs Center, takes calls from visitors about consumer trouble during their stay: 03-5449-0906, weekdays 10:00–12:00 and 13:00–16:00, in English, Chinese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, French and Japanese. It has no power to compel or instruct businesses; it advises you, and you negotiate with the business yourself.

I have seen advice that you should call the police before paying, because a report is harder to make afterwards. I could not confirm it in an official source, so I do not give it.

## Snack bars away from home

I found no official source comparing snacks in one region with another. JNTO's Hakodate article presents them as a lesser-known side of nightlife there; the warnings above come from Tokyo. Neither says snacks differ by region, and this page does not claim they do.

## What people get wrong

- **"A snack is a hostess bar."** Some are, under the law, and some are not. It depends on whether staff sit with you and entertain you, not on the sign.
- **"Karaoke means entertainment in the legal sense."** Setting up the machine or applauding the room does not count. Sitting with a particular customer and singing along does.
- **"A friendly stranger’s favourite bar is a safe bet."** The consumer centre and police describe exactly this as the usual route to an overcharge.
- **"The hotline can get my money back."** It advises; it cannot make a business pay.
