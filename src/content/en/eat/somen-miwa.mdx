Miwa, in Sakurai, Nara, is the place that claims the beginning of Japanese sōmen. It has a legend, a set of documents, a protected name, and a grading system that counts strands. This page sets them out, including where the dates do not agree.

For sōmen in general, start from the sōmen overview.

## Where it came from

**The legend.** The geographical-indication document filed by the Nara Miwa sōmen manufacturers' cooperative says that about 1,300 years ago, the second son of the chief priest of Ōmiwa shrine, Tanenushi, prayed for people suffering famine and plague, found the land suited to wheat, had it sown, and made sōmen by kneading water-milled flour with spring water and stretching it — as it is "told". The cooperative's own history goes back "over 1,300 years" but also gives 827 as the year his father took office, which leaves the order unclear. The farm ministry's food guide says over 1,200 years. All three tell it as legend.

**Documents.** The GI document lists sōmen eaten in the *Kojiki*, Shōsōin papers (around 750) and the *Engishiki* (927); the place name Miwa in a 1565 diary; "Miwa sōmen" by name in 1736; and Miwa as a famous product in 1754. Whether the "sōmen" of the Nara period was today's thin hand-stretched noodle, those documents do not say.

**Spreading.** The farm ministry and the cooperative say Miwa prospered as a post town on the way to Ise, and pilgrims carried the craft to Banshū, Shōdoshima and Shimabara. Banshū's own history records sōmen in 1418 and does not say it came from Miwa. This site does not decide which came first.

**Modern Miwa.** The cooperative was founded in 1895; the industry peaked from the Taishō to early Shōwa eras; Sakurai city passed an ordinance to promote Miwa sōmen on 7 July 2017. Every 5 February, Ōmiwa shrine holds a divination of the year's sōmen price.

## How it is made

The GI document gives the ingredients — wheat flour, salt, water, edible vegetable oil — and the process: kneading, cutting into a board, first twisting (with oiling and thinning), winding on rods, first pull, dividing, hanging on frames to stretch, drying to 13.5% moisture or less, cutting and weighing. Between steps come resting times for proper hydration and relaxation. The stretching at the first pull or on the frames, or both, is done by hand. After the war, machines took over what they could, but the hand-stretching method was kept.

The farm ministry adds that Miwa's repeated resting stage, *umashi*, drives off moisture and gives a smooth noodle that does not soften quickly.

## Grades

- **The GI standard.** Protein 9.5% or more (from strong or medium-strong flour of at least 10% crude protein). Strands per 10 g: standard 65–75, superior 75–95, top 95 or more.
- **The cooperative's brands.** Kamisugi 120 or more, Odamaki 95–105, Mizugaki 80–95, Homare 70–80, per 10 g.

Thinner means higher grade.

## Keeping and eating

- **Shelf life.** Three years and six months from manufacture.
- **Hine.** Noodles aged a year after making. The cooperative says aged noodles are generally prized — firmer and smoother — while new ones taste more of wheat.
- **Sauce.** For cold sōmen, dashi 2, light soy ½, mirin ½; for nyūmen, dashi 5, light soy ½, mirin ⅓.

## Miwa sōmen away from Miwa

No official source compares Miwa sōmen eaten in Nara with a packet bought elsewhere, so this page does not. What the GI does is fix the method and the place for the protected name.

## What people get wrong

- **"Miwa sōmen is exactly 1,300 years old."** That is the legend; the figures vary between 1,200 and 1,300 years.
- **"Sōmen spread from Miwa everywhere."** Miwa says so; Banshū's records do not.
- **"New sōmen is best."** Aged hine noodles are generally prized.
