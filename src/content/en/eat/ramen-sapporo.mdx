Sapporo is the city that made miso a ramen flavour. Before the 1950s, ramen was soy sauce or salt; a Sapporo shopkeeper stirred fermented soybean paste into the soup, a noodle maker across town built a noodle to stand up to it, and within a generation "Sapporo ramen" meant miso everywhere in Japan. The style is barely seventy years old, and the butter and corn that visitors associate with it are a later addition that the city itself does not treat as standard.

## Where it came from

**The word.** The Hokkaido Shimbun traces a claim that the word *rāmen* was coined in Sapporo in 1922, at Takeya Shokudō in front of Hokkaido University, where the proprietress is said to have named the dish after her Chinese cook's call of *hǎo le* ("it's ready"). Takeya closed in 1943 under wartime rationing. The claim is one of several origin stories for the word; this is Sapporo's.

**The alley.** After the war, the Sapporo tourism association says, the city's ramen grew out of stalls at the Nijō market and spread to Susukino. In 1951 a row of eight small shops opened beside a cinema in Susukino; one ramen shop, Rairaiken, did so well that the neighbours switched to ramen, and the row became the Kōraku Ramen Meitengai — the first Ramen Yokochō. It was demolished in 1969 for road widening ahead of the 1972 Winter Olympics and reopened on its present site in 1971. The alley is 42 metres long and 1.8 metres wide, and marked 75 years in 2026.

**Miso.** Aji no Sanpei, a shop in central Sapporo, calls itself the birthplace of miso ramen, and nobody disputes it. The year does: the agriculture ministry's timeline (compiled with the Shin-Yokohama Ramen Museum) gives 1954, the Sapporo tourism association gives 1958. The inventor was the shop's first owner, Ōmiya Morito. The noodle came from a second man: Nishiyama Takayuki, who had run the noodle section at a shop called Darumaken from 1950, founded Nishiyama Seimen in 1953, and in January 1955 — by the company's own history — succeeded in making the aged, high-water, egg-enriched wavy noodle that Sapporo ramen still uses.

**Butter.** In 1964 a shop called Kahei put butter into miso ramen. That is the first recorded appearance, a decade after the soup itself.

## What makes it Sapporo ramen

**The soup** is miso. The tourism association's definition of the old standard is simple: miso soup, a medium-thick wavy noodle, eaten with bean sprouts and onion. In the influential Sumire–Junren family of shops the method is to heat raw miso in a wok with lard, then fry minced pork, garlic, onion and bean sprouts hard before the stock goes in — which is why a good Sapporo bowl arrives with a sheen of fat and a smell of scorched miso. Grated raw ginger is a common finish.

**The noodle** is the Nishiyama type: medium-thick, strongly waved, with egg, aged to develop chew. The tourism association's explanation of the wave is practical — the curl holds the thick soup. Nishiyama Seimen says it now makes more than 500 different noodles to individual shops' specifications, which tells you how much Sapporo ramen is a noodle-maker's city.

**The toppings** of the classic bowl are the fried vegetables and mince already in the soup, plus pork. Butter and sweetcorn are options, and the Hokkaido Shimbun's 2026 piece on the alley is headlined, in effect, "not just butter and corn".

## Why Sapporo

Honestly, the sources I trust do not explain it beyond the people. The cold-climate story — that lard and miso suit a snowy city — is told everywhere and I could not find a public body or company that states it, so it is not on this page. What is documented is an industrial fact: a city with a noodle maker capable of building a new noodle for a new soup in 1955, and of exporting it from 1975 and training overseas shops from the 1980s. Sapporo ramen went national and then global on Nishiyama's trucks and later its ships, and the company appeared in the city's primary-school social-studies textbook in 1987 as "the work of a noodle factory".

- **Sumire** (中の島) and **Sapporo Junren** (平岸), the two shops of the family that defined the rich modern style. Founded as one shop in 1964 by Muranaka Akiko, split between her sons in 1987 and 1989. Neither shop's prices could be verified from an official page, so none are given.
- **Ganso Sapporo Ramen Yokochō** (南5条西3丁目, three minutes from Susukino station). The alley's own site lists 16 ramen shops; the tourism association counts 17. It has nothing to do with the nearby "Shin Ramen Yokochō", and says so.
- **New Chitose Airport.** The domestic terminal's third floor has a cluster of ramen shops; the airport's own guide shows bowls mostly in the ¥950–1,500 range. Ebisoba Ichigen's airport branch lists its shrimp-salt and shrimp-miso bowls at ¥990 each, tax included.

## How it is eaten

There is no Sapporo ordering ritual to learn — no hardness scale, no refill. One thing is worth knowing: at Aji no Sanpei the standard sides are rice (¥200) and a house-made shūmai, and the queue in February is the city's own warning about Snow Festival week. Everything else people will tell you — eat from the far side because the soup under the lard is hotter, drink the soup with the spoon not the bowl — is good advice but not something I can source, so take it as that.

## The other two of the "three"

Hokkaido's tourism bodies, and Hakodate's city tourism office, speak of the "three great Hokkaido ramens": Sapporo miso, Asahikawa soy, Hakodate salt. Who coined the phrase is not recorded, but the three styles are real.

- **Hakodate** makes a clear salt soup on pork, chicken, kelp and scallop, with a thin straight noodle and a slice of wheat gluten (*fu*) on top. The city points to a newspaper advertisement from 1884 for "Nanking soba, 15 sen" as possibly the earliest ramen sold in Japan, and counts around 150 ramen shops.
- **Asahikawa** combines a soy base with pork and seafood stock on a medium-thin wavy noodle. The Asahikawa Ramen Village, a cluster of eight shops with 220 seats, opened in 1996, and the national timeline marks 1998 as the year Asahikawa ramen became a talking point.

## Getting there

From New Chitose Airport, JR Hokkaido's Airport rapid reaches Sapporo in 33 minutes (special rapid) or 37 minutes (rapid), fare ¥1,230, six trains an hour through the day and 163 a day; a reserved seat is ¥1,000 extra, or ¥800 ticketless. Ramen Yokochō is three minutes on foot from Susukino subway station; Aji no Sanpei is by Ōdōri station in the city centre. Sumire and Junren are in the southern suburbs on the Namboku subway line.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sapporo ramen means butter and corn."** The old standard is miso, wavy noodle, bean sprouts and onion. Butter arrived in 1964 at a different shop; the city's own tourism office does not list either as part of the definition.
- **"Miso ramen is an old tradition."** It dates from 1954 or 1958. The word "ramen" in Sapporo is a century old at most.
- **"Ramen Yokochō is the original alley."** It is the 1971 successor to a 1951 row that was demolished for the Olympics — and there is a second, unrelated alley next to it.
- **"A bowl is under ¥1,000."** At the shop that invented the style it has been ¥1,200 since September 2026.
- **"Hokkaido ramen is miso."** Hakodate is salt, Asahikawa is soy, and Hakodate's claim on the oldest ramen in Japan is older than Sapporo's claim on the word.
