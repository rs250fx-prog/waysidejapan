Ōmi beef has the best story of the three great names and the least marketing. For two centuries when Japan officially did not eat beef, the Hikone domain on the shore of Lake Biwa produced it anyway, cured it in miso, and sent it to the shogun as medicine. When the ports opened, Ōmi cattle were driven over the mountains to Yokohama and shipped from Kobe — and sold as Kobe beef. The name "Ōmi beef" came only with the railway. It is the oldest beef tradition in the country, and it spent a century being someone else's brand.

## Where it came from

The council's chronology and the ministry's GI file tell the same story. In 1687 a man of the Hikone domain, Hanaki Den'emon, devised a miso-cured beef and named it *henpongan*, a medicine name; in 1771 the domain presented it to the shogun Ienari. The GI file states that in the Edo period Hikone was "the only domain in the whole country permitted to produce beef", under the cover of medicine. In 1866 cattle were driven by road to Yokohama to be sold directly to foreigners; from 1879 they went by sea from Kobe to Tokyo, and the GI file and the council both record that Ōmi animals were then handled as "Kobe beef". The name Ōmi beef came into use after the Tōkaidō main line opened, and by the late Meiji period Shiga was sending about 6,000 head a year to Tokyo and Yokohama. The definition was unified in 1991, the regional trademark registered in May 2007 with a council and certification scheme, a registered-shop system in 2008, and the GI in December 2017.

## What makes it Ōmi beef

**The animal.** Japanese Black cattle whose longest period of raising was in Shiga, "a prefecture rich in nature and water". Like Matsusaka and unlike Kobe, the basic name is about place, not bloodline.

**The certification.** "Certified Ōmi beef" is the tier with teeth: carcass grade A4 or B4 and above, produced by members of the council's constituent bodies, slaughtered and graded at the Shiga meat centre or Tokyo's Shibaura. Certified meat carries the council's mark, and the council runs a registered-shop list.

**The cure.** The miso-cured beef that started it, *henpongan*, is still made and sold in Hikone — the one wagyu product with a documented Edo-period recipe.

## Why Ōmi

Water, politics and a lake. The Hikone domain's monopoly came from its ruling house's standing with the shogunate, which let it present a forbidden food as a tonic; the lakeside plain fed the cattle; and the domain's position on the Tōkaidō and Nakasendō put it on the road to both Edo and Kyoto. When beef became legal, Ōmi had a two-century head start and no name of its own, which is why it took the railway to give it one.

## How to eat it

Sukiyaki and shabu-shabu are the local forms, and Ōmi houses are proud of the sweetness of the fat in a thin slice; steak is on the menus for visitors. Try the miso cure if a shop has it — a slice with rice is the taste the shogun was sent.

## Ōmi beef away from Shiga

Ōmi's history is the explanation for its present: it was sold under Kobe's name for so long that it has less brand recognition than its quality or antiquity would predict, and outside Kansai it is the least-counterfeited of the great names simply because fewer menus bother. The council's registered-shop system (from 2008) and the GI give you the same tools as elsewhere — the certification mark for A4-and-up, and the ten-digit ID. If a restaurant in Tokyo offers it, it is likely to be genuine and comparatively well-priced, for the unflattering reason that the name sells less.

## Getting there

Hikone is 50 minutes from Kyoto on the JR Biwako line, Ōmi-Hachiman 35; both are stops on the way to the Hokuriku line. Hikone castle and the old town are a short walk from the station, and the beef houses are among them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Beef-eating in Japan began in Meiji."** Hikone produced it through the Edo period as medicine, by the ministry's account.
- **"Ōmi is a newer brand than Kobe."** It is older; it was sold as Kobe.
- **"Certified means A5."** Certified Ōmi starts at A4.
- **"Three great wagyu is an official list."** The ministry's GI files record that Ōmi and Yonezawa are "often said" to be among them, and define nothing.
