Okonomiyaki is one name for two different ways of building a meal on a hot plate. In Osaka the cabbage goes *into* the batter and the whole thing is grilled as one. In Hiroshima a thin crêpe of batter is buried under cabbage, pork and noodles, capped with egg, and steamed. Asked to picture okonomiyaki, three in four people in each city picture their own. The official sources are more interesting than that: they show the two styles were closer once, and they cannot agree where either began.

For the rest of the batter family — takoyaki, Akashi-yaki — start from the konamono overview.

## Where it came from

**The Hiroshima account.** Hiroshima prefecture says the dish began with *issen yōshoku*, a children's snack before the war: flour and water spread thin on a hot plate and brushed with Worcestershire sauce. In the hunger after the war it kept people going. Around 1950 it was renamed *okonomiyaki* — "grilled how you like it" — and the addition of cabbage and Chinese noodles produced Hiroshima's basic style. The farm ministry adds that Hiroshima had many factories working iron, so iron plates were easy to come by and the shops multiplied; noodles and pork became standard in the Shōwa 30s (1955–64); and in the Shōwa 50s (1975–84) guidebooks made it known nationwide.

**The Osaka account.** The farm ministry's Osaka entry gives a different root: *funoyaki*, said to have been a sweet at Sen no Rikyū's tea gatherings in the Azuchi–Momoyama period, which reached ordinary people by the end of Edo. In the late Meiji era a snack called *yōshoku-yaki* — flour, cabbage, sauce — appeared, and shops refined it into okonomiyaki. Osaka's tourism bureau offers two more theories: that Tokyo's monjayaki became the stall food *dondon-yaki*, which in the Kansai became the sauce-flavoured *issen yōshoku*; or that it began as a playful dish customers cooked for themselves.

Whether Hiroshima's *issen yōshoku* and Osaka's *yōshoku-yaki* are the same snack, the sources do not say. They cannot all be the origin, and this page does not choose.

**How Osaka's became national.** Two explanations again. The farm ministry says chains that sold it as "an Osaka speciality" from the late Shōwa 30s into the 40s (the early 1960s into the 1970s) spread it. Osaka's tourism bureau credits the 1970 Osaka Expo. Chibo, an Osaka chain, says it was founded in Osaka in 1973; Botejyu dates itself to 1946 and opened in Hawaii in 1978.

## What makes each one

**Osaka — mixed.** The farm ministry's recipe: flour, dashi, egg, cabbage, tempura bits, and grated yam. The cabbage is mixed into the batter and the mixture laid on the plate. Over-mixing draws water out of the cabbage and makes it heavy; the ministry quotes the saying that 70% of the flavour is decided between mixing and the plate. Osaka's tourism bureau adds the yam is for fluffiness, cabbage is essential, pork is the popular filling, and the addition of cabbage after the war turned a snack into a meal. Modern menus add cheese, kimchi, rice cake, and *modan-yaki* with fried noodles.

**But Osaka used to layer.** The same ministry entry says that at first Osaka okonomiyaki were mainly *kasane-yaki* — ingredients laid on cooked batter — and the mixed *maze-yaki* spread later. So "Osaka mixes, Hiroshima layers" describes today.

**Hiroshima — layered.** The farm ministry's recipe: spread the batter thin in a circle; pile on cabbage, bean sprouts, spring onion, tempura bits and pork; pour a little more batter over and turn it. Separately, fry noodles; cook an egg thin and round on the plate; set the noodles on the egg, the cabbage stack on the noodles, and turn the whole thing once more. It is steamed under its own heap.

**The sauce.** Hiroshima's sauce is thick and sweet, made with fruit. At Okonomimura, a building of 23 okonomiyaki shops in Hiroshima city, every shop uses the same brand, Mitsuwa, according to the city. Japan's agricultural standard classes Worcestershire-type sauces by thickness as Worcester, medium (*chūnō*) and thick (*nōkō*).

**Local versions in Hiroshima prefecture.** The farm ministry and the prefecture list several: Fuchū-yaki with minced beef or pork; Kure-yaki with thin udon, folded in a half-moon; Shōbara-yaki with rice and ponzu; Onomichi-yaki with gizzard and fried squid; Mihara-yaki with chicken offal; Miyoshi's *karamen-yaki* with chilli noodles; and a Kumano version with nattō and yam. Hiroshima prefecture marks 10 October as Okonomiyaki Day.

## How to order it

**In Hiroshima.** The basic order is *niku-tama soba* — pork, egg, noodles. Hiroshima prefecture's researchers say udon instead of noodles is a minority choice, that some people take two or three portions of noodles, and that squid, prawn, fried squid (*ika-ten*), shiso and cheese are the common extras.

**Spatula or chopsticks.** The farm ministry says eating straight from the plate with a small spatula (*hera*) is common. The prefecture's researchers report that among themselves it is about half and half, and that "everyone uses the spatula" is not true. Use whichever you like.

**In Osaka.** Pork is the popular filling, with extras such as cheese, kimchi or rice cake, or *modan-yaki* with noodles inside. Expect it to arrive cooked. If you see someone eating it with a bowl of rice, that is normal in Osaka; the tourism bureau says it surprises people from elsewhere.

## Not all okonomiyaki is these two

Beyond Osaka's mixed and Hiroshima's layered, the farm ministry and Hiroshima prefecture record the town versions above, and Osaka's tourism bureau lists *modan-yaki* with fried noodles inside. Tokyo's *monjayaki*, named by the bureau in one origin theory, is on the list for a future page.

## Okonomiyaki away from home

The fullest comparison of how Tokyo, Osaka and Hiroshima eat okonomiyaki comes from a sauce maker: Otafuku surveyed 1,010 people aged 20–69 online in June 2023. It is a company's survey, about a product it sells, and should be read that way.

- **Which one is "okonomiyaki".** 76% in Osaka picture the Kansai style, 76% in Hiroshima the Hiroshima style. In Tokyo the largest group, 38%, says "both".
- **Who can explain the difference.** 88% in Hiroshima, 62% in Osaka, 48% in Tokyo.
- **Eating out and in.** In Hiroshima 95% eat the Hiroshima style when eating out and 70% make it at home — but 46% also make the Kansai style at home. In Tokyo and Osaka the Kansai style dominates both.
- **How often at home.** At least monthly: 40% in Osaka, 31% in Hiroshima. In Hiroshima 35% buy it ready-made at least monthly — a takeaway culture.
- **With rice.** About 40% in Osaka. Hiroshima's researchers say it is rarely eaten with rice there.
- **Cutting.** About half in Tokyo cut it like a pizza; about half in Osaka and Hiroshima cut it into squares.
- **Sauce.** About 60% in Osaka and Hiroshima cover the whole top; about half in Tokyo leave gaps, and 40% of Tokyo respondents use medium sauce.
- **Occasion.** More people in Tokyo treat it as a snack or something to drink with.

And one domestic detail from Hiroshima prefecture's researchers: a Hiroshima household's fridge is, in their words, quite likely to contain a bottle of okonomi sauce.

## What people get wrong

- **"Osaka style is the original okonomiyaki."** No official source says so. Osaka's own early okonomiyaki were layered.
- **"Everyone agrees which is okonomiyaki."** In the 2023 survey, 76% in Osaka pictured the Kansai style and 76% in Hiroshima the Hiroshima style.
- **"You must eat it with the spatula."** Hiroshima's own researchers split half and half.
- **"Okonomiyaki is never eaten with rice."** About 40% of Osaka respondents eat it with rice. In Hiroshima, by the prefecture's account, that is rarely heard of.
- **"Okonomiyaki is one dish."** Hiroshima prefecture alone lists half a dozen town versions.
