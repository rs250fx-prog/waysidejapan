Every other lineage on this site is defined by how it departs from this one. The Tokyo bowl is the plain sentence of Japanese ramen: a clear brown soup that tastes of chicken, pork and dried fish, seasoned with soy sauce; a wavy yellow noodle; a slice of pork, a few strips of bamboo shoot, a pink-and-white swirl of fish cake, some spinach, a square of nori. In Tokyo it is still called *chūka soba* on the menus of the shops that have been making it longest, and that is also what the government calls it when it prices a bowl every month.

## Where it came from

The Ramen Museum's history of Tokyo ramen credits Rairaiken, opened in Asakusa in 1910 by Ozaki Kan'ichi, with spreading the dish. The museum is precise about the claim: not Japan's first ramen shop — it lists a Hakodate "Nanking soba" advertisement from 1884 — but the shop that caused the first ramen boom, drawing 2,500–3,000 people a day at its peak, by the evidence of pre-war newspapers and books. Rairaiken closed in 1976; its descendants helped the museum recreate it from 2020 to 2025.

The name moved with the century. *Nanking soba* in early Meiji, *shina soba* from mid-Meiji, *chūka soba* after the war when the word *shina* fell out of use — and in the museum's account the katakana word *rāmen* only took over after Nissin's instant noodles in 1958. Shops that opened before the war still tend to say *shina soba*; shops that opened after it say *chūka soba*. It is the same bowl.

The postwar centre of gravity was Ogikubo, a station on the Chūō line in western Tokyo. Marucho opened there in 1947, run by five partners, and spun off so many apprentices that it became a family of shops in its own right; one of them, Yamagishi Kazuo, turned a staff meal into "special mori soba" at a Marucho-family shop in Nakano in 1955 — the origin of tsukemen. Harukiya began as a stall in postwar Ogikubo and became a company in April 1949. In 1985 a television feature made "Ogikubo ramen" a national phrase and started the first ramen boom of the modern era; Itami Jūzō's film *Tampopo* came out the same year.

## What makes it Tokyo soy

The museum's description of the old Tokyo bowl is the one to keep: clear stock from chicken carcasses and pork bones, simmered rather than boiled so it does not cloud, blended with a Japanese-style *dashi* — dried sardine, bonito flakes — and seasoned with soy. The museum's judgement is that this postwar move, adding fish dashi to a Chinese stock, is what made ramen Japanese, and that "soy, wavy noodle, Japanese dashi" became the shape of the national dish.

**The noodle** is a medium wavy noodle; the museum gives Tokyo's typical hydration as 20–24% and a portion as 130–150 g, so it is a drier, firmer noodle than the northern styles and thicker than Hakata's.

**Harukiya's version**, by the shop's own account: a stock of dried sardine and shaved dried fish with vegetables and kelp, the house soy *tare*, and a noodle made in-house from several flours with *kansui* and a crimp worked in by hand.

**On top:** pork, bamboo shoot, *naruto* fish cake, spinach, nori. No system of hardness or oil — you order a bowl and you get the shop's bowl.

## Why Tokyo

Because this is where the dish was assembled. Yokohama had Japan's first Chinese restaurant in 1870 and its Chinatown was where the Kurume pioneer went to learn the craft; Tokyo had the customers. The 1923 earthquake scattered Tokyo and Yokohama ramen cooks across the country, which the museum lists as one reason the dish spread. After 1945 the black markets of Tokyo ran on cheap wheat noodles, and the western suburbs along the Chūō line — Ogikubo above all — filled with stalls that became the shops of the classic style.

The national survey item is literally this lineage: "chūka soba, eating out, one bowl", central Tokyo, ¥620 in September 2026. Around that figure:

- **The chains** — Hidakaya's chūka soba at ¥420, Kourakuen's at ¥490 — are the floor, and they are soy bowls in exactly this style.
- **Harukiya**, Ogikubo (上荻 1-4-6, two minutes from the station's north exit, 18 seats, 11:00–21:20, no closing day, cashless only since August 2024). Chūka soba ¥950, large ¥1,150, wonton-men ¥1,350, chāshū-men ¥1,450, tsukemen ¥1,050, rice ¥180. The shop notes prices may differ at other branches.
- **Tsuta**, Yoyogi-Uehara (西原 3-2-4 B1F, 11:00–15:00). Opened in Sugamo in January 2012; Bib Gourmand 2015; the first ramen shop in the world with a Michelin star, in the Tokyo 2016 guide, and starred for four editions; moved to Yoyogi-Uehara in December 2019; relaunched under a new owner in December 2022. Shōyu soba ¥2,000, shio soba ¥2,000, a lighter chicken chūka soba ¥1,500. It is not in the current guide. The ¥2,000 bowl is the top of the Tokyo market and, for this lineage, the ceiling.

Between ¥620 and ¥2,000, then, is the same dish at three altitudes: the bowl the city eats on a weekday, the postwar classic, and the modern "clear" style that put ramen in a restaurant guide.

## How to eat it

There is nothing to learn, which is the point. Buy a ticket if there is a machine, sit, eat. At Harukiya the *wonton-men* is the house pride and the shop takes no cash. At Tsuta you will queue. Slurping is normal and functional — it cools the noodle — and nobody will look up.

## The country cousin: Sano

Two hours north in Tochigi, Sano makes the other famous clear soy bowl of eastern Japan, and its noodle is the attraction: hand-pressed under a green bamboo pole, very high in water (the museum gives 37–38% for the style and 45–50% for the hand-pressed version), flat and uneven. The museum dates it to a Chinese cook at a Western-style restaurant in the Taishō era, and in the early Shōwa years a town of about 50,000 had more than 150 ramen shops. The soup is clear chicken-based soy; the toppings are the Tokyo set; the water comes from a famous spring. Sano organised itself as a ramen town in 1988, when a trade paper counted 220 shops.

## Getting there

Harukiya: JR Chūō line or Marunouchi subway line to Ogikubo, north exit, two minutes. Tsuta: Odakyū or Chiyoda line to Yoyogi-Uehara. JR changed its fares in March 2026 and I could not confirm the Tokyo–Ogikubo fare from an official page, so none is given here.

## The Tokyo bowl away from Tokyo — and what Osaka means by chūka soba

This lineage is the one that got exported as "ramen" itself, so there is less to say about it changing abroad than about the city that resisted it.

**Osaka was a late convert.** Until the 1990s, by nippon.com's account, Osaka was a dashi-and-udon city where "ramen does not catch on" was a saying; the change came in the 2000s, and the city is now described as the second most competitive ramen market after Tokyo. The household survey still shows the old pattern: Osaka city is 41st of 52 for spending on ramen eaten out (¥7,408 a year) and Kobe last at ¥4,810, against Tokyo's ¥9,364 and Yamagata's ¥21,695.

**Osaka's own chūka soba is thicker and plainer.** The Takaida style, from the Takaida district on the Osaka–Higashiōsaka border, dates from the 1950s and is a very thick straight noodle in a soup where the soy sauce is the point; its origin shop, Kōyōken, began as a stall near Fuse station, and Sumiyoshi is the long-running reference. It is a different answer to the same question as Ogikubo, with the noodle doing the work the dashi does in Tokyo.

**"Tokyo ramen" elsewhere.** No public body describes how the Tokyo bowl is altered when it is served in other regions, so this page does not. What the Ramen Museum does say is that the postwar Tokyo formula — soy, wavy noodle, Japanese dashi — became the common inheritance of Japanese ramen. When a shop in Kyushu or Hokkaido serves a plain *chūka soba*, this is what it is reaching for.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tokyo ramen is tonkotsu."** Tokyo's own bowl is clear soy. The white pork-bone soup arrived from Kyushu as a fashion in 1989.
- **"Chūka soba is a different dish."** It is the postwar name for the same thing, kept by old shops and by the statisticians.
- **"Ramen is traditional."** The oldest documented sale is 1884, the first boom 1910, the classic postwar style 1947–49, and the Michelin style 2012.
- **"¥950 is expensive for ramen."** For the shop that has made it since 1949, it is the middle of the market. The survey bowl is ¥620 because the survey samples the chains too.
