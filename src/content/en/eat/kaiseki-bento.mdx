A bento is a meal packed to be eaten away from the kitchen: rice and several side dishes in one box. The official sources trace it through outings — the picnic, the theatre, the tea gathering, the railway — and the forms a visitor meets today, the *makunouchi* and the *Shōkadō* box, each come from one of those occasions. This page follows that line. Station bento in detail, with prices and where to buy them, are on the [ekiben page](/en/eat/food-shopping-ekiben/).

For the formal meal in general, start from the [kaiseki overview](/en/eat/kaiseki/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's booklet *Washoku no Ryūgi* gives this outline:

- **Song-dynasty China.** The bento began as food to carry. It reached Kyoto at the end of the 1300s and developed there.
- **Late 1500s.** In the Azuchi–Momoyama period came containers of wooden board, and *sagejū*, carrying sets with sake vessels and individual plates. These carried on into the Edo period.
- **End of the 1600s.** In the Genroku era, *shibai bentō*, boxes for watching kabuki.
- **Mid-1800s.** In Edo, the makunouchi bento went on sale, made for the theatre.
- **Meiji period.** Station bento for rail travellers.
- **1937.** A ryōtei created the Shōkadō bento, divided into four compartments.

## The makunouchi: food for the interval

The farm ministry's magazine *aff* explains the setting. Edo theatre ran all day, from morning to evening, and the audience ate in the intervals. Wealthy patrons had meals arranged for them; the makunouchi was made for everyone else. The magazine quotes the Edo-period *Morisada Mankō* on what was inside: ten rice balls, konnyaku, grilled tofu, simmered *kanpyō* gourd, simmered potatoes, kamaboko and rolled omelette. With no refrigeration, the box was built on simmered, fish-paste and grilled foods that kept.

JR West's report on station bento (March 2026) traces the name to the theatre tea houses of the Edo period, citing *Morisada Mankō* of 1848.

**The dates differ by source**, and this site does not choose:

- The farm ministry's booklet: the makunouchi went on sale in Edo in the mid-1800s, as a box made for the theatre.
- The farm ministry's magazine: it appeared in the Edo period.
- As a station bento: *aff* (2017) says a makunouchi modelled on the kabuki box went on sale at Himeji station in 1889 and spread nationwide.

The booklet pictures an "Edo-style makunouchi" from the shop inside the Kabuki-za next to a Kyoto ryōtei's Shōkadō box — the theatre box and the restaurant box side by side.

## The Shōkadō box

The farm ministry's booklet says the Shōkadō bento was created at a ryōtei in 1937, with its box divided into four compartments. The name rests on a story: Shōkadō Shōjō, a painter of the early Edo period, took a farmer's box for seed and used it to hold his paints. The box is from 1937; the painter lived in the early Edo period.

## The tea gathering's box

The bento also has a place in tea. The ministry's magazine distinguishes the *chaji*, a small gathering with a full kaiseki that usually takes about four hours, from the *chakai*, which serves only thick or thin tea. At a chakai the food is a bento called *tenshin*, a simplified kaiseki. The full tea meal is on the [tea kaiseki page](/en/eat/kaiseki-tea/).

## The station box

- **The wrapper.** JR West's report says the 1889 Himeji makunouchi was sold in a thin wooden box with a paper cover reading *go-bentō* on the lid — said to be the origin of the paper wrapper (*kakegami*) on station bento.
- **The mark.** The ekiben trade association set its *ekiben* mark in 1988, for use only on boxes its members make and sell. The frame is a wooden box, the cross a partition, the red circle the *hinomaru bentō*. The word *ekiben* is drawn in the Kantei-ryū lettering of kabuki, the association says, because the makunouchi takes its name from kabuki.
- **Built to be eaten cold.** *Aff* says that station bento are made to taste good cold.

## How to buy and eat it

- **At the station.** JNTO says large stations sell boxes from several regions and small stations sell their local one at a window or counter. Eating is generally accepted on long-distance trains such as the shinkansen; local commuter trains are not suited to it.
- **Heating.** *Aff* says polystyrene containers generally cannot go in a microwave, though some makers explain on their websites how to heat their box.
- **At the supermarket.** Supermarket industry figures for 2025 put prepared food at 11.3% of sales, in a category that counts bento with other items such as sushi, rice balls and in-store bakery.

This page gives no prices; the [ekiben page](/en/eat/food-shopping-ekiben/) has tax-included prices for two well-known boxes, with the date they were checked.

## Catered boxes in Kyoto

A Kyoto city handbook explains why catering (*shidashi*) took hold in Kyoto: in a city that valued hospitality, serving guests amateur home cooking was thought rude — and, it adds, if the food was not to a guest's taste, nobody had to feel awkward. That is the city's account of Kyoto, not of Japan.

## Bento away from its home ground

JR West's report says station bento began as food for the journey but are now bought mainly as ready meals. In its online survey of recent buyers, 62.8% had eaten their box at home and 36.0% on the train (more than one answer allowed). It lists permanent shops at major terminals that gather boxes from across Japan, so that, in the report's words, boxes once sold only locally can be bought at major terminals. The sources give no comparison of taste or price between a box bought at its home station and the same box elsewhere, and this page makes none.

## What people get wrong

- **"The makunouchi was made for the train."** It was a theatre box first; the station version came in 1889.
- **"The Shōkadō box is centuries old."** The farm ministry dates it to 1937; only the story behind the name is from the Edo period.
- **"Bento is a modern convenience."** The farm ministry traces it to the end of the 1300s in Kyoto.
- **"An ekiben should be eaten hot."** *Aff* says they are made to be eaten cold.
