Gyoza look like the most Chinese thing on a Japanese menu, and in one sense they are. But the plate you are served in Japan — six pan-fried dumplings, browned on one side, with a bowl of rice and a saucer of soy, vinegar and chilli oil — is a postwar Japanese arrangement, and it is not how China mostly eats them. This page is the map: what changed on the way, what a plate costs, and what the famous rivalry between Utsunomiya and Hamamatsu is actually measuring. The lineage pages go deep on one town at a time.

## What it is

A thin wheat wrapper folded around a filling of minced meat and vegetables, cooked one of four ways: boiled, steamed, pan-fried or deep-fried. All four exist in China. What differs is which one is normal and what the dumpling is for.

A lecture published by the Kikkoman food culture research centre sets out the contrast. In China the dumpling is a food for the Lunar New Year — the lecturer compares it to *zōni*, the soup Japan eats at New Year — and the boiled dumpling (*shuǐjiǎo*, Japanese *sui-gyōza*) is the mainstream. It is eaten as the staple, the meal itself, and raw garlic is put on the table to bite between dumplings. In Japan the pan-fried dumpling (*yaki-gyōza*) is the mainstream, it is eaten as a side dish with rice, and the garlic usually goes into the filling.

Nobody knows exactly when the dumpling began. The same lecture says it is thought to date from around the Tang dynasty, and that dried dumplings have been found in the ruins at Dunhuang.

Japan knew about it long before it ate it. A cookbook of 1778, the *Takkuchōhōhō*, describes deep-fried, pan-fried and steamed dumplings, but as an introduction to Chinese cooking, not as something Japanese people were eating. After the Meiji era the Chinatowns of Yokohama and Kobe served them, and still they did not become everyday food.

What changed it was the end of the war. Two sources point the same way:

- **The repatriates.** Japanese returning from China after 1945 are said to have introduced the dumplings they had eaten there, and the dish spread quickly. The Kikkoman lecture quotes a 1956 essay by the scholar Okuno Shintarō describing gyoza shops "flooding" the town as a new phenomenon since repatriation from the continent became active.
- **Manchuria preferred them fried.** The farm ministry's local-food database, in its entry on Kobe's miso-sauce gyoza, says the Japanese living in Manchuria before the war preferred pan-fried dumplings to the boiled ones their Chinese neighbours ate. After the war a man back from Manchuria opened a diner in Shinkaichi, Kobe, serving pan-fried gyoza with a miso sauce, and it became talked about among other repatriates.

So "repatriates brought it home" is common ground. *Where* Japan's pan-fried gyoza began is not. Utsunomiya, Kobe and Fukuoka each tell their own story, and I have not found a primary source that settles which came first. This site does not pick one.

## Why it is the way it is

Two things explain most of what you will see on a Japanese gyoza menu.

**It is a side dish, so it is fried.** Because Japan eats gyoza with rice rather than as the meal, a crisp, savoury pan-fried dumpling fits the plate better than a boiled one. The Kikkoman lecture makes the comparison between rice-eating Japan and dumpling-as-staple China; I would not push it further than a comparison. You can see the logic on menus: Utsunomiya Minmin sells a set of two portions of pan-fried, one of boiled, and rice, and describes its boiled gyoza as standing in for soup.

**The portion is the shop's decision.** Six pieces is common, but it is not a rule. Utsunomiya Minmin serves six pieces weighing 120 g in all. Gyoza no Ohsho serves six, and also a three-piece "just size" plate. Hidakaya sells three or six. At Utsunomiya's gyoza festival a plate is three to five pieces depending on the stall. When a menu says "one portion" (*ichi-ninmae*), read the count.

**The sauce is mixed by you.** The farm ministry describes the ordinary sauce for pan-fried gyoza as vinegar, soy sauce and chilli oil mixed together, and the shops put the three bottles on the table for that reason. Kobe's miso sauce — miso with soy, vinegar, sugar and chilli oil — is the best-documented regional exception, and the ministry adds that the mix differs from house to house and shop to shop.

- **The chain plate.** Hidakaya: three pieces ¥160, six ¥300. Gyoza no Ohsho on its Kanto menu: six pieces ¥363, the three-piece "just size" ¥192, six to take away ¥356. Ohsho says its menus differ by region, so these are Kanto prices only.
- **The specialist plate.** Utsunomiya Minmin's Miyamirai shop lists pan-fried, deep-fried and boiled gyoza at the same price, ¥380 for six, on the Utsunomiya Gyoza Association's page, which states that all prices include tax.
- **The festival plate.** At the Utsunomiya Gyoza Festival a plate is ¥200, three to five pieces. The organiser does not say whether that includes tax.
- **The neighbourhood Chinese diner** (*machi-chūka*) is where many Japanese people eat gyoza, but these shops rarely publish prices, and I have not given any.

The practical reading: at a chain, a plate of gyoza costs less than most bowls of ramen on the same menu, and it is priced to be added to an order rather than to be the order.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **The rivalry is about a statistic.** Utsunomiya and Hamamatsu are famous as gyoza towns because of a line in the government household survey, not because of a style. Utsunomiya built its brand after city staff announced in 1990 that it led the survey; Hamamatsu overtook it for the first time in 2011.
- **Utsunomiya has no house style.** Its gyoza association, which owns the trademark, does not define taste, shape or filling. The town's identity is the number of shops and the choice between fried, deep-fried and boiled.
- **Hamamatsu's look comes from one shop's story.** The ring of dumplings with bean sprouts in the middle is told as the invention of a station-front stall in 1953. It is the image of the town, not a rule every shop follows.

## Where to eat it

- **Utsunomiya** — the specialist shops and the November festival in the castle-ruins park. The festival in 2026 is on 7 and 8 November.
- **Hamamatsu** — the round plate with bean sprouts and a slightly sweet vinegar-soy, at the shop whose story it is.
- **Kobe** — miso sauce, in Shinkaichi, where the ministry places its origin.
- **Fukuoka** — the bite-sized *hitokuchi* gyoza of the Nakasu drinking district. The city's tourism site features a shop there started in 1949 by a founder back from the Chinese mainland.

## The same name, a different plate

Each lineage page ends with what changes when the dish leaves home. For gyoza the pattern is short:

- **The famous towns spend more at home, not necessarily in restaurants.** On the household survey's 2023–25 average, households spent ¥4,051 a year on gyoza in Hamamatsu and ¥3,192 in Utsunomiya, against ¥2,110 in central Tokyo and ¥1,984 nationally. That is money spent on fresh or cooked gyoza from shops and takeaway specialists. Frozen gyoza and eating out are not counted, and Shizuoka's statistics office says plainly that the figure does not show how many gyoza anyone eats.
- **Chains and specialists price differently.** In Utsunomiya a specialist sells three cooking styles at one price; in Tokyo the big chains sell by count, down to a three-piece plate. The two are not the same kind of shop, so I would not call one cheaper.
- **The town name is a brand only at home.** "Utsunomiya gyoza" has been a registered trademark of the town's gyoza association since 2002. What a shop outside Utsunomiya needs to use the name, I could not find.

## What people get wrong

- **"Gyoza are Japanese."** The dumpling is Chinese and very old. What is Japanese is the pan-fried, rice-side-dish plate, and that took hold after 1945.
- **"Pan-fried is the Chinese way."** In China all four methods exist, but boiled is the mainstream, and the dumpling is the meal.
- **"Utsunomiya eats the most gyoza in Japan."** It led the household spending survey for 15 years to 2010. Since 2011 the top place has moved between Hamamatsu, Utsunomiya and Miyazaki, and the survey measures shop-bought spending, not how many are eaten.
- **"One portion is six."** It is whatever the shop says — three, five, six, or a wheel of 20.
- **"There is a correct sauce."** The ordinary sauce is soy, vinegar and chilli oil mixed at the table, in proportions you choose. Kobe uses miso; Hamamatsu's best-known shop uses its own sweetish vinegar-soy.
- **"Utsunomiya gyoza have a recipe."** The association that owns the name does not define one.
