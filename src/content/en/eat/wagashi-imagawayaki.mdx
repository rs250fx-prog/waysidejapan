Ask for this cake by name and you reveal where you grew up. It is a thick, round baked cake with a filling inside. In Tokyo it is *imagawayaki*; in Hiroshima it is likely to be *ōbanyaki*; in Fukuoka *kaitenyaki*; in Himeji, a brand name. The best evidence for the map is a large survey, and it is a company's, which this page says up front.

For wagashi in general, start from the wagashi overview.

## The survey

Nichirei Foods, which sells frozen imagawayaki, surveyed 14,057 people online — about 300 in each of the 47 prefectures — between 10 and 19 October 2025, through the research firm Cross Marketing. Respondents were shown a photo of "a round, thick-baked wagashi with filling" and asked what they call it. The company published the results on 28 November 2025. It is a commercial survey by a seller of the product, not official statistics.

**By prefecture** (most common name): imagawayaki in 19, ōbanyaki in 15, kaitenyaki in 9.

**By votes**, nationally: imagawayaki 8,508 (about six in ten), ōbanyaki 7,309, kaitenyaki 3,646. People could give more than one name, so the total exceeds the number of respondents.

**By region:**

- **Kantō and Hokuriku** — imagawayaki is strong.
- **Kinki** — imagawayaki, kaitenyaki and local brand names compete.
- **Chūgoku and Shikoku** — ōbanyaki.
- **Kyushu** — kaitenyaki top in "almost every prefecture" (the release's headline claims all of them; its own commentary says almost).

**Names found in small areas:** *ajiman* in Yamagata (a registered trademark of the Ajiman company); a local brand name that topped Hyōgo alone, with 1,500 votes nationwide; and *taiko-manjū* or *taiko-yaki*, *jiman-yaki*, *nijū-yaki* and *enban-yaki* elsewhere.

## Gozasōrō, Himeji

The Hyōgo brand is Gozasōrō. The company, based in Himeji with its factory there, began selling ice confections and the cake in April 1950, incorporated in June 1959, and had 82 shops as of May 2026. On its own site it calls the product *kaitenyaki*. It explains the name as two phrases in old polite Japanese: thanks — "we are grateful for your purchase" — and pride — "this is a kaitenyaki I baked". It uses azuki from Hokkaido.

## Where the names come from

The explanations usually given for *imagawayaki*, *ōbanyaki* and *kaitenyaki* could not be confirmed from a primary source — the company page that discusses them was not accessible — so I do not repeat them. Nor could I find a primary source for the origin of *taiyaki*, the fish-shaped cousin.

## Keeping it

The Japan Wagashi Association's general advice for wagashi applies: avoid the fridge, freeze in portions, thaw at room temperature, and do not refreeze. It lists dorayaki, another bean-filled griddle sweet, among those that freeze well.

## The same cake, a different name

There is no "home" version to compare with. What changes as you travel is the word: the same order is imagawayaki in Tokyo, ōbanyaki in Hiroshima, kaitenyaki in Fukuoka, Gozasōrō in Himeji and ajiman in Yamagata. The survey records no difference in the cake itself, and I have found no official one.

## What people get wrong

- **"Imagawayaki is the correct name."** It is the most common in the survey, about six in ten, but a third of prefectures say ōbanyaki and most of Kyushu kaitenyaki.
- **"Gozasōrō is a different sweet."** It is the company's name for its kaitenyaki.
- **"This is official data."** It is one company's online survey of about 14,000 people.
