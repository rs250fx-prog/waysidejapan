*Kaku-uchi* (角打ち) is drinking at a liquor shop: you buy from the shelves or the counter and drink it there, usually standing. It looks like a standing bar, and the people who study it insist it is not one. This page covers what a private research group in Kitakyushu says about its history and customs, how Japan's tax agency separates selling alcohol from serving it, and what I could not confirm, which includes every price.

For izakaya in general, start from the izakaya overview.

## Where it came from

I found one detailed account, from the Kitakyushu Kaku-uchi Culture Research Society (北九州角打ち文化研究会, "Kakubunken"). It is a private group, not a city body. It was founded on 31 January 2005 at Uozumi Saketen, a liquor shop in Mojikō, with ten initial members, no fees and no rules. It holds drinking meetings, brewery visits and tastings, and makes maps of kaku-uchi.

The group's explanation runs like this. The Yawata steelworks opened in 1901 and drew large numbers of workers to the area. For them, it says, drink was how you recovered from a day's work and got ready for the next. Workers on three-shift rotas especially valued somewhere they could drink at any hour. The group describes the kaku-uchi as a place to calm down after a night shift and switch over between work and home.

That is the group's account. Its page does not cite a historical record that settles the origin, and I found no other origin account, so this page cannot list rival theories either. Kitakyushu city, on a ward office page about an event held inside the ward office, describes promoting "kaku-uchi culture, said to have originated in Kitakyushu". "Said to have" is the city's wording. The city page gives no definition or history beyond that line.

I could not find the origin of the word *kaku-uchi* in the group's pages or in any official source, so this page does not explain it.

**Numbers.** As of November 2018, the group counted about 340 liquor retailers in Kitakyushu, 280 of them excluding convenience stores. Its 2018 survey found 94 where you could drink. Counting shops that serve only regulars or open when the owner feels like it, it estimates the potential number at about 120. It also says traditional liquor shops have declined, and fewer people stop for a glass on the way home from work, though many distinctive shops remain. In recent years, it says, women and foreign visitors have taken an interest, drawn by cheap, easy drinking and closeness to the owner and other customers.

## What makes it a kaku-uchi

**A liquor shop, not a restaurant.** The group's definition is narrow: kaku-uchi means drinking at a liquor shop, and is distinct from standing bars run as restaurant businesses. A liquor shop, it says, is in the business of selling alcohol, not a service business like a restaurant.

**How the tax agency sorts it.** The National Tax Agency does not use the word *kaku-uchi* in anything I found, so I cannot tell you how a shop-counter drink is treated under licensing. What its Q&A and licence guide do set out is this:

- A bar or restaurant that serves alcohol to be drunk on its own premises does not need a sales licence.
- Selling alcohol to be drunk elsewhere, such as takeaway, needs a sales licence from the tax office for each point of sale.
- A retail licence lets a shop sell continuously to consumers and to bars and restaurants. Each point of sale needs an appointed alcohol sales manager, and purchases and sales must be recorded in books either way.
- One requirement for a general retail licence is that the applicant is not a bar, inn, restaurant or similar hospitality business, though the regional taxation bureau can allow it if it sees no problem.
- When one operator runs both a restaurant and a liquor shop, drinks served in the restaurant need no sales licence, but drinks sold in the shop do. So the two must be kept apart: separate spaces, and purchases, sales and stock of the two kinds of alcohol kept separately and checkable in the books.

So selling and serving are two separately organised things in the rules, and a business that does both keeps them apart by space and by books. The research group's line that a liquor shop is not a restaurant sits alongside this, but neither source links the two, and I am not drawing the link for them.

## How to drink at one

These come from the research group, which gives a reason for most of them. It does not call them rules, and I do not either.

- **Glasses, chopsticks, water** for mixing are favours the shop gives free, because a liquor shop is a retailer, not a service business. Some shops do not offer them. Many have no toilet.
- **Stay 30 to 60 minutes** as a guide, because a kaku-uchi is not an izakaya. It varies by shop, so ask the owner or follow how the regulars drink.
- **Mind the regulars.** The group puts consideration for regulars first. Come in small numbers so you do not take up too much room, and call ahead if you are a large group.
- **Keep a middle distance** from the owner and other customers, neither aloof nor too close.

The group's page does not cover how to pay, what snacks are sold or what things cost. Claims I have seen elsewhere, that most shops are cash only and a drink costs a few hundred yen, I could not confirm in the city's or the group's material, so I leave them out.

## What it costs

Nothing official gives a price. Tokyo's tourism site describes its shop-counter bars only as reasonably priced, with no figures, and the research group's pages give none. Whether there is a cover charge, and what measures drinks are sold in, I could not confirm either. The tax agency's guide says only that a shop must keep alcohol it sells and alcohol it serves separate in its books; it says nothing about how prices are set.

## Not every standing bar is a kaku-uchi

The sources describe two kinds of place where you drink on your feet.

- **The liquor shop.** Kaku-uchi proper, as above. Tokyo's tourism site (GO TOKYO) presents four shops with a standing corner inside: IMADEYA GINZA (about 400 labels, ten available to drink at the counter, staff who handle four languages), Ginza Kimijimaya, Sumiyoshi Shuhan (sake and Kyushu shōchū among others) and Ise-Go Honten's Nakameguro shop (over ten each of sake and shōchū to taste). It suggests ordering a few snacks and trying them against the drinks.
- **The restaurant or bar.** GO TOKYO's alley guide says counter-only places, standing-only places and standing bars are popular in *yokochō* alleys, that some are small enough to seat about ten, and that some in Shibuya's Nonbei Yokochō seat four or five. Its izakaya guide says standing-only izakaya are not that unusual.

The line between the two is the research group's: one is a shop that lets you drink, the other is a business that serves you.

## What to drink it with

Two official guides list the standard drinking food, for izakaya rather than liquor shops. GO TOKYO's izakaya guide names grilled meat and fish such as yakitori, *karaage* (fried chicken), *edamame* and pickles, and says, in effect, that anything that goes with a drink belongs. JNTO's izakaya guide lists sashimi platters, karaage, yakisoba, edamame with beer or sake, hotpot in winter, and Western-style dishes such as chips and chicken wings. It says izakaya food is for sharing, from small plates called *torizara*.

**Edamame.** The farm ministry's magazine explains that edamame are soya beans harvested unripe, and are shipped as a vegetable, while ripe soya beans count as a grain. I found no official source that pairs them with beer. A sales sheet for Sōka edamame, written by the grower and posted on Saitama Prefecture's website, calls them a summer tradition and ideal with beer; that is the grower's sales copy, not the prefecture's view. The same sheet says Sōka began growing them in earnest in the Shōwa 30s (1955–1964). The two sources give different seasons, harvest when the pods swell in September in the ministry's account, shipping from mid-May to mid-September in the grower's, so the season depends on the region and variety.

I found no official source on how chilled tofu (*hiyayakko*), grilled fish or karaage came to be standard drinking food, or on pairings other than edamame with beer.

## Kaku-uchi away from Kitakyushu

I found no official or group source that compares Kitakyushu's kaku-uchi with Tokyo's. What each says, side by side:

- **Kitakyushu.** Drinking at the front of a liquor shop; 94 shops in the group's 2018 survey; promoted by the city as a culture "said to have originated" there; described by the group as distinct from restaurant standing bars, with free favours you should not push and a 30-to-60-minute stay.
- **Tokyo.** GO TOKYO presents a standing corner inside a liquor shop as kaku-uchi, at four shops including two in Ginza and one in Nakameguro, and mentions multilingual staff and tasting by the glass.

No source compares prices, numbers or anything else between the two, and this page does not rank them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kaku-uchi is any standing bar."** The research group defines it as drinking at a liquor shop, separate from standing bars run as restaurants.
- **"The glass and water come with the drink."** At a liquor shop they are favours, and some shops do not offer them.
- **"It started with the steelworks."** That is the research group's account; the city says only "said to have originated" in Kitakyushu, and I found no record that settles it.
- **"It is always cash and a few hundred yen."** I could not confirm either in official or group sources.
