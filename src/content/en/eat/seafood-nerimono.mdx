*Nerimono* is the Japanese family of fish pastes: *kamaboko* steamed on a board, *chikuwa* grilled round a stick, *hanpen*, *jakoten*, the fried cakes called *satsuma-age* outside Kagoshima. The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods defines them all the same way — fish ground with salt, then set by heat — and then lists a different one for almost every coast. This page covers where the family comes from, how it is made, and the regional kinds the ministry describes. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the [seafood overview](/en/eat/seafood/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry says nerimono is held to have begun in Japan in the Heian period. A prototype appears as a banquet dish in the old document *Ruijū Zatsuyōshō*, and the oldest surviving record of kamaboko is said to be a menu for a celebration in 1115. Early kamaboko, the ministry adds, was shaped like a grilled chikuwa.

It does not claim the idea is uniquely Japanese. The ministry points to similar foods abroad — Nordic fish cakes, a Spanish product it calls *angulas*, Taiwanese fish balls — and says that because making fish paste needs no special or complicated process, it is thought to have arisen separately in many parts of Japan.

**The name chikuwa.** Paste smeared on a bamboo skewer and grilled looks like the head of a cattail, the ministry says, and that is where the name comes from. It adds that the San'in region's *ago no yaki* is well known, and that Toyohashi in Aichi is said to be where chikuwa-making close to today's method began.

## What makes it nerimono

**The method.** Salt is added to the fish and it is ground, other ingredients are mixed in, the paste is shaped, and heat sets it. The ministry describes two ways of grinding, pounding (*tsuku*) and rubbing (*suru*). Before the Meiji era the mortar and pestle were the usual tools; after Meiji, fishing was powered and mechanised, and from the Taishō era to the early Shōwa era mass production became possible.

**Four ways to heat it.** Grilled, boiled, steamed or fried. Most of the regional differences below come down to which of the four is used and which fish goes in.

**The fish today.** The ministry names Alaska pollock as the raw material. It also says that in 2021 trade statistics the top export destinations for fish-paste products were the United States, Hong Kong and China, and that crab sticks are accepted in Western Europe under the name "SURIMI".

## Not all of Japan's nerimono is this

The ministry's guide lists a main kind for each producing area: *sasa-kamaboko* in Miyagi, *hanpen* in Tokyo, steamed board kamaboko in Odawara, *noyaki* chikuwa in Shimane, *jakoten* in Ehime, *tsukeage* in Kagoshima, *chikiagi* in Okinawa and *tōfu-chikuwa* in Tottori. Its regional pages fill some of them in.

- **Odawara kamaboko, Kanagawa.** White fish (*guchi*) paste mounded on a board and steamed. The ministry's Kanagawa page says it was already being made in the Muromachi period but flourished in the late Edo period, with Sagami Bay's fish close by, water from the Hakone and Tanzawa mountains, and the town's place as a post station on the Tōkaidō. It became known as a steamed kamaboko suited to Edo tastes.
- **Kuro-hanpen, Shizuoka.** Mackerel, horse mackerel and sardine ground with their bones and skin, which turns the paste grey. In Shizuoka, the ministry says, "hanpen" means this black hanpen. It is a speciality of Yaizu and eaten across most of the prefecture, and it goes into Shizuoka's [oden](/en/eat/nabe-oden/).
- **Tōfu-chikuwa, Tottori.** Firm tofu and white-fish paste mixed roughly 7 to 3 and steamed. The leading explanation, the ministry says, is that in the Edo period, when fishing ports were slow to develop and fish was precious, the Tottori lord Ikeda Mitsunaka told people to eat tofu instead of fish. It was eaten on festive days and is now a souvenir; Tottori city held a "World Tōfu-Chikuwa Conference" in 2002.
- **Ago-chikuwa, Tottori.** Grilled chikuwa of flying fish, called *ago* in Tottori. The word appears in documents over 400 years old, the ministry says, but its origin is unknown; one saying is that the fish is so good your jaw (*ago*) drops. Flying fish are in season in spring and summer, when they spawn, and Tottori lands close to 180 tonnes a year. More on flying fish as stock is on the [iriko and ago dashi page](/en/eat/dashi-iriko-ago/).
- **Jakoten, Ehime.** Small fish ground with skin and bone and fried, in the Nanyo area around Yawatahama and Uwajima. The name is explained two ways: a corruption of *zako-ten*, "small-fry tempura", or from the fish used, *haranbo* (*hotaru-jako*). According to the Uwajima domain's history, the ministry says, it is told to have begun in 1615, when the first lord, Date Hidemune, brought kamaboko makers from Sendai. It is an everyday food in Nanyo, and Uwajima's oden always includes it.
- **Tsukeage, Kagoshima.** Fish paste mixed with tofu and Kagoshima's own *akumochi-zake*, a local sake, then fried and sweetened with sugar. Other prefectures call it *satsuma-age*. Its origin is told two ways: that Shimazu Nariakira took the idea from Kishū hanpen and kamaboko and fried it to keep better in a hot, humid climate, or that the name is a corruption of the Ryukyuan *chikiagi*. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' 100-year food listing says Ichikikushikino is also said to be its birthplace.

## Nerimono away from home

No official source compares a region's fish paste eaten where it is made with the same product in Tokyo, so this page does not rank them. What the sources show is that the name itself moves: hanpen in Tokyo is a different thing from hanpen in Shizuoka, and the fried cake is tsukeage in Kagoshima and satsuma-age elsewhere. Each kind is tied to the fish of its coast — flying fish in Tottori, small fish in Nanyo, white fish in Odawara.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kamaboko is a modern factory food."** The ministry dates the oldest surviving record to 1115; machines came after the Meiji era.
- **"Fish paste is a Japanese invention."** The ministry points to similar foods abroad and says it probably arose separately in many places.
- **"Hanpen is always white."** In Shizuoka, hanpen means the grey kuro-hanpen.
- **"Satsuma-age has one origin story."** The ministry gives two, and this site does not choose.
