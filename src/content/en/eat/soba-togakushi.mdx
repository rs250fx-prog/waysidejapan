Togakushi is a mountain, a shrine complex and a village an hour above Nagano city, at around a thousand metres, in the fog belt where buckwheat likes to grow. Its soba is served in a way no other place uses: five or six small bundles of noodles laid around a round bamboo tray like horseshoes, still wet from the water. The arrangement looks decorative and began as quality control for the gods.

## Where it came from

The village tourism association's history starts in the Heian period, when buckwheat flour was prized as portable food by the *shugenja*, the mountain ascetics who made Togakushi one of the three great centres of their practice alongside Mount Hiei and Kōya. The noodle came later: the association cites a record that the technique of *soba-kiri* was transmitted to the Togakushi temple complex from Kan'eiji in Edo during the Edo period, and that after the temples became Togakushi Shrine in the Meiji era the priests kept the craft. Nagano more broadly is where the Japanese soba noodle is first recorded — at Jōshōji in Kiso in 1574 — and the prefecture's own account is that "Shinshū soba" was a known name across the country by the Edo period.

## What makes it Togakushi soba

**Botchi-mori.** The prefecture's tourism body explains the serving: the noodles are gathered into *botchi* (bundles) of a mouthful or so, shaped like horseshoes, five or six to a tray, and set down with the water barely drained. Bundling leaves the short, uneven strands behind and keeps only the good ones — which is why, the explanation goes, this became the form for offering soba to the gods and then for serving guests. The tray is woven from *nemagari-dake*, the bent bamboo that grows on the mountain.

**The noodle.** A prefectural comparison of the three great soba traditions describes Togakushi's as pale green and thin — the colour of flour milled with a little of the green inner husk — against Izumo's black whole-grain noodle.

**The condiment.** Spicy grated daikon (*karami daikon*) rather than wasabi, which also marks it as mountain rather than city soba.

## Why Togakushi

Altitude and fog. Buckwheat grows fast on poor, cold ground and dislikes heat; a plateau at a thousand metres with heavy mist in the growing season suits it, and the prefecture's account credits exactly that terrain. The ascetics brought the flour, the Edo temple network brought the noodle, and the pilgrim trade to the shrine's five sanctuaries gave the village a reason to serve it well. Nagano is still the fourth-largest buckwheat grower in Japan (about 4,450 hectares, roughly 2,500 tonnes), though the national harvest is dwarfed by imports.

Prices in Togakushi itself are mostly not published online by the shops, so this page gives only what an official source lists:

- **Togakushi Soba Museum "Tonkururin".** A hands-on museum with a soba-making room. A lesson is ¥7,800 for a bowl of dough making four servings or ¥4,800 for two, tax included, plus ¥100 for an apron, and takes one and a half to two hours; the winter programme (early December to late March) differs. The museum's soba set meal is ¥1,800. It has its own bus stop on the Togakushi line.

Nagano city households rank 13th of 52 for noodles eaten out, at ¥8,705 a year, above the national average and above Tokyo.

## How to eat it

The tray arrives with five or six bundles. Take one bundle at a time with the chopsticks, dip it — the bundles are sized for exactly that — and eat. The daikon goes in the cup. The water left on the noodles is intentional; it keeps them from sticking and softens the sauce. When the tray is empty, the cooking water comes.

## Togakushi soba away from Togakushi

The bundles stay on the mountain. Shops elsewhere that serve "Togakushi soba" mostly serve a Shinshū-style noodle on an ordinary tray, and the bamboo tray and botchi arrangement are the thing to look for if you want to know whether a shop has actually studied the original. The wider label is the slippery one: "Shinshū soba" is a style and a prefectural brand — Nagano runs an annual Shinshū soba competition with its noodle associations, judging over a hundred entries on look and taste — but the prefecture grows about 2,500 tonnes of buckwheat a year against 88,000 imported nationally, and no public standard this site could find ties the name to Nagano-grown grain.

## Getting there

From Nagano station (bay 7), Alpico's limited-express Togakushi bus: about 56 minutes to Togakushi Chūsha and 59 to the Okusha approach, ¥2,500 one way for adults, half for children, eleven departures a day between 06:50 and 14:10 in the April–December season. Seats can be reserved a month ahead online; unreserved boarding is allowed if there is room. Since April 2025 this limited-express service has replaced the old local bus for tourists, and stops within Togakushi are set-down only on the way up.

## What people get wrong

- **"The bundles are a garnish."** They are the point: a sorting method that became a serving.
- **"Shinshū soba means Nagano buckwheat."** It means Nagano style. The grain is mostly imported.
- **"Togakushi is a soba town."** It is a shrine complex and a mountain; the soba came with the pilgrims.
- **"Any bus from Nagano station."** Since 2025 it is a limited-express service with its own bay and a fare of ¥2,500.
