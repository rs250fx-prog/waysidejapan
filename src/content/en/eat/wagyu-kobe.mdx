Kobe beef is the most famous food name Japan has exported, and the one with the most paperwork behind it. It is not a breed, a cut or a recipe. It is a certificate awarded to a few thousand carcasses a year from one prefecture's cattle, by a council that licenses every farmer, slaughterhouse, wholesaler and restaurant allowed to touch them, and marks the restaurants with a bronze cow. Understand the certificate and you understand why the word is on so many menus that have never seen the animal.

## Where it came from

The name is older than the rules. Kobe's port opened to foreign trade in 1868, and by the council's own telling the first people to eat Tajima cattle as beef were English residents, who asked for more; foreign ships came to the port for it, and "Kobe beef" became the trade's word for Hyōgo cattle. Hirobumi Itō, the prefecture's first governor and later prime minister, is said to have been fond of it. For a century the name meant roughly "good beef out of Kobe" — Ōmi cattle shipped through the port in 1879 were sold as Kobe beef too, by Ōmi's own history.

The rules came in 1983, when producers and distributors formed the Kobe Beef Marketing & Distribution Promotion Association to define the term and issue certificates. The trademark followed in 2007, overseas registrations from 2010, and in December 2015 "Kobe Beef" and "Tajima Beef" became Japan's second and third registered Geographical Indications.

## What makes it Kobe beef

**The animal.** *Tajima-gyū*: Japanese Black cattle from Hyōgo's own closed bloodline, bred only from the prefecture's sires for generations, born and raised in Hyōgo by registered farmers, slaughtered in Hyōgo at 28 to 60 months (32 on average). Tajima is also, the GI file notes, the seed stock behind much of Japan's Japanese Black, and Hyōgo has kept it pure by not crossing with other prefectures' lines.

**The grade.** A Tajima carcass becomes Kobe beef only if it is a heifer or steer grading A or B, quality 4 or 5, with a marbling score (BMS) of 6 or more on the 12-point scale, and a carcass weight between 270 and 499.9 kg (heifers) or 300 and 499.9 kg (steers). The council stamps qualifying meat with a chrysanthemum-like *nojigiku* seal.

**The numbers.** About 7,500 animals a year qualify as Tajima beef and about 7,000 as Kobe beef — under 0.2% of the beef eaten in Japan.

**The chain of custody.** Every farm, slaughterhouse, wholesaler, retailer and restaurant must be a registered member. Shops display a certificate and a bronze cow; the council runs DNA checks on them. On the day this page was written the council's register listed 408 outlets in Hyōgo, 181 in Osaka and 147 in Tokyo (36 in Minato ward, 31 in Chūō). The meat carries the ten-digit ID required by law, so the animal can be looked up.

## Why Kobe

A port and a closed herd. The Tajima cattle of northern Hyōgo were small, hardy draught animals with an unusual tendency to marble; the foreigners of the treaty port created the demand; and the prefecture's decision to keep the bloodline closed gave the council something to certify. Most other brands are defined by where the animal was fattened. Kobe is defined by what it is, as well as where.

Two things follow from the numbers. The cheaper cuts of a certified animal are a quarter the price of its sirloin, at the same shop, with the same certificate — the brand is on the animal, not the cut. And a registered Tokyo restaurant is paying Tokyo rent on Kobe meat; the certificate does not cap the price.

## How to eat it

Steak on a *teppan* griddle is the Kobe way, cut into cubes and eaten with salt, pepper, garlic chips or a little soy; sukiyaki and shabu-shabu use the thinner cuts. At a certified house, order the cut you can afford of the certified animal rather than a smaller portion of the dearest — round and rump at a Kobe house are Kobe beef, and a quarter of the price of the sirloin. Ask to see the certificate and the ID number; registered shops expect it.

## Kobe beef away from Kobe

**In Japan,** the registration system travels with the meat. The 147 registered outlets in Tokyo are real Kobe beef; the many more menus that say "Kobe" without a certificate, a bronze cow or an ID number are not, and the council's online register is the test. Price is not the test — a Tokyo registered shop will be dearer than a Kobe one for the same cut, and an uncertified shop may be dearer still.

**Abroad,** the problem inverts. The industry's export body reports that in Europe, America and the Middle East "Kobe beef is known but Japanese wagyu is not", while in Asia the wagyu mark is copied and misused; and most "Kobe" on foreign menus predates Japan's beef exports and was never Japanese. Australian and American "WAGYU" is from the same genetics but, in JETRO's words, raised differently; it cannot be sold as wagyu in Japan.

## Getting there

Kobe is 30 minutes from Osaka by JR special rapid and under an hour from Kyoto; the registered houses cluster around Sannomiya and Motomachi. Tajima, where the cattle are raised, is the northern half of the prefecture, around Toyooka and Kinosaki Onsen — the hot-spring town is on the Sanin line and serves the beef too.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kobe is a breed."** It is a certificate on Tajima cattle that pass a grade.
- **"Kobe beef is everywhere."** About 7,000 animals a year. The council lists every shop.
- **"The brand means A5."** It means A or B, quality 4 or 5, marbling 6-plus. Much certified Kobe beef is not A5.
- **"Kobe beef is a sirloin."** The round from a certified animal is Kobe beef at a quarter of the price.
- **"Foreign Kobe is the same thing."** It is almost never from Hyōgo, and often not from Japan.
