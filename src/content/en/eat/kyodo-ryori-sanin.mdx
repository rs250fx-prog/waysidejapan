San'in is the Japan Sea side of western Honshu, and this page covers its two prefectures, Tottori and Shimane. Their official dish descriptions keep returning to tofu where fish was scarce, crab and mackerel from a rough winter sea, and the brackish lake at Matsue. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/). Okayama, Tottori's neighbour on the Inland Sea side, is on the [Setouchi page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/).

## Where it came from

### Tottori

**Tōfu chikuwa.** Firm tofu and white fish paste, roughly seven to three, steamed into a tube, from the central and eastern parts of the prefecture. The farm ministry says the most widely held explanation is that in the Edo period, when the fishing ports were undeveloped and fish was precious, the lord of Tottori, Ikeda Mitsunaka, ordered people to eat tofu instead of fish. It was eaten on feast days too, and is now a souvenir.

**Ago chikuwa.** Grilled fish paste made from flying fish, which Tottori calls *ago*, eaten across the prefecture. The ministry says the name *ago* appears in documents more than 400 years old but its origin is unknown; one saying is that it is so good your jaw (*ago*) drops. Flying fish are best in their spring-to-summer spawning season, and Tottori lands nearly 180 tonnes a year. For flying-fish stock in Kyushu, see [iriko and ago](/en/eat/dashi-iriko-ago/).

**Dondoroke-meshi.** Tofu stir-fried and cooked into rice, later mixed in instead, from the east and centre. *Dondoroke* is local dialect for thunder, from the noise tofu makes in a hot pan. The ministry says tofu was once a treat, and every village had a tofu hut. It was made after rice planting, at breaks in farm work and at village gatherings.

**Rakkyō-zuke.** Pickled shallot-like *rakkyō*, from Fukube in Tottori city and from Hokuei. The ministry says growing it goes back to the Edo period, said to have been brought back from the Koishikawa herb garden on the lord's journeys to Edo. It grows in the sand dunes, farmed on a large scale since the early Taishō era. In fiscal 2015 "Tottori Sakyū / Fukube Sakyū rakkyō" was registered as a Geographical Indication.

**Oyagani no miso-shiru.** Miso soup with the female snow crab and daikon, from Tottori city. The male is *matsuba-gani*, the female *oyagani*. The ministry says the female is small but in season fairly cheap in the prefecture's supermarkets, so it is everyday food. It is caught only in November and December, the soup keeps other fillings few to let the crab come through, and it appears in school lunches.

**Matsuba-gani.** Tottori prefecture calls the grown male snow crab matsuba-gani, fished from early November to March in the rough winter Japan Sea; the fourth Saturday of November is Matsuba-gani Day. For the name it gives several theories, that the thin legs look like pine needles (*matsuba*), or that fishermen grilled the crab on the beach over pine needles. This page does not choose. The prefecture's page does not give the date the season opens, and neither do I. More on the crab, its names and its seasons in [oysters and crab](/en/eat/seafood-oyster-crab/).

**Kaki-no-ha-zushi in Chizu.** Salted trout on sushi rice on a persimmon leaf. The ministry says Chizu is far from the sea, and the tannin in the leaves kept the fish from spoiling. For guests at Obon and festivals. See [persimmon-leaf sushi](/en/eat/sushi-kakinoha/).

**Igisu.** A seaweed (*egonori*) melted down and set, from the centre of the prefecture, said to have been sent to the court as tribute in the Asuka and Nara periods.

### Shimane

**Izumo soba.** Dark and fragrant because the buckwheat is ground with its husk (*hikigurumi*). The ministry names two styles: cold *warigo* soba, which began in Matsue, and hot *kamaage* soba, said to come from around Izumo Taisha, where stall-holders at the Kamiari festival in the tenth lunar month skipped rinsing the noodles. It is said to have spread after Matsudaira Naomasa, lord of Matsue, brought soba makers from Shinshū. The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page (fiscal 2021, traditional, special award) gives the year as Kan'ei 15, 1638. For other dates and the noodle itself, see [Izumo soba](/en/eat/soba-izumo/).

**Shijimi-jiru.** Clam soup with the *Yamato shijimi* of Lake Shinji, everyday food in the east of the prefecture. The ministry says Lake Shinji is Japan's seventh-largest brackish lake, its shijimi catch is among the largest in the country, and the clam is one of the lake's "seven delicacies". About 300 people fish for it, under a catch limit of about 100 kg per fisher. The clams are left overnight in water about 1 per cent salt, as in their lake, to spit out sand, and the miso goes in once the shells open. An Izumo saying, that shijimi go in from cold water and come to the boil only once, means "do not overcook", the ministry says.

**Uzume-meshi.** Chopped sea bream and vegetables hidden under rice, eaten with stock and wasabi, from Iwami (Hamada, Hikimi in Masuda, Tsuwano and elsewhere). For why it is hidden there are several theories:

- **Frugality**: to keep a luxury out of sight in the thrifty Edo period.
- **Modesty**: the fillings were plain and people were embarrassed to show them.

This page gives both. The ministry adds that a similar dish exists in Yamaguchi, and that it is also said to have been handed down with Buddhist faith. In 1939 (Shōwa 14) a national survey of local food by the Imperial Household chose it as one of Japan's "five great rice dishes". It is a winter supper and a New Year dish for guests; Tsuwano grows wasabi for it.

**Koi no itozukuri.** Lake Shinji carp cut into threads and coated with roasted carp roe, a winter dish of Matsue. The ministry says it was served to emperors and shoguns from the Kamakura to the Meiji period, and the method was learned from the Shijō school of cooking. It takes great skill, so it is celebration food at Matsue's *ryōtei* and restaurants, not home food.

**Loach kenchin-jiru.** From Yasugi, home of the *dojō-sukui* "loach-scooping" dance of the Yasugi-bushi song. The ministry cites an old record of Izumo's products that mentions loach, and puts loach production second in Japan.

**Sumoji.** A scattered sushi with flaked grilled mackerel, from Kisuki and Mitoya in Unnan. The ministry says Shimane's mackerel catch is among Japan's highest, and that grilling mackerel to keep and carry it is said to be where grilled mackerel began. It drew attention as a tourist draw from around the 1990s.

**Kujira-gohan.** Rice cooked with whale skin, from Hamada and Masuda in Iwami, eaten at *setsubun* so children grow up to be "big ones".

**Heka and nodoguro.** Nodoguro, the blackthroat seaperch, has no entry of its own. The ministry's Shimane story names it with tilefish and flounder among the seafood in *heka*, Iwami's sukiyaki-style dish cooked in a flat iron pan. Tsuwano's carp is not in the farm ministry's list.

## What makes it San'in

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Tottori 27, Shimane 28 — 55 in all. I counted both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages, and the counts matched. Among them:

- **Tottori**: *itadaki*, Daisen *okowa*, dondoroke-meshi, tōfu chikuwa, oyagani miso soup, ago chikuwa, hatahata sushi.
- **Shimane**: uzume-meshi, *bakudan* rice balls, sea bass *hōsho-yaki*, koi no itozukuri, sumoji, Izumo soba, heka.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them: Tottori 2, Shimane 7. Tottori's are itadaki (fiscal 2021, modern) and Daisen okowa (2021, traditional). Shimane's are all traditional: Takatsu river ayu dishes (2023), Izumo soba (2021, special award), Ōda *hako-zushi*, Shimizu yōkan, Tsuwano *imoni*, Higashi-Izumo dried persimmons and Matsue's tea-ceremony culture (all 2021). Tottori has 27 entries on one list and two on the other; I found no official explanation, and the two lists are chosen separately.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: matsuba-gani as a dish, beef-bone ramen, ago no yaki and Tsuwano carp. Nodoguro has no entry of its own.

## What shaped it

**Tottori.** The ministry's Tottori story says the prefecture faces the Japan Sea along 133 km of coast, with the Chūgoku mountains close behind and little flat land; rice grows along the rivers, vegetables on the dunes, and dairy cattle on the slopes of Daisen. Winter is crab season. It says Ikeda Mitsunaka urged frugality and "is said" to have ordered tofu eaten instead of fish, which spread a tofu culture. A prefectural official, quoted in the story, describes "liar's tofu" on 8 December, eating tofu to cancel out the year's lies.

**Shimane.** The ministry's Shimane story divides the prefecture into Izumo, Iwami and Oki, and says the differences between them are large. Izumo has Lake Shinji's seven delicacies — sea bass, icefish, carp, shijimi, eel, *moroge* shrimp and *amasagi* smelt. Iwami has ayu from the Gōnokawa river, coastal fishing and uzume-meshi in the hills. Oki has seaweed and bakudan rice balls.

**Together.** I found no official source that describes Tottori and Shimane as one food culture. Both have Japan Sea seasons in the record, Tottori's crab and Shimane's mackerel and heka, but I found no official source giving a crab season for all of San'in, and I did not read Shimane's crab season.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Oyagani miso soup**: at home, in school lunches, and at events in the fishing season.
- **Ago chikuwa**: local product halls, roadside stations and online shops.
- **Rakkyō**: as a souvenir of the dunes.
- **Koi no itozukuri**: ryōtei and restaurants in Matsue.
- **Loach dishes**: restaurants serving local food.
- **Sumoji**: restaurants, delis and grilled-mackerel shops in Unnan.

**Prices.** I found no official dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** Oyagani in November and December, matsuba-gani from November to March, rakkyō picked from late May to mid-June. Oyagani soup and shijimi soup are everyday food; koi no itozukuri is for winter celebrations, kujira-gohan for setsubun.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Misasa and crab, at one remove.** The farm ministry's list does not name Misasa (see the [Misasa guide](/en/soak/misasa/)). But the ministry's Tottori story lists Misasa Onsen Kagaribi no Yado among those who helped, and quotes its head chef saying that matsuba-gani is expensive, so local people often eat the cheaper oyagani, and that in miso soup the crab gives a good stock. That is the ministry's account; I found nothing on Misasa's own official pages to match it, so I do not call crab soup a Misasa dish.

**Tamatsukuri, without a link.** The ministry's pages for Izumo soba and shijimi-jiru do not mention Tamatsukuri. See [Tamatsukuri](/en/soak/tamatsukuri/).

**Kaike, from the town side only.** Yonago city's tourism association names rock oysters from the Japan Sea in summer and matsuba-gani in winter as [Kaike](/en/soak/kaike/)'s specialities. The farm ministry's Tottori list does not name Kaike, so I give this as the association's account, not as a Kaike dish on the ministry's list.

**Getting there.** Checked 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Matsue** (Matsue tourism association, no update date): Izumo Enmusubi airport is about 90 minutes from Haneda; the airport bus takes about 35 minutes to JR Matsue and about 30 minutes to Tamatsukuri Onsen. Yonago Kitarō airport is about 80 minutes from Haneda, then about 45 minutes by bus to JR Matsue. By rail, shinkansen to Okayama and the limited express *Yakumo*, about 6 hours.
- **Tottori** (Tottori tourism federation): Haneda to Tottori Sand Dunes Conan airport about 1 hour 15 minutes, five ANA flights a day; airport bus to Tottori station about 20 minutes. By rail from Tokyo about 4 hours 50 minutes by shinkansen and the *Super Hakuto*. The prefecture's airport page says "about 80 minutes" to Tokyo; I give both figures.

## San'in food away from San'in

- **Tottori.** The Tottori–Okayama Shinbashi-kan, nicknamed *Momotenashi*, shared with Okayama prefecture, at Shinbashi Center Place, 1-11-7 Shinbashi, Minato-ku. A shop on the ground floor; upstairs, a restaurant serving Western-style dishes with fish landed in Tottori and Okayama and Tottori wagyu, plus event, information and co-working space. It was set up publicly and is run by a private firm chosen by open call. The prefecture's page gives no hours or prices.
- **Shimane.** The former Nihonbashi Shimane-kan has closed. At the governor's press conference of 13 February in fiscal 2019, he said it closed on 31 January and that Hibiya Shimane-kan would open on 22 April; the prefecture's magazine says the shop moved after 16 years in Nihonbashi. [Hibiya Shimane-kan](https://www.shimanekan.jp/) is in the basement of Hibiya Chanter, 1-2-2 Yūrakuchō, Chiyoda-ku, open 11:00–20:00 and closed when Hibiya Chanter closes. Its site lists October 2026 tastings of *shime-saba* fish cake and Saijō persimmons.

**What changes.** At the source, oyagani follows a two-month season; the shops are open all year. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Matsuba-gani is a listed Tottori dish."** Matsuba-gani is not in the farm ministry's list as a dish; oyagani miso soup is.
- **"Matsuba-gani and oyagani are different crabs."** They are the male and female snow crab.
- **"San'in is one cuisine."** I found no official source that treats it as one, and Shimane alone splits three ways.
- **"Izumo soba has one date."** The sources give different years; see the Izumo soba page.
