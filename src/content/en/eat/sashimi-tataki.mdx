*Katsuo no tataki* is bonito seared on the outside, left nearly raw inside, sliced and dressed with citrus vinegar and soy — and in Kōchi it always comes with sliced garlic. The official sources agree on most of it and differ in places: on where it began, on what happens after the fish leaves the fire, and on the dressing, which changes across Kōchi itself. This page sets the accounts side by side.

For sashimi in general — the cuts, the garnishes and what does and does not make raw fish safe — start from the [sashimi overview](/en/eat/sashimi/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's dish page says tataki is held to have been the meal fishermen ate on board, which then spread to everyone. It adds that it is also said to have developed, in an age with no way of keeping fish, as a way to eat bonito that had lost its freshness at sea.

**The name.** The ministry says *tataki* is said to come from *tataku*, to slap: salt or sauce was patted into the fish so the flavour soaked in.

**Three accounts.** The ministry's Kōchi story goes further and gives three origins, one after another:

- **On board.** It was fishermen's food at sea.
- **Two lords.** Chōsokabe Motochika ate bonito half-grilled, and Yamauchi Kazutoyo later banned eating it raw.
- **For foreigners.** In the Meiji era it was served to Westerners in place of steak.

None is given as the answer, and this site does not choose.

## What makes it tataki

**The fish.** Kōchi prefecture says almost all its bonito is caught by pole and line, one fish lifted high at a time. Boats follow bonito migrating from south of Okinawa past Tosa, Bōsō and Sanriku from March to November. The 100-year food page says the fatty returning bonito of autumn is best for tataki, and that the first bonito of early summer is popular too.

**The fire.** Searing over straw is held to be best, the 100-year food page says, though at home most people use a gas flame.

**Two methods.** The prefecture and the farm ministry describe the steps differently, and both are given here.

- **Kōchi prefecture.** Fillet the fish, split each side at the dark meat into a back block and a belly block, sprinkle with salt and pat it in by hand. Sear over a straw or *kaya* grass fire, smoking it, so that only the surface is cooked and the inside stays nearly raw. Plunge it into cold water at once, drain, and serve with leek, ginger, shiso and garlic in *nihaizu*, a two-part vinegar.
- **Farm ministry.** At home, the block is seared quickly, not cooled in iced water, patted with salt and sliced while still warm. The whole surface, not just the skin, is seared over a strong fire of straw or similar. Too long and it becomes grilled fish; too short and it smells fishy.

So one source cools the fish in water and the other does not. This site does not choose.

**The dressing.** The farm ministry says it changes by region within Kōchi:

- **Central Kōchi** — nihaizu of soy and brewed vinegar.
- **The west** — mainly soy, mixed with mirin and similar.
- **The east** — soy cut with yuzu juice.
- **The mountains** — *sanbaizu*, a three-part vinegar.

**The garlic.** Sliced garlic is essential, the ministry says, and it explains this as the wisdom of earlier generations, who counted on garlic's sterilising effect.

## Not only bonito

The 100-year food page says some areas of Kōchi make tataki of moray eel, or of vegetables such as aubergine and pumpkin. Aubergine tataki is itself on Kōchi's 100-year food list, in the "future" category, from 2021.

## At the table

**Sawachi.** Tataki is a fixture of *sawachi*, Kōchi's large platters, 36 to 39 cm across, laid with sashimi, tataki, sushi, simmered and fried food. The farm ministry calls sawachi a style of serving rather than a dish. It is the food of *okyaku*, the Tosa word for a banquet with guests, and of big days — weddings, funerals, births, sixtieth birthdays. The ministry's tataki page adds that tataki is indispensable at weddings, funerals and shrine rites.

**Every day, too.** Kōchi prefecture says that on the 2010–2012 average, annual bonito consumption per household in Kōchi city was 5,163 g, the most in Japan. Bonito has been the prefectural fish since 1988.

**On two lists.** Katsuo no tataki is in the farm ministry's list of Kōchi's local dishes, and on the Agency for Cultural Affairs' 100-year food list (2021, traditional, with a special award). The two lists are chosen separately.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

The farm ministry says tataki is sold at izakaya, farm stalls and fishmongers. None of the sources for this page gives a tax-included price, so none is given here.

**Getting there.** Kochi Tabi Net, the Kōchi travel site, checked on 2026-10-12, gives about 1 hour 30 minutes from Haneda to Kōchi Ryōma airport, with ten flights a day. By rail, it gives about 3 hours 15 minutes from Tokyo to Okayama by Nozomi, then about 2 hours 35 minutes to Kōchi by the Nankaze limited express. It notes that services and times can change with the season, weather and traffic.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Tataki away from Kōchi

Kōchi prefecture opened an antenna shop, Marugoto Kōchi, in Ginza, Tokyo, on 21 August 2010. It has a food shop on the ground floor and a restaurant, TOSA DINING Okyaku, on the second. No source checked for this page compares tataki in Kōchi with tataki elsewhere, in taste, freshness or price, and this page makes no such comparison. What can be checked on any menu is the method the sources describe: seared outside, nearly raw inside, with garlic.

## What people get wrong

- **"It has one origin."** Kōchi's own story gives three.
- **"There is one correct method."** The prefecture plunges the seared fish into cold water; the ministry's home method does not.
- **"The dressing is always ponzu."** It changes across Kōchi: two-part vinegar, soy with mirin, soy with yuzu, three-part vinegar.
- **"Tataki is always bonito."** Some areas make it of moray eel or vegetables.
