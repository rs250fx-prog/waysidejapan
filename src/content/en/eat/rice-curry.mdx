Curry rice is everyday Japanese food with a foreign passport. It reached Japan from Britain, not India, in the Meiji era; the navy adopted it; food companies turned it into a block of roux any household could use; and a chain founded outside Nagoya turned it into a menu where you choose how much rice you want, by the 50 g. This page is about how that happened, what the different kinds of curry shop are, what a plate costs, and how to order.

For rice in general, start from the rice overview.

## Where it came from

The Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force says that curry, born in India, reached Japan by way of Britain at the end of the Edo period. Its timeline:

- **1860** — Fukuzawa Yukichi's *Zōtei Kaei Tsūgo* introduces the word "Curry" in Japan for the first time, glossed as *koruri*.
- **1863** — Miyake Hiizu becomes the first Japanese to meet curry, and writes in his diary that it is an extremely filthy thing.
- **1871** — Yamakawa Kenjirō, later president of Tokyo Imperial University, eats curry on the ship to America.
- **1872** — two cookbooks, *Seiyō Ryōri Tsū* and *Seiyō Ryōri Shinan*, give Japan's first curry recipes.
- **1908** — a navy cookery manual, *Kaigun Kappōjutsu Sankōsho*, introduces "curry rice" to the navy.

**The first recipes were not today's curry.** The National Diet Library's exhibition on Meiji curry notes that the 1872 *Seiyō Ryōri Shinan* recipe used leek, ginger, garlic, chicken, prawn, sea bream, oysters and red frog. Onion, carrot and potato, now the standard, were not yet widely grown. Other cookbooks of the period each had their own ingredients.

**Why it came with rice.** The Japan curry manufacturers' cooperative (an industry body) says curry went from India to Britain in the 17th century, and a British company, Crosse & Blackwell, began making curry powder commercially at the end of the 18th. In the early Meiji era its curry powder reached Japan from Britain with the recipes, joined up with rice and spread as *raisu karē*, "rice curry". Thickening the sauce with flour, it says, came with the British version. Carrot and onion arrived in curry after their cultivation spread, from about 1887 onwards.

**Restaurants, schools, and price.** House Foods' curry dictionary says that after Japan lifted its ban on eating meat in 1871, Western-style restaurants opened one after another and rice curry became a popular item. It says the army's cadet school made Saturday curry day in 1873, and that at Sapporo Agricultural College, opened in 1876, William Clark had students eat rice curry every other day. House Foods also says it cannot say who named "rice curry", or when. S&B Foods, another curry maker, writes that around 1877 Fūgetsudō in Ginza sold curry at 8 sen when a plate of plain soba cost 1 sen, and it was still popular; by the end of the Meiji era, curry rice, curry udon and curry soba were on the menus of cheap diners. These are companies' accounts; I have not seen the primary sources.

## What makes it Japanese curry

**The navy made it a habit.** The Maritime Self-Defense Force explains that the young Imperial Japanese Navy, built on British lines, was suffering from beriberi caused by chronic poor nutrition. The Royal Navy already served curry, and the Japanese navy valued it as nutritious, easy to cook in quantity and good to eat, and adopted the British style. Recipes appeared in a training regulation for cooks in 1889 and in the 1908 cookery manual, as part of reforming sailors' food.

On the Friday curry, the same page is careful. The common explanation — that it helps crews keep track of the day at sea — it calls an explanation that probably came after eating curry regularly at the weekend became a custom. After the war, it says, the five-day week moved the curry from Saturday to Friday. The page mixes a nutrition reason, a day-of-the-week reason and a working-week reason; this site does not pick one.

**The roux made it a home dish.** The industry cooperative says Japanese curry needs meat, potato, carrot and onion, while India centres on onion and tomato.

Who made the first Japanese curry powder depends on whose history you read:

- **House Foods** says domestic curry powder was first made and sold in 1905, without naming the maker.
- **S&B Foods** says its founder, Yamazaki Mineijirō, first succeeded in making curry powder in Japan in 1923, launched a household curry powder in 1930, and the red-tin curry powder in 1950.

**Solid roux** came in 1950, House Foods says, with block curry roux; as company after company followed, curry became something a family could make at home. The cooperative says many types of roux were developed in 1950–51 and curry spread widely into households from the Shōwa 30s (1955–64) onwards. House Foods launched its "Indo Curry" roux in 1960 and "Vermont Curry" in 1963, made with apple and honey so children could eat it alongside the hot adult curries of the time.

**Retort pouches** came in 1968 according to House Foods, or in the middle of the Shōwa 40s (1965–74) according to the cooperative, which calls it a Japanese invention. The two dates are a little apart; both are given.

**School lunches.** House Foods says curry was introduced to school lunches nationwide in 1948. The cooperative's Curry Day, 22 January, comes from 1982, when the national association of school nutritionists asked schools across Japan to put curry on the school-lunch menu, ahead of school-lunch week. 

**How much roux.** The cooperative's survey of its members counted 102,338 tonnes of curry roux in fiscal 2009 and 91,485 tonnes in fiscal 2016. I could not find later figures.

## The shops, and what they charge

Prices are tax included, as posted by the company on 10 October 2026.

**CoCo Ichibanya.** The first shop opened in January 1978 in Nishibiwajima, Nishikasugai district (now Kiyosu), on the edge of Nagoya. Its starting point was the curry rice at "Bacchus", the coffee shop the founding couple ran. The company was set up in July 1982, and since 1981 it has let employees open their own franchise shops. At the end of February 2026 it had 1,285 shops in Japan and 218 abroad.

- **Pork curry: ¥646**, with 300 g of rice, eating in or taking away; takeaway costs ¥54 more. Delivery prices are different.
- On the company's order simulator (Tokyo, Kanagawa and Osaka prices): vegetable curry ¥683, beef ¥794, hayashi rice ¥896. Other areas have a different base price.
- Some shops have different menus and prices, and orders between 22:00 and 05:00 carry a 7% late-night charge.

**Go Go Curry.** Founded in December 2003 and based in Kanazawa, Ishikawa. It defines Kanazawa curry as: thick sauce, shredded cabbage on the side, a stainless-steel plate, eaten with a fork or spoon, and a cutlet set on top of the rice and covered with sauce. It opened its first shop in 2004 in Shinjuku, Tokyo, and says it started a Kanazawa curry boom; it has about 120 shops in Japan and abroad. I could not confirm a current price, so none is given.

**Soba shops.** House Foods says that in the late Meiji and Taishō eras soba shops took up the fashionable Western food and created curry udon, *karē nanban* and curry rice bowls flavoured with dashi. I could not find official sources for individual curry shops.

## How to order it

At CoCo Ichibanya, the menu is built from choices, and the company publishes an English ordering guide that says prices include tax.

1. **Pick the curry** — pork, mild pork, vegetable, beef or hayashi.
2. **Pick the rice.** 300 g is standard. You can go down to 150 g (¥90 less) or up to 900 g or more. Between 150 and 400 g it goes in 50 g steps, above that in 100 g steps; 350 g is ¥65 more, 400 g ¥130 more, then ¥130 for each extra 100 g (for pork, mild pork and vegetable; beef and hayashi are priced separately). The sauce goes up or down with the rice.
3. **Pick the heat and the toppings.** Spice level and toppings are chosen the same way, and the company’s simulator shows the options.

## Not all of Japan's curry is this

Besides the Kanazawa plate and the soba-shop curries above, there is navy curry as a city project. The Maritime Self-Defense Force says Yokosuka's 1999 "Yokosuka navy curry", planned by the city and the local naval district, was the first, and projects built on Self-Defense Force curry spread to Kure, Sasebo, Ōminato, Maizuru and other bases.

## Curry away from Yokosuka

Yokosuka is where the navy-curry projects began, and the city sets rules for its curry.

- **What it is.** Yokosuka city says "Yokosuka navy curry" is a curry recreated today from the recipe in the navy's 1908 cookery manual, and that its taste is curry at its most basic. The city's tourism site lists curry powder, flour, beef or chicken, carrot, onion and potato, with the powder and flour fried into a roux and chutney served as a condiment, and says the aim is a nostalgic, retro curry.
- **The rules.** The basic rule is to serve it with salad and milk. In principle it is served only inside Yokosuka. The name is a registered trademark; to use it, a business joins the city's curry business group and has its product checked.
- **Away from the city.** Under the city's own rules, a plate called Yokosuka navy curry outside Yokosuka is the exception; other ports run their own projects.

What I could not find: any official comparison of curry in Yokosuka and in Tokyo by taste, price or size, or an official explanation of how "navy curry" and "Self-Defense Force curry" differ. Nothing official says the curry is better at its source.

## What people get wrong

- **"Japanese curry is Indian curry."** It came through Britain, with British curry powder and a flour-thickened sauce.
- **"The navy eats curry on Fridays so sailors know the day."** The Maritime Self-Defense Force itself treats that as an explanation that came after the habit, and says it was Saturday before the five-day week.
- **"There is one first maker of Japanese curry powder."** House Foods says 1905; S&B Foods says its founder succeeded in 1923.
