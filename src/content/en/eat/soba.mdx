Soba is the thin grey-brown noodle made from buckwheat, and it is the noodle of the other half of Japan: the east, the north, the mountains, the places where rice and wheat grew badly and buckwheat grew in seventy days on poor soil. Udon is the noodle of the warm west; soba is what Edo ate, what Nagano and Shimane and Iwate made into regional cuisines, and what most of the country still eats on the last night of the year. It is also the Japanese noodle with the most ritual attached to eating it, and the one where the raw material is now mostly imported.

## What it is

Buckwheat is not wheat; it is a seed from a plant related to rhubarb, and it has no gluten. The ministry's own account is that it has been grown in Japan since the Jōmon period, probably from China, and was eaten for most of that time as *sobagaki* — a porridge or dumpling of buckwheat flour and hot water. The noodle, *soba-kiri*, "cut soba", is comparatively recent. The oldest record is a document from Jōshōji temple in Ōkuwa, Kiso, in Nagano, noting that soba-kiri was served to the craftsmen who repaired the main hall in 1574; the prefecture calls Kiso the birthplace of the noodle, with the usual caveat about other theories. The noodle reached Edo and became a city food in the eighteenth century.

Because buckwheat has no gluten, pure buckwheat noodles are hard to make and break easily, so most soba contains wheat. The names tell you the ratio: *jūwari* is 100% buckwheat, *ni-hachi* is 80:20, *kuichi* 90:10. The national noodle association's explanation of *ni-hachi* is simply the ratio; a food-industry paper notes the folk etymology — two times eight is sixteen *mon*, the Edo price of a bowl — and rejects it. The flour itself is graded by how deep into the grain the mill goes: the first milling from the kernel's centre is the white *sarashina* flour, the second takes the germ, the third the inner husk, and grinding the whole grain with its husk gives the dark *hikigurumi* of Izumo.

## Why it is the way it is

**The cold plate and the dark sauce.** Edo ate soba cold, on a bamboo tray, with a concentrated dipping sauce of dark soy sauce and bonito called *kaeshi*. The national association's instruction for eating it is still the city's: dip only half the noodle, so that you taste the buckwheat and not the sauce, and slurp, drawing air through the noodle. At the end the server brings *soba-yu*, the cooking water, to thin the leftover sauce into a drink. The difference between *mori* and *zaru*, the two cold plates on every menu, used to be a better sauce on the zaru; the association's own officer says it is now mostly the sheet of nori on top.

**Three houses.** Edo's shops sorted themselves into lineages that still name the city's restaurants. *Sarashina* makes the white noodle from first-milled flour, a name its founding house traces to the Shinshū soba trade and a lord's permission to use a character from his name, in 1789. *Yabu* is the dark, firm noodle with a very salty sauce, from a house founded in 1880. *Sunaba* is the oldest name — from the sand yard of Hideyoshi's Osaka castle, where a soba shop prospered among the builders — and its Tokyo house, from 1869, says it invented the tempura-and-cold-soba plate.

**Everywhere else kept its own idea.** Izumo pours the sauce over the noodle instead of dipping; Togakushi stacks it in little bundles on a round tray as a shrine offering; Iwate feeds it to you a mouthful at a time until you shut the lid. The table below is where those differences live.

**The grain comes from abroad.** Japan harvests about 38,000 tonnes of buckwheat a year and imports about 88,000 — three-quarters of it from China — so the domestic share is about 30%. Hokkaido grows by far the most (16,800 t), then Tochigi, Ibaraki, Nagano and Fukui. "Shinshū soba" is a style and a brand; it is not a guarantee that the buckwheat grew in Nagano.

## What it costs

The spread is the widest of any noodle. A standing counter in a Tokyo station charges ¥390–450 for a hot bowl and expects you gone in five minutes. The statistical plate in central Tokyo is ¥788. The old Edo houses charge ¥1,000 (Sarashina Horii's mori) to ¥1,320 (Kanda Yabu's seiro) for the plain cold plate and ¥2,310–2,860 for one with tempura. In the household survey, the city that spends most on soba and udon eaten out is Takamatsu — for udon — and the soba cities are further down: Nagano 13th (¥8,705), Tokyo 22nd (¥8,166), Matsue 32nd, Morioka 38th.

## Where to eat it

- **Edo** — Tokyo's old houses in Azabu-Jūban (Sarashina Horii), Kanda (Yabu) and Nihonbashi (Sunaba); standing counters at every station.
- **Izumo** — the approach to Izumo Grand Shrine and Matsue; airport bus about 35 minutes to the shrine.
- **Togakushi** — the limited-express bus from Nagano station, 56 minutes to Chūsha, ¥2,500.
- **Wanko** — Morioka and Hanamaki, on the Tōhoku Shinkansen.

## What people get wrong

- **"Soba is gluten-free."** Only *jūwari*. Most is 80:20 with wheat, and the standing counters use more.
- **"Dunk the whole noodle."** Half. The association that represents the shops says so.
- **"Ni-hachi means it cost sixteen mon."** It means 80:20. The price story is folklore.
- **"Shinshū soba is grown in Nagano."** Nagano grows about 2,500 tonnes a year and Japan imports 88,000. The name is a style.
- **"Soba is a Tokyo food."** Tokyo made it a cuisine. The oldest record is in Nagano, and Izumo, Togakushi and Iwate each eat it in ways Tokyo never did.
