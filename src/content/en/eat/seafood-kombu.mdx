*Kombu*, kelp, is grown in the cold seas of the north and dried, and it is the base of much of Japan's stock. Its story is a story of shipping: the kelp comes almost entirely from Hokkaido, but the places that built a cuisine on it — Osaka, the Hokuriku coast, Okinawa — are the places the ships reached. This page covers where kombu grows, its main kinds, how to draw stock from it, the route it travelled, and the dishes it left behind. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi, start from the [seafood overview](/en/eat/seafood/); for stock itself, the [dashi overview](/en/eat/dashi/).

## Where it came from

**The name.** The Japan Kombu Association, an industry body, says the origin of the word is not clear. It gives one theory: that the Ainu called it *kompu*, the word travelled to China, and came back to Japan from there. A story that people from China ate kombu in the late Jōmon period has, it says, no reliable record behind it.

**The first records.** The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods says kombu appears in the *Shoku Nihongi* (797). The association says that from the middle of the Kamakura period trading ships went back and forth between Matsumae in Hokkaido and Honshu.

**The route south.** The ministry says that in the Muromachi period kombu went from Hokkaido through Tsuruga to Kyoto and Osaka, and from the Edo period by *kitamae* ship round the west — through Shimonoseki and the Seto Inland Sea. The association calls this the "kombu road", citing a 1997 junior-high-school textbook supplement and material from Hokkaido's fisheries federation. The National Diet Library adds that the Edo merchant Kawamura Zuiken established the western route in the late 17th century, and that the kombu road, built up from the mid-Edo period to the end of the shogunate, reached Edo, Kyushu and the Ryūkyūs.

## What makes it kombu

**Where it grows.** The association says over 95% of the kombu harvested in Japan comes from Hokkaido, and the rest from the Sanriku coast of Aomori, Iwate and Miyagi. Hakodate city says Hokkaido produces over 90% of the country's kombu, and the farm ministry puts it at 90%. The Umami Information Center says it is harvested mainly from July to September.

**The main kinds.** The association and the Umami Information Center describe them a little differently, so both are given:

- ***Ma-kombu*** — from the Hakodate coast; a clear stock (association). Used mainly in Kansai, around Osaka (Umami Information Center).
- ***Rishiri*** — used in *kaiseki* cooking (association); a clear stock that suits Kyoto's kaiseki (Umami Information Center).
- ***Rausu*** — a rich stock (association); thin and soft, used in many Japanese dishes (Umami Information Center).
- ***Hidaka*** — soft and quick to cook, for *kombu-maki* and oden (association).
- ***Naga-kombu*** — from Kushiro and Nemuro, the largest output of any kind (association).

The farm ministry's page on Osaka says that even within Kansai, Osaka prefers the rich ma-kombu and Kyoto the lighter Rishiri.

**Drawing stock.** The association's method: 10–20 g of kombu to a litre of water. Do not wash it; wipe it lightly with a well-wrung cloth, because the white powder on the surface is mannitol, a flavour component, and washing removes it. Soak for about 30 minutes, heat over a medium flame, and take the kombu out just before the water boils — boiled too long, its sticky components dissolve and spoil the flavour. Another way is to hold the water at about 60°C on a low flame for about 40 minutes; the association does not say why 60°C. Why kombu pairs with bonito is on the [dashi overview](/en/eat/dashi/).

## Along the kombu road

The ships left dishes behind wherever they called.

- **Osaka.** The association says Osaka simmers kombu in soy into *tsukudani*. The ministry suggests Osaka's taste for kombu stock came from kombu arriving by the western route and from soft water that draws it out well — both given as possible factors, not settled ones. The two explanations are set out on the [east–west dashi page](/en/eat/dashi-east-west/).
- **Toyama, *kobujime*.** Fish cured between sheets of kombu. The ministry says it was born from Hokkaido kombu carried by *kitamae* ships and fish from Toyama Bay; the kombu draws off excess water and helps the fish keep, which mattered before refrigerators. Most of the kombu Toyama cooks with is Rausu kombu, and one reason given is that many Toyama people who moved to Hokkaido as settlers in the Meiji era lived around Rausu and sent kombu home to relatives. The usual fish is swordfish, called *sasu* locally.
- **Ishikawa, *nishin no kobumaki*.** The ministry says the goods *kitamae* ships brought to the Noto ports were above all herring and kombu. Dried herring is rolled in kombu and simmered sweet and salty; it is a dish for New Year and celebrations and is still part of *osechi*.
- **Hokkaido, *Matsumae-zuke*.** Dried squid and kombu cut into strips and marinated in soy, a preserved food said to have begun in the Matsumae domain. Herring roe went into it from the late Edo period into the Meiji era; as the herring catch fell, squid and kombu became the core.
- **Kōchi, *kobu-zushi*.** Sushi wrapped in kombu: white board kombu in the centre of the prefecture, black kombu in the west. The ministry thinks the split arose because white board kombu was expensive and hard to get.
- **Okinawa.** The association says kombu is stir-fried or stewed there with pork and vegetables.

## Kombu away from home

Kombu is the clearest case of a food eaten most distinctively far from where it grows. The association says Kantō, which the kombu road reached late, is a region that eats comparatively little kombu by national standards.

The government household survey (2023–25 average) tells a more mixed story about what homes buy. The national figure for kombu is 727 (the file gives no unit; presumably yen per household a year). The top cities are Toyama 1,587, Fukui 1,285, Aomori 1,174, Yamagata 1,112, Sakai 1,044 and Kyoto 1,034 — the old *kitamae* coast, Tōhoku and the Kansai cities. Central Tokyo, at 765, spends almost exactly what Osaka does, at 761. These are home purchases, not restaurant cooking, and the survey gives no reasons.

No tax-included price for kombu was confirmed, so none is given.

## What people get wrong

- **"Osaka kombu comes from Osaka."** It comes from Hokkaido; Osaka is where the ships arrived.
- **"Wash kombu before using it."** The association says to wipe it, not wash it, so the mannitol stays on.
- **"Boil it for a strong stock."** The association says to take it out just before the boil.
- **"Tokyo buys no kombu."** The association describes Kantō as a low-kombu region, but in the household survey central Tokyo spends about the same as Osaka.
