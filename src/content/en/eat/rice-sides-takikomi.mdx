*Takikomi gohan* is rice cooked together with other things — vegetables, fish, chestnuts — so that the rice is the dish. *Kamameshi*, "pot rice", is the name best known to travellers from station lunches. The farm ministry records local versions of cooked-in rice prefecture by prefecture.

This page covers the versions the ministry describes in detail, and what can and cannot be said about kamameshi. For rice and the things around it in general, start from the rice and sides overview.

## Where it came from

The one account of a beginning I could find comes from Nara. The ministry’s entry on Nara’s *iro-gohan* says that in the Nara period there was *awa-gohan*, rice bulked out with millet, and that when rice was precious, cooking it with various ingredients to save rice is said to be how cooked-in rice began. That is one prefecture’s account, introduced with "it is said".

Kamameshi has no confirmed origin that I could use. Several stories exist, one placing it in Asakusa, but I found them only in search summaries and restaurant introductions, not in a public or primary source, so none is given here.

What I can confirm is the history of the best-known kamameshi maker. Oginoya, in Yokokawa, Gunma, says it was founded on 15 October 1885, the day Yokokawa station opened, with permission from the railway authority to sell on the premises. Its first station lunch was salt onigiri and *takuan* pickle wrapped in bamboo sheath. The founding couple had run an inn of the same name at Kirizumi hot spring. The page I opened did not give the year its kamameshi was launched or why a ceramic pot was chosen, so I have left both out here; the station-lunch box itself is covered with food shopping, under ekiben.

## What makes it takikomi gohan

**Tai-meshi, Ehime: two dishes, one name.** The ministry says *tai-meshi* (sea bream rice) is handed down across the whole prefecture, in two different forms:

- **Tōyo (Imabari and around).** A whole sea bream is cooked with the rice; when it is done, the flesh is broken up and mixed in. The ministry notes that fish raised in the Kurushima Strait off Imabari are said to be firm and springy. In Chūyo, the cooked-in type typified by the Hōjō district of Matsuyama was the mainstream.
- **Nanyo (Uwajima, Seiyo and around).** Slices of raw sea bream are soaked in a sauce of soy sauce, mirin, egg, sesame and stock, and poured, sauce and all, over hot rice. This one is said to have begun as something fishermen could make easily on boats where they could not use fire. At first horse mackerel was often used; the version with the festive sea bream came to be called *tai-meshi*.

It is eaten all year, the ministry says, and often at celebrations because sea bream is considered a lucky fish. It is also sold as a station lunch and as ready-mix packs.

**Chestnut rice, Tanba (Kyoto).** Lightly salted rice cooked with Tanba chestnuts, which the ministry calls an essential of autumn, with the salt bringing out the chestnuts’ sweetness. The name "Tanba chestnut" appears in the *Engishiki*, and chestnut rice was eaten among the nobility from around the Heian period. Because it uses raw chestnuts it is limited to autumn, and the chestnuts are usually cooked soon after they are bought because they spoil easily.

**Iro-gohan, Nara.** Nara calls soy-seasoned cooked-in rice *iro-gohan*, "coloured rice". The usual ingredients are carrot, shiitake, burdock and thin fried tofu, with seasonal additions such as peas or bamboo shoots. The ministry gives no particular season. It was served at village events, is eaten at home as everyday food, and today also appears in supermarket and convenience-store boxed lunches.

## How to eat it

The ministry’s pages describe how these are made at home rather than how to order them, and I have no prices I could confirm. Two practical points come from the pages themselves:

- **In Ehime, ask which tai-meshi.** The Tōyo and Nanyo versions are different dishes under the same name.
- **Chestnut rice is seasonal.** The Tanba version is an autumn dish.

*Kayaku gohan* is another name for cooked-in rice, but I could not open the ministry’s page on it, so it is not described here.

## Not all of Japan’s cooked-in rice is this

These three are only the entries I could read in full. The ministry’s database covers many more by prefecture, and I have not described any I did not open.

## Takikomi gohan away from home

I could not find a source that compares any of these dishes where they come from with the same dish elsewhere. The clearest difference the sources show is inside one prefecture: Ehime’s *tai-meshi* is cooked-in in Tōyo and poured-over in Nanyo.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tai-meshi is one dish."** In Ehime it is two.
- **"Kamameshi was invented in a particular place."** Several stories exist; I could not confirm any.
- **"Oginoya started with kamameshi."** Its first station lunch, by its own account, was salt onigiri and *takuan*.
- **"Cooked-in rice is festive food."** Some is, like *tai-meshi* at celebrations; Nara’s *iro-gohan* is everyday food as well as village-event food.
