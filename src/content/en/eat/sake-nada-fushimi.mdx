Nada, on the coast between Kobe and Nishinomiya, and Fushimi, in the south of Kyoto, are two of the historic sake districts of the Kansai region, and both owe their position to the same thing: water. They are on one page here because the comparison is the point. The official documents describe them in almost opposite terms — one hard-watered, cold-winded and built for shipping, the other soft-watered and built, in the tax agency's phrase, to sit beside food.

For what sake is and how to read a label, start from the sake overview.

## Where it came from

**Nada.** *Nada Gogō*, "the five villages of Nada", is the collective name for Nishi-gō, Mikage-gō, Uozaki-gō, Nishinomiya-gō and Imazu-gō. Under its geographical indication the area is Nada and Higashinada wards of Kobe, Ashiya, and Nishinomiya. The tax agency's GI document explains why brewing gathered here. In winter the westerly seasonal wind comes down off the Rokkō mountains as the *Rokkō oroshi*, ideal for brewing in the cold. The steep slopes gathered nine fast streams into ten kilometres from east to west, which in the old days drove water-wheel rice mills and produced large amounts of well-polished rice. And the sea was close, so the casks could be loaded straight onto ships.

**Miyamizu.** The Nada brewers' association says the water was discovered at the end of the Edo period by Yamamura Tazaemon of the Sakura Masamune brewery, who brewed in both Nishinomiya and Uozaki, noticed that the sake differed by place, and traced the cause to the water. The Umenoki well in Kubo-chō, Nishinomiya is where he is *said* to have found it — that is the association's own wording. I have not been able to confirm the year that is often quoted, so I do not give one.

**Fushimi.** The Fushimi brewers' association writes that sake-making here is *said* to go back to the Yayoi period, when rice farming reached Japan — a claim I could not trace further. On firmer ground, it says brewing flourished in the Azuchi–Momoyama period, when Toyotomi Hideyoshi built Fushimi castle and demand rose; that in the Edo period Fushimi was the gateway on the Yodo river route between Kyoto and Osaka, and the number of brewers grew; and that in the late Meiji era it became known nationally as one of the great sake towns. Kyoto's Fushimi ward office calls sake the area's representative industry, built on its groundwater, with production among the highest in the country.

## What makes it Nada and Fushimi sake

**The water.**

- **Nada — hard.** In the GI document Miyamizu is groundwater that rises through the strata carrying moderate amounts of chlorine, potassium and other minerals the yeast needs, and almost no iron, which colours sake. The brewers' association adds phosphorus and calcium to the list, and says the minerals feed the kōji mould and yeast and help the enzymes work. Nishinomiya city describes it as flowing two to five metres underground, south-east of Nishinomiya shrine, where three underground streams meet. The city has a conservation ordinance setting procedures for development that might affect it.
- **Fushimi — moderately hard.** The name was once written 伏水, "hidden water", and the Fushimi brewers' association describes spring water that passes under the Momoyama hills and rises near their foot, with a balance of potassium and calcium. The spring at Gokōnomiya shrine is one of Japan's 100 selected waters; the shrine's tradition is that a fragrant spring rose in its grounds over a thousand years ago. Fushimi has many water legends: Kin-meisui, Gin-meisui, Shiragikusui.
- **Kyoto as a whole — soft to moderately hard**, in the GI document.

**The rice.** The Nada breweries have long used Yamada Nishiki, and grow it nearby. Hyōgo prefecture says Yamada Nishiki was bred in Hyōgo from the varieties Yamadaho and Tankan Wataribune, that Hyōgo grows about 60% of Japan's crop, and that in 2023 it covered 4,610 ha of the prefecture, mainly in northern Harima and parts of Hanshin. The brewers' association calls it "the king of sake rice" for its large grains and large white starchy core, especially for daiginjō. On *when* it was created, the sources disagree: the brewers' association says 1923, at the prefectural experiment station; the tax agency's nihonshu GI document says it was bred in 1936. Neither explains the gap.

**The people.** Nada's brewery workers came largely from the Tanba region, and the Tanba *tōji* — with the Nanbu and Echigo, one of Japan's three great brewer guilds — developed methods suited to hard water, which the GI document credits for Nada's character.

**The taste, officially.**

- **Nada Gogō**: well-balanced, with a clean finish; sake that has been stored improves in autumn (*aki-agari*), becoming rounder and more settled.
- **Kyoto**: soft and round in the mouth, with moderate savouriness and sweetness and a refined aroma — "sake that does not get in the way of food, and brings each other out". The GI document links this to Kyoto cooking and its dashi.

The common shorthand that Nada makes "masculine" sake and Fushimi "feminine" does not appear in any of the official or industry sources I used, so it is not used here.

## Why here

The official texts give the same three reasons for both towns: water, a winter suited to brewing, and a way to move the sake. Nada had hard water, the Rokkō wind, water mills and a harbour. Fushimi had abundant spring water and the river between the capital and Osaka. Hyōgo is, by the Nada association's account, Japan's largest sake-producing prefecture, and Nada its largest district.

## The protected names

- **Nada Gogō** — GI since 28 June 2018. Japanese rice of inspection grade 3 or better only; water drawn within Nada Gogō only; brewed, stored and bottled there; added alcohol no more than 25% of the rice weight. Managed by the Nada Gogō brewers' cooperative.
- **Kyoto** — GI since 1 October 2025, covering the whole prefecture. Water from within the prefecture; brewed, stored and bottled there; added alcohol no more than 50% of the rice weight. Managed by the Kyoto prefectural brewers' cooperative, based in Fushimi.

There is no separate "Fushimi" GI. A bottle that says Fushimi is telling you where the brewery is; the legal guarantee is "Kyoto".

## Nada and Fushimi sake away from home

**The bottle is the same bottle.** A sake labelled Nada Gogō or Kyoto under the GI must be brewed, stored and bottled in the region, with local water. On a Tokyo menu it is the same sake.

**The season is not always on the menu.** Nada's *hiyaoroshi* — sake that has matured through summer and is shipped without a second heating once it has cooled to the outside temperature — has traditionally gone out from September. You meet it in autumn or not at all.

**What only the town offers is the comparison.** Gekkeikan pours three of one brewery's seasonal sakes side by side; Nada's breweries are close enough to compare a hard-water district on foot. In Tokyo, the brewers' association's information center in Toranomon pours sake from all over Japan from ¥100 a glass — a different exercise, across regions rather than within one.

## What people get wrong

- **"Fushimi is a GI."** It is part of the Kyoto GI.
- **"Miyamizu was discovered in 1840."** The year is widely repeated; the brewers' association's own page gives the discoverer and the well, not the year, and calls the well the place he is *said* to have found it.
- **"Hard water makes better sake."** It makes a different sake. The official descriptions of Nada and Kyoto differ, and neither claims to be better.
- **"Yamada Nishiki dates from 1936."** Or 1923. The sources disagree.
- **"Nada is men's sake, Fushimi is women's."** Not in the official or industry texts.
