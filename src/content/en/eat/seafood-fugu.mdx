Fugu, the pufferfish, is the one fish in Japan where the rules matter more than the recipe. Its toxin cannot be cooked out, so the system is built around who is allowed to cut it and which parts may never be served. In Shimonoseki, the city most associated with it, it is not even called fugu but *fuku*. This page covers the toxin, the licence, the banned parts, and why Shimonoseki is called the home ground. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Where it came from

**A ban, and its lifting.** The farm ministry tells the story this way. During Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea, soldiers died after eating fugu, and that led to an order banning it. Itō Hirobumi was impressed by its flavour, and in 1888 (Meiji 21) fugu was legalised in Yamaguchi prefecture alone. The first officially recognised fugu restaurant was Shunpanrō. The ministry adds that ordinary people were said to eat fugu even before the ban was lifted, sometimes in miso soup.

**The name fuku.** In the Shimonoseki area, the farm ministry says, fugu is called *fuku* for luck, after the word for good fortune, and its sashimi is *fuku-sashi*. Shimonoseki city lists several theories for the origin of the word: a Korean origin, an entry in the Heian-period dictionary *Wamyō Ruijushō*, and the fish's swelling shape or its resemblance to other things. This site does not choose. The city says Shimonoseki calls it fuku because the word leads to happiness.

## What makes fugu different

**The toxin.** The health ministry's risk profile says fugu toxin is tetrodotoxin. It binds to the voltage-gated sodium channels of nerve and skeletal muscle membranes, blocks the inflow of sodium ions, and so stops nerve signals. The lethal dose for a person is estimated at 1–2 mg of tetrodotoxin.

**Where it is.** The toxin is concentrated in the guts, especially the liver and ovaries. How toxic a fish is varies greatly by species and by part. The tiger puffer genus has many hybrids, and the ministry says fugu of unknown species, or hybrids that cannot be identified, must be removed without fail. Shimonoseki city adds that the toxin is thought to come through the food chain, and that its strength varies with species, habitat and season; the health ministry's risk profile does not mention the food chain, so that point is the city's.

**What it does.** Symptoms appear about 20 minutes to 3 hours after eating, in four stages: numbness of the lips and tongue tip; paralysis of movement and difficulty breathing; paralysis of the whole body; loss of consciousness and breathing stopping. There is no effective antidote, but keeping the person breathing artificially can keep them alive.

**How often.** In recent years there have been about 10 cases a year with about 10 patients, sometimes with deaths. Anglers and amateur home cooking are often the cause.

**What does not help.** The health ministry says the toxin is not destroyed or weakened by rubbing with salt, soaking in water, or heating.

## The rules

**Who may prepare it.** Fugu that reaches consumers in Japan must be prepared by a specialist handler approved by the prefectural governor or equivalent. Requirements for handlers and premises are set by each prefecture's ordinances; the health ministry refers questions to the local public health centre.

**Which fish and which parts.** A 1983 notice from the health ministry, "On ensuring the hygiene of fugu", sets the species, parts and sea areas that may be eaten. Selling or serving anything outside that list is prohibited under the Food Sanitation Act. Penalties are up to three years' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥3 million, and up to ¥100 million for a company.

- **Liver** — may not be sold or served, whatever the species, wild or farmed.
- **Ovaries** — prohibited, except salted ovary and skin products specified in the notice.
- **Species not on the list** — every part is prohibited, including the flesh, skin and milt.

**Farmed liver.** The Food Safety Commission, in reports of 2005 and 2017, said it could not confirm for now the safety of liver from tiger puffer farmed by the proposed method. As of 2017, the liver had not been approved as food.

**Not at home.** The health ministry asks people not to prepare fugu they caught themselves or were given.

**The licence differs by prefecture.** The farm ministry says the name of the qualification, the exam and what a holder may prepare all differ by prefecture, and that it is not a national qualification. Even the name varies: the farm ministry writes *fugu chōrishi* (fugu cook), Yamaguchi prefecture *fugu shorishi* (fugu handler). In Yamaguchi, a licence is issued to people who passed its exam, and to people recognised by another prefecture whom Yamaguchi also accepts as equivalent; someone with another prefecture's licence still has to apply to handle fugu in Yamaguchi. The application fee listed by the prefecture is ¥2,890 (checked 2026-10-10). The prefecture may refuse a licence to someone unable to do the work properly because of a mental or physical disability, to drug addicts, or to people punished for breaking the ordinance. I could not confirm the exam rules of other prefectures, such as Tokyo, or the content of the national criteria for approving handlers.

## Why Shimonoseki

Shimonoseki city says it handles more fuku than anywhere in Japan and has a wholesale market specialising in it. It gives four reasons it is called the home ground:

- it faces good fishing grounds;
- skill in removing the poisonous parts has built up there;
- fuku restaurants multiplied after the 1888 legalisation in Yamaguchi;
- in 1974, Haedomari market opened — the only producing-area market in Japan centred on fuku.

The farm ministry adds that wild and farmed fugu are gathered at Shimonoseki from across Japan, and processors and restaurants are concentrated there.

**Shimonoseki fuku, a protected name.** "Shimonoseki fuku" was registered as a geographical indication on 12 October 2016 (No. 19), the first fishery product to be registered. The production area is Shimonoseki in Yamaguchi and Moji ward in Kitakyūshū, Fukuoka; the registered group is the Shimonoseki Karato fish market's intermediate wholesalers' cooperative. It is tiger puffer kept alive for a while to firm the flesh (*ikashikomi*), with the poison removed by skilled processing (*migaki*) that keeps it fresh. Intermediate wholesalers judge fish gathered from all over Japan before shipping them.

## How to eat it

The farm ministry describes *fuku-sashi*:

- **Sliced thin.** The knife goes in at a slight angle to draw thin slices. The ministry does not explain why it is cut so thin, so I do not either.
- **With ponzu.** Citrus juice mixed with soy, with *asatsuki* and *momiji-oroshi* as condiments.
- **Arranged.** In a chrysanthemum (*kiku-mori*) or a crane (*tsuru-mori*). Fishmongers and supermarkets sell it ready arranged in the chrysanthemum style.
- **When.** The fishing season is September to March; it is a winter food. It is an expensive fish, eaten mostly on celebrations and special days.

I could not verify a tax-included price for fugu at any shop.

## Not all of Japan's fugu is Shimonoseki's

The sources describe fugu in Shimonoseki in detail and say little about other regions' styles. What they do establish is that fugu everywhere in Japan is prepared by a prefecturally licensed handler, and that the licence itself differs from prefecture to prefecture.

## Fugu away from Shimonoseki

I could not find an official source comparing fugu eaten in Shimonoseki with fugu eaten in Tokyo, so this page does not compare them. The common ground is the rule, not the place: wherever you eat it, a licensed handler has prepared it, and the banned parts are banned everywhere. What changes is the licence, whose name and content depend on the prefecture.

## What people get wrong

- **"Cooking destroys the poison."** It does not, nor do salting or soaking.
- **"Fugu chefs hold a national licence."** The qualification is prefectural and differs by prefecture.
- **"Farmed fugu liver is safe, so it can be served."** It has not been approved; serving liver is prohibited.
- **"Fuku is a typo for fugu."** It is Shimonoseki's own name for the fish, tied to the word for good fortune.
