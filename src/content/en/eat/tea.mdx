Japanese green tea looks like one drink and is really several, made from the same plant and separated by two decisions: whether the bushes are shaded before picking, and what is done to the leaf afterwards. This page is the map — what the main kinds are, how the price of the leaf is set, why the bottle overtook the teapot in Japanese homes, and what a cup or a matcha sweet costs in Uji. The lineage pages go deeper: one on the producing regions, one on shaved ice and the old-style coffee house.

## What it is

The farm ministry's 2017 feature on tea and its 2026 report on the tea industry describe the main kinds this way.

- **Sencha** — the green tea most commonly drunk in Japan. The new shoots are steamed, rolled and dried, giving thin, needle-like leaves. Kyoto prefecture describes sencha and hōjicha as grown in open sunlight, which gives a fresh taste with some astringency.
- **Fukamushi sencha** (deep-steamed sencha) — the fresh leaf is steamed two to three times longer than for ordinary sencha. The leaf ends up finer, and the ministry describes the brewed tea as deep green, less astringent and richly sweet.
- **Gyokuro** — shaded for about 20 days before picking with straw or cloth screens, then steamed, rolled and dried like sencha. The ministry notes a seaweed-like aroma it calls *ōika*, "the scent of covering".
- **Kabusecha** — the same idea with about seven days of shade.
- **Tencha and matcha** — tencha is grown in fields covered for two to three weeks before picking, steamed, and dried without rolling. Matcha is tencha ground to a fine powder in a stone mill or similar.
- **Tamaryokucha** — steamed and rolled like sencha, but without the final shaping step, so the leaf stays curled.
- **Bancha** — made from leaves that have grown tough, or from summer and later pickings. Shizuoka prefecture says it is made like sencha and is cheap.
- **Hōjicha** — sencha or bancha roasted over high heat. The ministry describes it as fragrant and lower in caffeine.
- **Genmaicha** — bancha or sencha mixed with roasted rice, in Shizuoka's description.

Gyokuro, kabusecha and tencha are grouped together as *ōi-cha*, "covered tea". The sources agree closely on how long gyokuro is shaded: about 20 days in the ministry's report, 20 days or more on the ministry's Uji page, and from about 20 days before picking in Fukuoka prefecture's account of Yame.

**How much of each?** The ministry's report gives production shares for 2025, from the national federation of tea producers: sencha 48.1%, tencha 8.6%, kabusecha 2.0%, tamaryokucha 2.0%, gyokuro 1.0%. Shizuoka prefecture, on its own page, says sencha and deep-steamed sencha make up about 85% of tea in circulation. The two figures use different categories and different years, so I give both rather than choose.

## Why it is the way it is

**Shade changes the leaf.** Kyoto prefecture's explanation is that covering the field cuts out the sunlight, which holds back the change into catechins (the astringent component) and leaves more theanine, the component behind the savoury taste. That is the reason given for the savoury, less astringent character of the shaded teas.

**The price is set before the tea reaches a shop.** The ministry describes the chain: the fresh leaf is processed near the field into *aracha*, an unfinished crude tea, and the crude tea is finished and blended in the consuming areas before it is sold. The crude-tea stage was a ¥76.3 billion industry in 2024. The ministry says the biggest price gaps come from the kind of tea and from the picking season, with quality differences on top. Average crude-tea prices for the whole 2025 season, per kilogram:

| Kind | Crude tea, ¥ per kg (2025, all seasons) |
|---|---|
| Tencha | 8,562 |
| Gyokuro | 3,699 |
| Kabusecha | 2,387 |
| Tamaryokucha | 2,094 |
| Bancha | 1,812 |
| Sencha | 1,746 |

These are wholesale prices of unfinished leaf, not shop prices. By the ministry's own reckoning tencha fetches about 4.9 times the price of sencha. The first picking of the year is worth more: in 2025 first-flush tencha averaged ¥10,030 per kg and first-flush sencha ¥2,278, against ¥882 for first-flush bancha. The ministry adds that first-flush tea in 2026 traded at about twice the previous year's price in Kagoshima and about 70% higher in Shizuoka.

**The bottle changed what was worth growing.** Shizuoka prefecture's 2020 plan for its tea research centre lays out the shift. Demand for high-quality tea fell inside Japan, while demand grew for relatively cheap tea used as raw material for bottled drinks. The prefecture says first-flush crude tea fell from ¥3,137 per kg in 2000 to ¥1,946 in 2018, and that expensive tea was left unsold — a mismatch it names as one cause of falling farm incomes. It also says demand for high-quality tea brewed in a *kyūsu*, the Japanese teapot, has fallen quickly as habits changed. How many households still own or use a teapot, I could not find in an official source.

The household numbers in the ministry's report, from the government's household survey, show the same thing from the buyer's side:

- Leaf tea bought per household fell from 937 g in 2009 to 593 g in 2025, about 37% less.
- Spending per household on tea went from ¥10,480 a year in 2009 (¥4,780 on leaf tea, ¥5,700 on tea drinks) to ¥12,115 in 2025 (¥3,153 on leaf tea, ¥8,962 on tea drinks).
- Younger households spend more on tea drinks; older households spend more on leaf tea.

Shizuoka dates the turning point: from fiscal 2007, households have spent more on tea drinks than on leaf tea, and that has not reversed. Where leaf tea is bought has changed too. In a government survey for 1999, general shops had 38% and supermarkets 29%; in a 2020 ministry survey supermarkets had 48.7%, specialist tea shops 15.7%, mail order 15.1%, drugstores 10.9% and convenience stores 5.2%.

**The tea ceremony shaped matcha.** The ministry's Uji page says shade growing was developed in the 16th century to meet the demand of the flourishing tea ceremony, producing a matcha that was deep green, less astringent and rich in savoury taste. Kyoto prefecture's tea ordinance credits tea culture, together with the tea ceremony, with a large part in the development of Japanese cuisine, ceramics, crafts and architecture. The regions page covers the schools of the ceremony.

## What it costs

Prices below are tax included, as posted by the operator on the date at the top of this page. As of 2026-10-10, Japan's consumption tax is 10% for eating in and 8% for takeaway, which is why some chains post two prices.

**Itohkyuemon, Uji main shop tea room.** Every price is shown tax included and most sweets come two ways, alone or as a set with tea:

- Itohkyuemon parfait — ¥1,090 alone, ¥1,590 with tea
- Special Itohkyuemon parfait — ¥1,390 alone, ¥1,890 with tea
- Special "greedy" matcha parfait (*yokubari*) — ¥1,690 alone, ¥2,190 with tea
- Uji matcha, Uji sencha or Uji gyokuro — ¥990 each
- Matcha experience set, in which you whisk both thin and thick matcha yourself — ¥2,500
- Uji kintoki shaved ice (late April to mid-September) — ¥1,090 with vanilla ice cream, ¥1,190 with strong matcha ice cream, ¥500 more as a tea set

Many of the parfaits are seasonal; a matcha Mont Blanc parfait, for example, is planned from mid-September to late October at ¥1,390 alone or ¥1,890 with tea.

**A chain in Tokyo.** Starbucks lists a GINZA stone-ground matcha Frappuccino at ¥1,240 for a Tall, in limited stores. It states that its posted prices are eat-in prices including tax, that the tax rate differs for takeaway, and that some stores charge differently.

I have not given prices for Nakamura Tōkichi, another well-known Uji tea house, because its menus are images and I could not confirm whether they include tax.

## The same name, a different place

**Cities in the tea prefectures buy more leaf; Tokyo buys more drinks.** The household survey (households of two or more, 2023–25 average) records what families buy for home:

| City | Leaf green tea, g a year | Rank | Leaf green tea, ¥ a year | Tea drinks, ¥ a year | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| National | 647 | – | 3,187 | 8,633 | – |
| Shizuoka | 1,215 | 1st | 7,697 | 7,393 | 37th |
| Kyoto | 1,030 | 3rd | 3,176 | 5,633 | 52nd (last) |
| Fukuoka | 946 | 5th | 3,616 | 6,679 | 41st |
| Nagoya | 699 | 17th | 2,766 | 8,181 | 25th |
| Tokyo (23 wards) | 571 | 40th | 3,444 | 9,319 | 10th |
| Osaka | 554 | 44th | 2,074 | 7,481 | 35th |

By quantity of leaf tea the top five are Shizuoka, Hamamatsu, Kyoto, Tsu and Fukuoka. Shizuoka prefecture says its people drink about twice the national average. The survey covers home purchases; how it treats eating out, business use and gifts, I could not confirm. The table shows a difference in what households buy. It does not show why.

**Tea is not always finished where it is grown.** The ministry's chain — crude tea near the field, finishing and blending in the consuming areas — means a tea sold in a tea town need not be that town's leaf alone. Shizuoka described itself in 2020 as the market where 55% of Japan's tea gathers, and "Uji tea" is by definition a blend that can include leaf from outside Kyoto.

**I could not compare like with like.** I found no official, tax-included prices for the same parfait, cup of matcha or shaved ice in Uji and in Tokyo, so this page does not say which is cheaper.

## What people get wrong

- **"Matcha is just powdered green tea."** It is a specific leaf, tencha: shaded for two to three weeks, steamed, dried without rolling, then ground.
- **"Matcha comes from Uji."** Kagoshima grows the most tencha. Uji's name is about history and finishing, not volume.
- **"Japanese people drink tea from a pot."** Fewer every year. Households now spend far more on tea drinks than on leaf tea.
- **"Uji tea is grown in Uji."** In the ministry's definition it is leaf from Kyoto, Nara, Shiga and Mie, finished by Kyoto firms using methods that come from the Uji area.
- **"Sencha is most of Japan's tea."** By one count (Shizuoka's, for sencha and deep-steamed sencha together) it is about 85% of tea in circulation; by the ministry's production table, sencha is 48.1%. It depends how the kinds are divided.
