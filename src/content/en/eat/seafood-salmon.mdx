Salmon in Japan is a northern fish and an autumn fish. It runs up the rivers of Hokkaido, Tōhoku and Niigata in the autumn, and the farm ministry's pages on the dishes of those places share a theme: the whole fish is used, and much of it is salted where it is landed. This page covers salted salmon, the two forms of salmon roe, and the regional dishes built on them. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's page on salting says salted salmon was paid to the imperial court as one form of tax from the late 7th century, using chum, sockeye and coho salmon. Its regional pages add their own long histories:

- **Murakami, Niigata.** Salmon culture there is said to go back to the Heian period, when salmon was sent to the court in Kyoto as tax.
- **Iwate.** *Hizu*, the cartilage of the salmon's head, is described in the *Engishiki* as an offering to the court.
- **Sujiko** is said to have been prized since the Heian period.

The ministry's pages do not agree on the date of the *Engishiki*: one gives 905, another 926. I cannot settle that from these sources, so I give no date for it.

**The Ainu.** The ministry says the Ainu regarded salmon as a fish the gods sent from their own world for people. Cutting salmon into thin strips lengthwise, washing them in seawater and hanging them under the eaves is given as the origin of *sake-toba*, dried salmon strips.

## What makes it salmon country

**Salting, and where it is done.** Salting is the first step after the catch, so, the ministry says, it is basically done at the fishing grounds, which is why Hokkaido's output is large. It describes two methods:

- **Dry-salting** (*furijio-zuke*): salt rubbed directly on the fish. Usual for large salmon and trout. It draws out water well, but the salt can penetrate unevenly and the fat oxidises more easily.
- **Brining** (*tatejio-zuke*): soaking in salt water. Usual for roe and lightly salted products. The salt penetrates evenly and oxidation is slower.

**Shiobiki and aramaki.** Two names for salted whole salmon. *Shiobiki* is salted for several days to one or two weeks and is quite salty. *Aramaki* (*shinmaki*) is salted within about a day or two of the catch and is comparatively less salty. Where the name aramaki comes from, and the traditional way of gutting and wrapping the fish in straw, are not described on the ministry's page, so I leave them out.

**Sujiko and ikura.** The ministry's definitions:

- **Sujiko** — salmon eggs still packed in the thin membrane of the ovary.
- **Ikura** — the eggs loosened from that membrane into single grains.

Both are seasoned either with salt, to bring out their own savouriness and sweetness, or with soy-based seasonings; at home, soy is more common. In the Edo period, salted sujiko was broken up into grains and kept as a preserved food, and there was no separate name for the two.

**The word ikura.** It is said to come from Russian, arriving in the Meiji era. The ministry gives two accounts of how: from Russian prisoners of the Russo-Japanese War, or through contact with Russia when the northern fisheries were active. This site does not choose. The ministry's pages also give two dates for the product: one says the method for brined ikura came from Russia in the late Meiji era; another says production of loose-grained ikura began in the Taishō era and spread across the country.

## The whole fish

The ministry's regional pages describe using almost every part of the salmon.

- **Hizu-namasu, Iwate.** Iwate lands the most autumn salmon in Honshu, the ministry says. Almost everything except the gills can be eaten, and people once bought a whole fish and cooked it part by part. *Hizu-namasu* is a vinegared dish of the head cartilage, essential at New Year. The head of a salted or aramaki salmon can be used after soaking out the salt. On the Sanriku coast it is sometimes topped with ikura. Iwate's "Nanbu hanamagari" salmon is named for the male's hooked snout near spawning; it was an important source of income for the Nanbu domain in the Edo period and is now the prefectural fish.
- **Yakizuke, Murakami.** Murakami is known as a salmon town because salmon run up the Miomote River, and over 100 salmon dishes are said to exist there. *Yakizuke* is said to have been made since the Edo period as a way to keep fish before freezing existed. The salmon fishery runs in October and November, using a traditional net method called *iguri-ami*. Out of gratitude, the belly is not cut all the way through: the ministry quotes a belief that salmon, a gift from heaven, must not be made to commit seppuku.
- **Harako-meshi, Miyagi.** *Harako* is the local word for ikura, said to come from "belly child". Miyagi has many rivers where salmon run in autumn, its catch of chum salmon is among the highest in Japan, and it has run hatcheries for over 100 years, with 20 in the prefecture. The dish is rice cooked in the soy-based liquid used to simmer salmon, topped with the salmon and with ikura dipped in the same liquid. It was once a mixed rice; today the toppings are laid on separately. The season is about September to November.

**A story, as told.** The ministry passes on a tradition that the people of Arahama presented harako-meshi to Date Masamune when he inspected canal works there, and adds that fishermen were already eating it with salmon from beach seines on the Abukuma River. In Watari, each household's version differs, and "ours is the best" is the saying.

## How to eat it

**Ikura at home.** In autumn, when the salmon fishery is busy, sujiko appears in supermarkets and households make their own ikura. The ministry says ikura made in late autumn will keep until New Year if frozen.

**Hizu-namasu** is sold at fishmongers and supermarkets in the salmon season and before New Year.

**Sake-toba** is made by cutting half a fish into long thin strips, soaking them in brine and drying them on a net for about a week, in the season when it is below 10°C — late autumn to before spring.

## Not all of Japan's salmon is this

The ministry's regional pages show the same fish taking different forms: rice with roe in Miyagi, vinegared head cartilage in Iwate, grilled and marinated fish in Murakami, wind-dried strips in Hokkaido. I did not find official pages on salmon dishes in other regions, so the list stops there.

## Salmon away from the north

I could not find an official source comparing salmon eaten where it is landed with salmon eaten in Tokyo, so this page does not compare them. What the sources do say is that salting is done at the fishing grounds, so Hokkaido’s output is large. In Iwate, hizu-namasu appears in shops in the salmon season and before New Year; that describes how it is sold there, not a difference from anywhere else.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ikura and sujiko are different roe."** Same salmon eggs: in the membrane, or loosened from it.
- **"Ikura is an old Japanese word."** It is said to be from Russian, from the Meiji era, though how it arrived is told two ways.
- **"Aramaki means heavily salted."** The ministry says aramaki is salted soon after the catch and is comparatively less salty than shiobiki.
- **"Salmon is only eaten as fillets."** In the north the head, the roe and the whole body each have their own dishes.
