Steak in Japan comes in two forms that are easy to confuse. One is teppanyaki, where a chef cooks wagyu on an iron griddle in front of you, and which a Kobe restaurant says it served first. The other is the steak chain, where the same steak is sold at 150 g, 200 g or 300 g and the price goes up with the weight. This page covers both, and the system of cut names that sits behind every steak menu.

For wagyu in general, start from the wagyu overview.

## Where it came from

**Beef first.** The Agriculture and Livestock Industries Corporation (ALIC), a government agency, says the navy adopted beef as a nourishing food in 1869, and that beef-eating began to spread after the Meiji emperor ate it in 1872, with Fukuzawa Yukichi also promoting it. ALIC treats that boom as the source of today’s beef dishes. Its page does not discuss steak or the old word *bifuteki*, and I could not reach a primary source on where that word came from, so I do not give one.

**Teppanyaki, by its own account.** Misono, a restaurant founded in Kobe, says on its official site that it has a history going back to 1945 (Shōwa 20) and that it was the first restaurant in the world to serve teppanyaki steak. It says it was born in Kobe and later opened in Osaka, Kyoto and Shinjuku. The Osaka tourism board repeats the same claim in its listing of the Osaka branch, and VISIT KOBE, the city’s official tourism site, also says Misono was the first to serve teppanyaki steak. All three are passing on what the restaurant says. I found no independent check of it, so this site reports it as the restaurant’s claim and leaves it there.

**The chains, by their own histories.**

- **1976** — Steak no Don opened its first shop in Maebashi, Gunma. The company says its ¥980 steak was considered unusually cheap at the time, and that cars queuing for the car park backed up onto the national road. Its operator, Arcmeal, listed 62 Steak no Don shops as of 31 March 2026.
- **1978** — Bronco Billy’s founder opened a steakhouse called Bronco in Kita-ku, Nagoya, in June, according to the company’s history.
- **2013** — Ikinari Steak opened in Ginza in December, serving thick steak sold by weight to customers eating standing up, at half the price of the company’s own charcoal-grill steak restaurants. Its operator’s history records 30 shops by December 2014, the 200th shop in February 2018, shops in all 47 prefectures by November 2018, and the 500th shop in December 2019.

## What makes it teppanyaki

**A chef and a griddle.** VISIT KOBE describes teppanyaki as steak grilled on a hot iron plate, cooked by the chef in front of the guest, so that the fat melts into a browned surface and the juices stay in. JNTO, the national tourism organisation, calls it a particular way of cooking steak in Japan, in which a chef grills wagyu on a griddle in front of the customer, and says it is cooked to at least medium to medium-rare.

**A steakhouse is something else.** Bronco Billy’s history calls its first shop a steakhouse, and its menu sells charcoal-grilled steak. Neither Misono nor Bronco Billy compares itself with the other, and I found no official text that sets out the difference. Put side by side, their own descriptions point to one thing. Teppanyaki is a method, a chef cooking on a griddle in front of you. A steakhouse is a type of restaurant. The difference you will notice is who does the cooking and where.

## How the cuts are named

A steak menu borrows from two different naming systems, and they do not match.

- **The shop’s cuts.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says Japanese beef is divided into 11 retail cuts under a retail quality standard, and that yakiniku restaurants and similar places divide it further into rarer cuts. The same article’s illustrated guide shows 19. I could not open the standard itself, so I do not list its cut names here.
- **The trade’s cuts.** The Japan Meat Grading Association’s standard for wholesale beef uses 13 names: *nekku*, *kata*, *kata-rōsu*, *kata-bara*, *hire*, *rib-rōsu*, *sāroin*, *tomo-bara*, *uchi-momo*, *shintama*, *ran-ichi*, *soto-momo* and *sune*. Several are familiar from steak menus: *hire* is fillet, *sāroin* sirloin, *rib-rōsu* rib loin.
- **The menu’s cuts.** *Karubi*, the yakiniku word, is Korean for rib, and the ministry’s guide lists it as *karubi* (*tatebara*). The yakiniku page covers how *rōsu* and *karubi* became menu names.

The 11 retail cuts and the 13 wholesale names are two separate yardsticks, one for shops and one for the trade. A steak chain’s rib loin, sirloin and fillet are names that appear on the wholesale list.

## The shops, and what they charge

Prices below were posted on the chains’ official sites, tax included, as checked on 2026-10-11. All three say, or show, that some shops differ.

- **Ikinari Steak.** The menu says all prices include tax and that eating in and takeaway cost the same. Wild Steak ¥1,710 for 150 g, ¥2,190 for 200 g, ¥2,920 for 300 g, ¥3,890 for 450 g. Shoulder loin ¥1,450 / ¥1,860 / ¥2,470 / ¥3,300 at the same weights. Rib loin ¥2,200 for 150 g, ¥2,940 for 200 g, ¥4,410 for 300 g, ¥5,880 for 400 g, then ¥750 for each extra 50 g. Fillet ¥1,890 for 100 g, ¥2,830 for 150 g.
- **Steak no Don.** Gekiatsu Steak ¥1,738 for 130 g, ¥2,288 for 180 g, ¥3,058 for 280 g. Sirloin ¥3,179 for 150 g, ¥3,949 for 200 g, ¥5,049 for 300 g. Rice, bread and the soup bar are included, with refills. After 10 pm a late-night charge of 10% is added.
- **Bronco Billy.** Charcoal-grilled Black Diamond sirloin, on sale from 1 April 2026, ¥2,893 for 150 g, ¥3,663 for 200 g, ¥5,313 for 300 g, ¥6,633 for 400 g. I could not open the pages for its regular sirloin, so those prices are not here.

I did not collect teppanyaki prices for this page. For prices at Kobe’s steak houses, see the Kobe beef page.

## How to order it

**At a chain, choose the weight.** The same steak is offered in several sizes, and the price rises in steps. At Ikinari Steak, above a set size the price goes up by a fixed amount for every 50 g. Check the weight next to the price before you order.

**Check the extras.** At Steak no Don, rice, bread and soup come with the steak. After 10 pm the bill is 10% higher.

**At a teppanyaki counter, the chef cooks.** JNTO says the steak is cooked to at least medium to medium-rare.

## Not all of Japan’s steak is this

The three chains on this page started in three different places: Maebashi in Gunma, Nagoya, and Ginza in Tokyo. Teppanyaki, by Misono’s account, started in Kobe. I found no official source describing regional styles of steak in Japan, so I do not invent any.

## Teppanyaki away from Kobe

By its own account, Misono began in Kobe and later opened in Osaka, Kyoto and Shinjuku. Its Osaka branch is listed by the Osaka tourism board with the same claim to be the first. I found nothing that compares the Kobe and other branches, in price or anything else, so I do not.

## What people get wrong

- **"Teppanyaki is a kind of steakhouse."** It is a way of cooking, with a chef at a griddle in front of you. A steakhouse is a kind of restaurant.
- **"The first teppanyaki restaurant is a known fact."** It is Misono’s own claim, repeated by two tourism sites. I found no independent check.
- **"Steak in Japan is always wagyu at a high price."** The chains sell steak by weight, from ¥1,450 for 150 g of shoulder loin at Ikinari Steak, tax included, as checked on 2026-10-11.
- **"The menu name is the cut."** Retail uses 11 cuts, the wholesale trade 13 names, and yakiniku menus have names of their own.
