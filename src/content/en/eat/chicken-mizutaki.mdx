Mizutaki is a pot of chicken simmered in its own stock, followed by vegetables, then rice or noodles in what is left. What sets Hakata’s apart, on the official record, is that it has both a named founder with a date and a broader story about how Chinese, Western and Japanese cooking met in it — and that Fukuoka has written down an order in which to eat it.

For chicken in Japan generally — what jidori means and how the parts are named — start from the chicken overview.

## Where it came from

Two accounts, given side by side by the same official sources. This site keeps both.

- **The broad story.** The farm ministry’s local-food database says mizutaki began with a Chinese-style chicken stew, lightly salted, that spread when foreign ships called at Nagasaki. Western soups such as consommé and elements of Japanese cooking were added, and it became a dish of Fukuoka’s own. The ministry presents this as what "is said".
- **The founder.** The ministry and Fukuoka city both name Hayashida Heizaburō, from Nagasaki prefecture. The ministry says he used the Western cooking skills he learned in Hong Kong to open a mizutaki shop in the city in 1905. Fukuoka city adds that he went to Hong Kong at 15, learned Western cooking in an English household, brought the dish to Fukuoka in 1905 (Meiji 38) and opened the old house Suigetsu — again as what is said.

The two do not contradict each other, but they answer different questions: one is about what the dish is made of, the other about who first sold it. I could not find Suigetsu’s own current account, so I have not quoted it.

Fukuoka city also gives mizutaki another name, *Hakata-ni* (Hakata stew), and says it spread from Hakata across Japan. It does not say how.

## What makes it mizutaki

**The chicken, whole.** The ministry’s recipe uses a young chicken (*wakadori*) cut at the joints into bone-in pieces, started in cold water, with liver, heart and gizzard added. A strong stock made from the head and carcass goes in too. The parts match the names in the ministry’s trading standard for chicken meat.

**Two soups, or one.** The same recipe makes a clear soup and a cloudy one and combines them. Another ministry page describes the pure white soup as stock drawn from the chicken’s head and bones. Fukuoka prefecture’s tourism site says shops differ: some serve a cloudy soup, some a clear one. So a white soup is one style, not the definition.

**The pot and the table.** The recipe moves everything into an earthenware pot (*donabe*) and serves it with vegetables, condiments and ponzu.

## How to eat it

There is an order, and the official sources describe it slightly differently.

**Fukuoka city:**

1. Ladle some soup alone into your bowl and taste it with *kōtō negi*, a thin leek that is a Fukuoka speciality, as the condiment.
2. Then eat the chicken, with citrus juice, soy sauce and *yuzu-koshō*.

**The farm ministry:**

1. Add salt and leek to the soup and eat the chicken first.
2. When the soup has grown rich, add the vegetables.
3. Use a dipping sauce of rice vinegar, soy sauce, *daidai* citrus vinegar and mirin, and condiments, as you like.
4. Finish by putting rice cakes (*mochi*), udon or rice into the pot, and make a rice porridge (*zōsui*) with egg and leek.

Fukuoka prefecture’s tourism site gives *zōsui* as the finish and recommends udon too. The point common to all three: the soup comes first, and the last of it is eaten with starch.

## The shops, and what they charge

I did not find tax-included prices published by mizutaki shops, so none are given here. The ministry says there are many old specialist houses, and that more casual shops that younger people find easy to walk into are increasing.

## Not all of Hakata’s mizutaki is the same

- **Cloudy or clear.** It depends on the shop, according to Fukuoka prefecture’s tourism site.
- **The finish.** Zōsui, udon, mochi or rice, depending on whose description you read.
- **At home and at school.** The ministry says mizutaki is still made in Fukuoka homes, handed down from parents to children, and served in school lunches.
- **At the Yamakasa.** The ministry says young-chicken mizutaki is always eaten at the *naorai*, the ceremonial meal, of the Hakata Gion Yamakasa festival. It is said the men who carry the heavy floats chose chicken to build their strength.
- **All year.** Fukuoka city says Hakata eats it in every season, not only winter.

## Mizutaki away from Hakata

I found no official source comparing mizutaki in Hakata with mizutaki in Tokyo or elsewhere — in soup style, order of eating or price — and this site does not say one is better. What can be said is how the official descriptions from Fukuoka put it together: bone-in chicken with the offal, the soup tasted first, and zōsui or noodles at the end. A pot served differently elsewhere is a different choice, not something this page ranks.

## What people get wrong

- **"Mizutaki is just chicken boiled in water."** The name sounds like it, but the ministry’s recipe builds a strong stock from the head and carcass.
- **"The soup must be white."** Fukuoka’s tourism site says some shops serve it clear.
- **"It is a winter dish."** Fukuoka city says Hakata eats it all year.
- **"Its origin is unknown."** Two official sources name a founder and a year, 1905, while the ministry also gives a broader Chinese and Western origin. Both are on the record.
- **"There is one fixed order."** The city and the ministry describe the order differently; what they share is soup first and a starch to finish.
