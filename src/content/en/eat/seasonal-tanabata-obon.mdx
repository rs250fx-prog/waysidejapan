Two summer events, a month apart, share one food. At Tanabata on 7 July and at Obon in mid-August, *sōmen*, the thin wheat noodles, is offered and eaten. The official sources give each event its own reason for them, and the reasons have nothing to do with each other. Obon adds more: dumplings set out to welcome and send off the ancestors, Buddhist meals without meat or fish, and regional dishes that change from one prefecture to the next.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the [overview of food for annual events](/en/eat/seasonal/).

## Where it came from

**Tanabata.** The farm ministry’s *washoku* culture material says the event combines a Chinese legend of two stars, the Herd Boy and the Weaver Girl, with *kikkōten*, a Chinese rite of praying for skill in the arts, both brought to Japan and merged with an older Japanese belief in *tanabatatsume*, a weaving maiden. The material does not explain that belief further. Tanabata is one of the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*.

**Obon.** The ministry’s *aff* magazine says Obon combines ancestor worship with Buddhism, and that its formal name is *urabon-e*. It falls around 13 to 16 August. The basic offerings, the magazine says, are the five called *go-ku*: incense, flowers, light, pure water, and food and drink.

## What makes it Tanabata

The sources give several explanations for sōmen at Tanabata, and they do not all agree. They are set side by side here:

- **Sakubei.** The ministry’s culture material says there was a Chinese-style confection called *sakubei*, wheat flour kneaded into a cord and fried in oil. Eating it was believed to protect against epidemics, and it is said to be the forerunner of sōmen.
- **Thread.** The ministry’s Hokkaido office says that at Tanabata sōmen is offered or eaten as a likeness of thread.
- **The Milky Way.** A Setagaya ward food leaflet, which says it draws on the ministry, says sōmen is likened to the Milky Way or to the thread the weaver princess spins, and that sakubei was offered at Tanabata rites in the Heian period.
- **Court ladies.** The Miwa sōmen cooperative in Nara says that from the Heian period sōmen, called sakubei, was prized at court rites and banquets, and that in the Muromachi period court ladies called it *ozoro* and served it at Tanabata. It does not cite documents for this.

The culture material adds a Heian court custom: a poem was written on a leaf of the paper mulberry, *kaji*, sōmen was wrapped in the leaf, and the bundle was thrown onto the roof.

This site does not choose between these accounts. For the noodles themselves, and how Miwa and Banshū make them, see [sōmen](/en/eat/somen/).

## What makes it Obon

**Dumplings for coming and going.** The *aff* magazine says the food offering is *mukae-dango*, welcoming dumplings, on the 13th, and *okuri-dango*, send-off dumplings, on the night of the 15th.

**Sōmen as reins.** The same magazine says sōmen at Obon is likened to the reins of the horse the ancestors ride, and carries a wish for happiness to last a long time. It is a separate explanation from any given for Tanabata: one food, two events, two stories.

**Shōjin meals.** The magazine says Obon food follows *shōjin ryōri*, Buddhist cooking that uses no meat or fish, based on the Buddhist teaching against taking life. Its basic form is one soup and three dishes: rice, a clear soup, a simmered dish, a dressed or vinegared dish, and pickles. It adds that cooking with ingredients the person who died used to like is also a form of remembrance. The temple side of this cooking is in the guide to [kaiseki and shōjin ryōri](/en/eat/kaiseki-shojin/).

## How to eat it

The official pages describe offerings and meanings, not a way of eating. One leaflet from the ministry’s Kyushu bureau, on Tanabata and the midsummer *doyō* days, introduces sōmen sent down a channel of green bamboo, *nagashi-sōmen*, but gives no origin for it or for sōmen at Tanabata. For how sōmen is boiled and served at home, the [sōmen page](/en/eat/somen/) gives the producers’ own instructions.

## Not all of Obon is this

The *aff* magazine lists regional Obon foods:

- **Parts of Hokkaido and Tohoku**: *sekihan*, rice with azuki, to ward off evil.
- **Miyagi**: *zunda mochi*.
- **The Matsusaka area of Mie**: *bon-jiru*, a vegetable soup.
- **Nagano**: tempura *manjū*.
- **Kagoshima**: *kainoko-jiru*.

The magazine does not describe each dish in detail, and this page does not add to it.

The ministry’s Hokkaido office adds two more. At *nanuka-bon*, *arare* of washed rice mixed with chopped daikon and cucumber is scattered on graves. It also gives sekihan, called *okowa*, as an Obon food. How Hokkaido and Akita make their own sekihan is in the guide to [mochi, sekihan and zōni](/en/eat/rice-sides-mochi/).

## Sōmen away from Tanabata

Here the food is the one that travels, not the people. Miwa sōmen is made in the cold, dry months of winter and eaten in summer, and on two dates in the summer calendar it picks up a meaning: thread, the Milky Way or a fried forerunner at Tanabata, the reins of the ancestors’ horse at Obon. Official sources do not say whether the noodles eaten on those days differ from ordinary sōmen, or compare how the two events are kept in producing areas such as Miwa and in cities elsewhere.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sōmen at Tanabata and at Obon mean the same thing."** The ministry gives different reasons for each.
- **"There is one Tanabata story."** The ministry, a ward leaflet and a producers’ cooperative give at least four explanations between them.
- **"Obon food is whatever the family likes."** The ministry describes shōjin meals without meat or fish, while also saying that cooking the dead person’s favourite ingredients is a form of remembrance.
- **"Obon food is the same everywhere."** The ministry lists different dishes from Hokkaido to Kagoshima.
