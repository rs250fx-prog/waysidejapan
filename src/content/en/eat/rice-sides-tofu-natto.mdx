Tofu and nattō are both made from soybeans. Beyond that they have little in common. Tofu is soy milk set into a block; nattō is whole boiled beans fermented by bacteria. And "nattō" itself turns out to be more than one food.

For rice and the things around it in general, start from the rice and sides overview.

## Where it came from

**Tofu.** The sources I could use describe how tofu is made, not where it began. The national tofu makers’ association’s site would not open (a certificate error), so I have no industry account to give.

**Nattō.** The farm ministry’s page on Ibaraki nattō gives two stories and does not choose between them. Neither does this site.

- **The warhorse beans.** In the Heian period, beans boiled as feed for the horses of Minamoto no Yoshiie’s army, on its way to pacify the north-east, were left wrapped in straw and began to form strings. His retainers ate them, found them good, and offered them to Yoshiie. The page presents this as a legend.
- **The small beans.** The Mito domain encouraged growing early-ripening soybeans as a precaution against floods of the Naka river. The beans were small and not suited to soy sauce or miso, so they came to be used for nattō, which could be made well even with small beans.

The ministry then says what made Mito’s name: in 1889 the Mito Railway (today’s JR Mito Line) opened, nattō was sold on the platform at Mito station and elsewhere, and the town became widely known for it. Small beans, it adds, are said to have been well liked because they cling easily to rice.

## What makes it tofu and nattō

**Tofu, pressed or not.** The ministry’s guide defines tofu as soybeans ground and heated with water, the pulp (*okara*) removed, and the soy milk set with a coagulant. Then the two familiar kinds:

- **Momen** — the soy milk is set once, broken up, put into a mould box and pressed to squeeze out water, and set again.
- **Kinugoshi** — thicker soy milk is set with a coagulant just as it is.

That is the whole difference in method: whether the water is pressed out, or the curd is left as it sets.

The same guide also mentions tofu wrapped in straw and boiled, such as *tsuto-dōfu* in Fukushima and *komo-dōfu* in Ibaraki, handed down in several places as food for special occasions.

**Nattō, two families.** The ministry separates them clearly:

- **Itohiki nattō** (stringy nattō) — boiled soybeans fermented with nattō bacteria, with no salt. This is the sticky kind.
- **Shiokara nattō** (salty nattō, also *tera-nattō*, "temple nattō") — soybean *kōji* soaked in salt water, lactic-fermented, then dried.

The guide lists regional nattō as nattō from Miyagi and Ibaraki, *yukiwari nattō* from Yamagata, and *tera-nattō* from Kyoto.

**Mito’s range.** The ministry’s Ibaraki page says Mito’s speciality is small beans in particular, sold not only in plastic packs but as *kyōgi* nattō (wrapped in thin wood), *warazuto* nattō (wrapped in straw), *soboro* (or *shoboro*) nattō, and dried nattō. Its main home is the centre of the prefecture, around Mito city.

## How to eat it

From the Edo period on, the ministry says, the basic ways to eat nattō were in soup, or with soy sauce poured over.

*Shoboro nattō* is nattō mixed with dried strips of daikon, salted, then matured for about a month with soy sauce and mirin. It is eaten with rice, in *ochazuke*, and with drinks.

In Yamagata, nattō goes into soup. The ministry’s entry on *nattō-jiru* describes it as a winter home dish: the nattō is crushed in a mortar to thicken the soup. Because food ran short in winter, nattō was made at home as a preserved food, and nattō that did not form strings is said to have gone into the soup. When it is eaten depends on the area — around the seven-herb day in Murayama; on New Year’s Day and 3 January in Mogami; and in Shōnai on 9 December, the eve of the god Daikoku, and on New Year’s Eve.

I could not find a source on how *yudōfu* (simmered tofu) is eaten that I could use, so it is not covered.

## Not all of Japan’s nattō is this

Mito’s small beans in straw, Yamagata’s nattō soup and Kyoto’s salted, dried *tera-nattō* all carry the name. Whether nattō is eaten differently in eastern and western Japan as a whole, I could not confirm: the national nattō association’s pages would not display properly when I opened them, and I have not used them. Nor could I confirm any ranking of which prefecture eats the most.

## Nattō away from home

I could not find a source comparing nattō or tofu in their home towns with what is sold elsewhere. What the ministry shows is that the same name covers different foods in Mito, Yamagata and Kyoto.

## What people get wrong

- **"Momen and kinugoshi are the same curd, firmer or softer."** The ministry describes two methods: one pressed, one set as it is from thicker soy milk.
- **"Nattō is one food."** The salted, dried *tera-nattō* is made by a different method from the sticky kind.
- **"Nattō was invented by Yoshiie’s army."** That is one of two stories the ministry gives, and it presents it as a legend.
- **"Ibaraki eats the most nattō."** It may or may not; I could not confirm a ranking.
