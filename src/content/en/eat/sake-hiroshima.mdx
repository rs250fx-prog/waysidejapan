Hiroshima's sake story is not about a famous spring but a problem. In the prefecture's account, its coastal brewers' water was thought unsuitable for sake, and a brewer from the port of Mitsu spent twenty years learning to brew with it anyway. Today the centre is Saijō, an inland town in Higashihiroshima with seven or eight breweries near the station — and, the city says, water that is not soft at all. This page sets out both halves.

For what sake is and how to read a label, start from the [sake overview](/en/eat/sake/).

## Where it came from

**A rice port on the Inland Sea.** Hiroshima prefecture's account begins in the Edo period at Mitsu, now Akitsu in Higashihiroshima, where the Hiroshima domain kept a rice store. Along the coast — Kure, Yasuura, Akitsu, Takehara — brewing flourished from the late Edo period.

**A free-for-all, and spoiled sake.** In 1871 the old brewing licences (*sake-kabu*) were abolished, and anyone who filed a notice could brew. Newcomers multiplied across Japan, and many breweries struggled with *fuzō*, spoiled batches. The prefecture explains the cause as *hiochi*, contamination by lactic bacteria that raises the acidity of the mash or the sake and spoils its aroma.

**Miura Senzaburō.** Born in Mitsu in 1847, he began brewing in 1876. From books, and from visits to advanced districts such as Nada, he learned that water could be hard or soft, and that with soft water fermentation struggles to get going and the sake spoils easily. His answer, in the prefecture's words, was to treat the basics as important: brewing by measured values, how vats were washed and the kōji room built, and constant use of thermometer and clock. He beat the spoilage, and in 1898 published twenty-odd years of findings as *Kaijō-hō Jissen-roku*, sharing them with the whole trade. The prefecture says the quality of sake rose not only in Mitsu but across Hiroshima, and that overcoming soft water led in time to the birth of *ginjō* sake. The only original copy known to survive is in the National Diet Library, which has put it online.

**Machines and a school.** Hiroshima had few rivers steep enough for water-wheel mills, so rice was polished by hand. Satake Riichi began work on a polishing machine in 1895 and the next year produced Japan's first powered rice mill, improving it further on orders from Saijō's Kimura Wahei, whom the prefecture's Hiroshima Lab page calls the founder of today's Kamotsuru. The prefecture's technology centre credits the mill with raising the quality of ginjō brewing, and says Kamotsuru's founder Kimura Shizuhiko set up the Saijō Sake Brewing School to spread Miura's methods. The two prefectural pages name different Kimuras as Kamotsuru's founder; I give both as written.

**Public research.** A prefectural brewing experiment station opened in 1922, and a Saijō brewing station ran trial brewing from 1928 to 1975. Today the prefecture's food technology centre is the only public research institute in Japan that sells its trial sake, under the brand *Meikon*. The national research institute for brewing is also in Higashihiroshima.

## The water: two official accounts

**The coast.** Hiroshima prefecture says the water of the coastal brewing towns such as Mitsu was soft, and that Hiroshima's methods grew out of overcoming it.

**Saijō.** A walking map published by Higashihiroshima city in March 2012 tells a different story for the inland town. Saijō's brewing water is groundwater from Mount Ryūō. As rain from the headwaters seeps down through strata where rhyolite and granite are mixed, calcium and magnesium dissolve into it and turn the soft headwater into *moderately hard* water. The average hardness in the brewing district is about 80 mg/L. The map says this water is *thought to be* the secret of Saijō's sake — its wording, not a certainty. It also said in 2012 that the breweries opened their wells to visitors; whether that is still so, I have not confirmed.

So "Hiroshima sake is soft-water sake" is only half the record. And the prefecture's pages say only that soft water ferments weakly and spoils easily; they do not say it makes the sake taste a particular way, and neither does this page.

## Saijō's brewery street

**A post town.** In the Edo period Saijō was a post town called Yokkaichi on the old Saigoku road, with merchants and inns along the highway, and brewing began there. In the mid-Meiji era the town took the name Saijō and brought in the railway, and brewing along the road took off.

**Seven or eight breweries.** The count depends on the source and the year. The 2012 city map names eight around Saijō station: Kirei, Kamoizumi, Hakubotan, Fukubijin, Sanyōtsuru, Saijōtsuru, Kamokin and Kamotsuru. Kamotsuru's own page says seven within one kilometre, and the 2026 festival listed seven breweries as venues.

**What you see.** Red-brick chimneys, *namako* walls of tile and plaster, cedar balls (*sugidama*) under the eaves — a brewery's sign, once replaced when the new sake was ready — old press weights (*kakari-ishi*), and eight manhole covers found only on this street.

**A national historic site.** In a press release of 12 October 2023, Hiroshima prefecture said the national cultural council was due on 20 October to recommend the *Saijō Sakagura-gun*, the Saijō brewery group, as a historic site — one where you can follow small breweries growing into large ones. Kamotsuru's page says its No. 1 brewery was designated in February 2024 as part of the group. I could not open the national or city record of the formal designation date.

**"One of the three."** The prefecture calls Saijō one of Japan's three great sake districts; the city map ranks it with Nada and Fushimi. I found no official definition of "the three". The farm ministry adds that Saijō's sake took the top places at national competitions from the Meiji into the Taishō era, and that in the early Shōwa period the haiku poet Kawahigashi Hekigotō called it "the new capital of sake".

**The numbers.** Fiscal 2023 tax statistics give Hiroshima prefecture 56 licensed sake production sites and 6,696 kilolitres produced, about 2.1% of Japan's total. The prefecture itself speaks of 47 breweries. A licence is not a working brewery, so the two are not compared.

## The protected name

There is none. Neither Hiroshima nor Saijō is on the national tax agency's list of geographical indications for sake. A region name on a label still means all of the sake was brewed there, but GI rules such as Niigata's on local water and bottling do not apply. For how a GI works, see [Niigata sake](/en/eat/sake-niigata/).

## Bishu-nabe, the brewers' hotpot

The farm ministry and the festival organisers tell the same story. *Bishu-nabe* is chicken, gizzard, pork and vegetables seasoned only with salt, pepper and sake, said to have begun as a brewers' staff meal, kept plain so as not to spoil their tasting. The name is said to come from *bisho*, "soaked", for the brewery workers drenched by their work with water; the characters for "beautiful sake" were fitted to it later. The alcohol cooks off, and the festival says children and people who do not drink can eat it. The two sources list slightly different vegetables, so expect it to vary by place and year. For the region's other dishes, see [Setouchi's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/).

## Hiroshima sake away from Hiroshima

**A bottle in Tokyo carries no regional guarantee.** With no GI, a "Hiroshima" bottle promises that it was brewed in the region, nothing more about its water or rice.

**The breadth comes once a year.** The festival pours 801 sakes from all over Japan on two October days. In Tokyo, the brewers' association's information centre in Toranomon offers small glasses from many regions all year. Whether Hiroshima's Tokyo shop pours sake, I have not confirmed.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hiroshima sake is soft-water sake."** The coast's water was soft; the city says Saijō's is moderately hard.
- **"Soft water makes softer sake."** The prefecture's pages say soft water ferments weakly and spoils easily, not that it changes the taste.
- **"Saijō is officially one of Japan's three great sake towns."** It is often called that; I found no official list.
- **"Saijō is a GI."** Hiroshima has no sake GI.
- **"The festival is free."** The Sake Hiroba tasting cost ¥5,000 in 2026. Whether the main grounds charge entry is not stated on the pages I read.
