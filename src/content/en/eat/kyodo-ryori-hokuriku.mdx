Hokuriku here means four prefectures on the Japan Sea side of Honshu: Niigata, Toyama, Ishikawa and Fukui. The official descriptions of their dishes keep returning to the Kitamae trading ships, snow, and yellowtail, though not every theme appears in every prefecture. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Niigata

**Hegi soba.** Soba bound with *funori* seaweed and served on a *hegi*, a square tray of split wood. The farm ministry says it is said to come from Uonuma, born where the textile trade met soba: funori stiffened the weft threads on the looms, and someone wondered whether it could bind soba. Uonuma could not grow wasabi, so it is eaten with spring onion and mustard. The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page tells the same story of kimono thread, also as "said". For the noodle in general, see [soba](/en/eat/soba/).

**Noppe.** Taro simmered with vegetables, mushrooms and dried scallop, then thickened — a simmered dish, the ministry says, not a soup like *noppei-jiru* elsewhere. In this snowy country people made a great deal and kept the whole pot in the snow, which is why some still eat it cold. At New Year, festivals, memorials and weddings, every household in Niigata makes it; vegetables are cut round for celebrations and in rough pieces for Buddhist occasions.

**Sasa-dango.** Mugwort rice dumplings with bean paste, wrapped in bamboo leaves, eaten for about 500 years in Chūetsu, Kaetsu and part of Aizu. The ministry gives more than one explanation:

- **A travelling ration**, which Uesugi Kenshin is said to have carried.
- **Thrift**, using broken rice that could not be paid as tax.

This page gives both. They became famous as a recommended souvenir at the 1964 Niigata National Sports Festival.

**Tare katsudon in Niigata city.** Thin pork cutlets dipped in sweet soy-based sauce, two or more to a bowl, with no egg. The ministry's story: Furumachi, a port that prospered on the Kitamae ships, took to Western food early, and around the early Shōwa era an old shop there is said to have dipped the cutlet in soy sauce. Its apprentices spread it. The ministry gives only this one story. For the cutlet bowl elsewhere, see [katsudon](/en/eat/donburi-katsudon/).

### Toyama

**Masu-zushi.** The ministry calls it Toyama's most famous local dish. The sources tell its beginning differently:

- **The farm ministry**: sweetfish sushi in the Kyōhō era of the Edo period, later made with cherry salmon, spreading nationally as a station lunch box in the Taishō era.
- **The Cultural Agency**: a record in the early Heian *Engishiki* of Etchū (old Toyama) sending masu-zushi to the court.

This page gives both and does not choose. See [masu-zushi](/en/eat/sushi-masu/).

**White shrimp kakiage.** Toyama, the ministry says, is the only place in Japan where enough deep-water *shiro-ebi* are caught to make a fishery. Once dried whole for stock, the shrimp reached the market raw and peeled once freezing came in, and the taste was rediscovered.

**Buri daikon.** Yellowtail trimmings simmered with daikon. *Buri-okoshi*, thundery weather in late autumn and early winter, marks the height of the season. In Himi and some other coastal places, a bride's family sends a whole yellowtail as the year-end gift in the year of the marriage, and half is sent back. Some families use miso instead of soy sauce, and the Cultural Agency says the same of Himi.

**Kobu-jime.** Fish cured in kelp, born, the ministry says, when Hokkaido kelp on the Kitamae ships met Toyama Bay's fish. Most kelp used in Toyama is from Rausu; it is said that Toyama people who settled there in the Meiji era sent it home. The usual fish is swordfish, which Toyama calls *sasu*.

### Ishikawa

**Jibu-ni.** Duck dusted in flour and simmered with *sudare-fu* gluten and vegetables, a samurai-house dish from at least the Edo period. The ministry gives three theories of its origin:

- **Takayama Ukon**, who learned it from missionaries.
- **Okabe Jibuemon**, who brought it from Korea.
- **Russian castaways.**

The name is unsettled too — from *Jibu*, or from the sound of simmering, *jibu-jibu*. This page does not choose. It is served in its own wide, shallow bowl; homes often use farmed duck or chicken.

**Kabura-zushi.** Salted yellowtail pressed between slices of salted turnip and fermented with *kōji*, a *nare-zushi* with no vinegared rice. The ministry says the origin is unclear:

- **Fishermen of Kanaiwa** made it a New Year dish for good catches.
- **A Maeda lord** was served it at a hot spring inn where he took the waters. The ministry names no spring.
- **Commoners**, who could rarely afford yellowtail, ate it pressed in turnip.

This page gives all three. Toyama's list has a dish of the same name; neither page says which came first.

**Sawara no kobu-jime.** In Ishikawa, the ministry says, swordfish is called *sawara*; the fish called sawara elsewhere is *yanagi-sawara* or *sagoshi*. The same page says Toyama has a similar dish and calls the fish *sasu*.

**Nishin no kobu-maki and buri daikon.** Of the goods the Kitamae ships brought to Noto's ports, herring and kelp were the most plentiful; herring rolled in kelp is still part of New Year *osechi*. Ishikawa's buri daikon page also has buri-okoshi, and says the prefecture's fishing cooperative registered "wild Noto winter yellowtail" as a trademark in 2006. For the city's sushi, see [Kanazawa sushi](/en/eat/sushi-kanazawa/).

### Fukui

**Heshiko.** Fish, mostly mackerel, salted and then packed in rice bran, protein for a hard winter from Wakasa and the Echizen coast, made by the mid-Edo period, the ministry says. Two theories of the name:

- **From *heshi-komu***, the fishermen's word for pressing fish into a barrel.
- **From *hishio***, the liquid drawn out by salting.

This page gives both. The ministry adds that the route for salted mackerel to Kyoto has lately come to be called the "mackerel road"; see [saba-zushi](/en/eat/sushi-saba/).

**Oroshi soba.** Cold soba with grated daikon, for weddings, memorials, New Year's Eve and the late-night meal at *hōonkō*. The ministry says it is held to have begun when Asakura Takakage, who built his castle at Ichijōdani in 1473, had buckwheat grown as an emergency food. Honda Tomimasa, lord of Fuchū from 1601, brought soba makers, and the name "Echizen oroshi soba" spread from words the Shōwa emperor spoke after eating it in Fukui.

**Seige.** From Kōno in Minami-Echizen: legs of *seiko-gani*, the female snow crab, simmered in miso with grated daikon. Once eaten locally every day in winter, it began as a fisherman's dish cooked in a shell on board.

**Hōba-meshi.** Hot rice and sweet *kinako* in a magnolia leaf, for the celebration after rice planting. It was chosen in 1986 among the "100 home-town rice balls".

**Sauce katsudon.** Not in the farm ministry's list; it is a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2021, modern). The agency says Takabatake Masutarō returned from cooking in Germany in 1913 and made a katsudon with sauce; he opened in Tokyo around 1917 and began serving it in Fukui after the Great Kantō Earthquake. Two to four thin cutlets, no cabbage underneath. The agency calls oroshi soba and sauce katsudon the classic pair for eating out.

## What makes it Hokuriku

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Niigata 30, Toyama 30, Ishikawa 30, Fukui 29 — 119 in all. Among them:

- **Niigata**: noppe, sasa-dango, hegi soba, tare katsudon, *igo-neri*, *sasa-zushi*.
- **Toyama**: masu-zushi, white shrimp kakiage, buri daikon, kobu-jime, *hōonkō ryōri*.
- **Ishikawa**: jibu-ni, kabura-zushi, buri daikon, sawara no kobu-jime, *ishiru-nabe*.
- **Fukui**: heshiko, oroshi soba, seige, hōba-meshi, *honkosan*.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them: Niigata 6, Toyama 6, Ishikawa 4, Fukui 10. Niigata's hegi soba entry shows two fiscal years, 2021 and 2022, and I could not tell whether it is one entry or two. Ishikawa's four share no name with its 30 farm ministry dishes.

**What recurs.** Buri daikon and kabura-zushi are on both the Toyama and Ishikawa lists, kelp rolls on Toyama's and Fukui's, hōonkō food on Toyama's and, as honkosan, on Fukui's. Herring turns up in Ishikawa's kelp rolls, Fukui's herring sushi and Niigata's herring in kōji.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: Niigata's *Itarian*, Toyama Black, Kanazawa oden, Hanton rice, Echizen crab as a dish name, Wakasa's *kodai no sasazuke*, and Fukui's sauce katsudon. That is all this says. The nearest item to Echizen crab is seige.

## What the four share

I found no public source I could read that describes Hokuriku's food as a whole; the regional farm office's booklet could not be read as text, so I do not use it. What follows sets the prefectures' own descriptions side by side.

**The Kitamae ships.** Toyama's area story says the ships brought Hokkaido kelp to Toyama Bay, giving kobu-jime and kelp rolls. Ishikawa's herring and kelp came to Noto. Fukui's ports were Mikuni, Tsuruga and Obama, and the Cultural Agency says Fukui's herring sushi joined Kitamae herring to Wakasa's *nare-zushi*. Niigata's tare katsudon begins in a Kitamae port town.

**Snow and keeping food.** Noppe kept in the snow, and Niigata's 100-year foods include a "snow-country fermented and preserved food culture". Toyama's story says its cold, humid snowy climate suits fermentation. Fukui's says long, snowbound winters gave pickles and heshiko.

**Hōonkō, the Jōdo Shinshū memorial meal.** Toyama, the ministry says, is called a "Shinshū kingdom": around 28 November, the anniversary of Shinran's death, families serve hōonkō food on red lacquer trays, keeping the year's best vegetables for it. In Fukui, Jōdo Shinshū is strongest in Reihoku, and honkosan brings a one-soup, three-dish vegetarian meal. In Ishikawa, Wakura Onsen's tourist association says kinshi-uri squash has long been used in hōonkō food. I found nothing on hōonkō in Niigata.

**Rice and fish.** Toyama's story says it buys the most mochi and glutinous rice in Japan; Fukui's says Koshihikari rice was born there in 1956. Yellowtail is the fish of luck and gifts in Toyama and Ishikawa. Ishikawa's story names Noto's *ishiru* fish sauce one of Japan's three great fish sauces, with Akita's *shottsuru* and Kagawa's sand-eel sauce.

I found no public source that calls Hokuriku food light or strong in flavour, so I say neither.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Masu-zushi**: mostly bought from specialist shops, more than 20 in Toyama city.
- **Tare katsudon**: restaurants, mail order and souvenirs.
- **Kabura-zushi**: supermarkets and department stores at the year's end.
- **Kobu-jime**: izakaya, kelp shops and souvenir shops.
- **Jibu-ni**: *ryōtei*, *kappō* restaurants and delis.
- **Buri daikon**: Himi's yellowtail fair, at hotels, inns and restaurants.
- **Hōonkō food**: inns in Nanto offer it in stay plans; more Fukui inns make it a speciality.
- **Oroshi soba**: Fukui's many soba shops, and new-soba festivals at harvest.

**Prices.** The only official tax-inclusive price I found is not for a meal. Fukui's tourism federation lists a soba-making session at Echizen Soba no Sato at ¥2,200 a batch, tax included, enough for two plates (checked 11 October 2026). The page gives no update date, so it may not be current. I found none for the dishes above.

## How it is eaten

- **Hegi soba**: with spring onion and mustard.
- **Heshiko**: rinsed of bran and grilled, in *ochazuke*, in rice balls, lately on pasta.
- **Oroshi soba**: three ways in Fukui — stock and daikon apart, daikon in the stock, or daikon juice in the stock.

**When.** Hōonkō food around 28 November. Buri daikon in late autumn and winter. Echizen crab from 6 November to 20 March, female seiko crab only until 31 December. White shrimp are caught April to November but frozen, so eaten all year. Hōba-meshi in May. Noppe and kabura-zushi at New Year.

## Not all of Hokuriku is this

**Two katsudon.** Niigata's tare katsudon is on the farm ministry's list; Fukui's sauce katsudon is a 100-year food instead.

**One fish, two names.** The swordfish Toyama calls sasu is sawara in Ishikawa.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Unazuki Onsen.** Toyama's tourism site says the kaiseki at Entaiji-sō, an inn at Unazuki, uses white shrimp, firefly squid, *bai* whelk and yellowtail from Toyama Bay. These overlap with the ministry's Toyama dishes, though the page names ingredients, not dishes.

**Wakura Onsen.** Wakura's tourist association names kinshi-uri for hōonkō food, and winter yellowtail and Kanō crab. I did not confirm from an official page how Wakura is operating since the 2024 Noto earthquake. See [Wakura Onsen](/en/soak/wakura/).

**Awara Onsen.** A writer's piece on Fukui's tourism site (8 August 2023) points to a diner serving sauce katsudon and a soba shop known for *ten-oroshi* near Awara-Yunomachi station. Awara-Onsen station is on the Hokuriku shinkansen.

**No link found.** Official sources checked for this page tie no dish on the farm ministry's Ishikawa list to the Kaga spa towns such as [Yamanaka](/en/soak/yamanaka/), and the kabura-zushi hot-spring story names no spring. For Niigata, the official pages checked on [Tsukioka](/en/soak/tsukioka/), Senami and Echigo-Yuzawa tie none of these dishes to the spa.

**Getting there.** Official figures from different bodies and dates; check before you travel.

- **The shinkansen**: JR West says the Hokuriku shinkansen reached Kanazawa on 14 March 2015 and Tsuruga on 16 March 2024.
- **Kanazawa**: about 2 hours 30 minutes from Tokyo; or about 1 hour by air to Komatsu, then about 40 minutes by bus.
- **Toyama**: about 2 hours 5 minutes from Tokyo.
- **Fukui**: about 2 hours 51 minutes from Tokyo (a figure given before the extension opened).
- **Niigata**: the prefecture says you are inside it 1 hour 20 minutes from Tokyo; I found no official time to Niigata station.

## Hokuriku food away from Hokuriku

- **Niigata.** Nespas in Omotesandō closed on 25 December 2023. Its successor is Ginza Niigata Jōhōkan THE NIIGATA, 5-6-7 Ginza, with produce, sake tastings, rice balls, and Bit, an Italian restaurant from Tsubame-Sanjō — not a local-dish restaurant.
- **Toyama.** Nihonbashi Toyama-kan, Nihonbashi Muromachi, with a shop and the restaurant Toyama Hamasaku, which held a masu-zushi evening on 4 October 2026.
- **Ishikawa.** Hyakumangoku Monogatari Edo-honten in Ginza closed in October 2023; Yaesu Ishikawa Terrace opened in Yaesu in March 2024.
- **Fukui.** Fukui Shoku no Kuni 291, 1-5-8 Ginza, with an eat-in serving Echizen soba. I could not confirm from an official page how it relates to the shop once known by an older name.

**What changes.** At the source these dishes have seasons and settings: crab with a closing date, hōonkō in November, kabura-zushi at the year's end. The Tokyo shops sell all year. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sauce katsudon is Fukui's official local dish."** Sauce katsudon is not in the farm ministry's list; it is a Cultural Agency 100-year food.
- **"Sawara means Spanish mackerel."** In Ishikawa it means swordfish.
- **"Nespas is Niigata's Tokyo shop."** It closed in December 2023.
- **"Hokuriku is one cuisine."** The sources speak prefecture by prefecture.
