Butadon is pork grilled with a sweet soy sauce on it and laid over rice. Elsewhere in Japan the word can mean any pork bowl. In Obihiro, in the Tokachi district of eastern Hokkaido, it means one particular dish, with a settler history behind it and a city that calls itself the birthplace. This page sets out what the official sources say about where it came from, what goes into it, and how to eat it near the source.

For rice bowls generally, start from the [donburi overview](/en/eat/donburi/).

## Where it came from

**Pigs in Tokachi.** The farm ministry's local-food page says pig farming began in the Tokachi district around the end of the Meiji era, and pork has been eaten there since. The ministry's Hokkaido page gives a name to that start: Yoda Benzō of the Banseisha, a group of settlers who came to farm Tokachi, began keeping pigs for food, beginning with four.

**The bowl.** The official accounts tell the same story at different levels of detail. They are set out here side by side.

- **The farm ministry, dish page.** In the early Shōwa era a diner in Obihiro served a bowl of charcoal-grilled pork coated in a sauce like the one used for grilled eel, *kabayaki*. It was devised as stamina food for farmers and settlers. The diner had wanted to use eel, but eel was expensive and hard to get, so it used pork, which was close at hand.
- **The farm ministry, Hokkaido page.** In the early Shōwa era a popular diner in Obihiro put the bowl on its menu as something "truly Tokachi", and it caught on.
- **The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page.** The Banseisha brought four pigs to Tokachi in the Meiji era and raised them under the motto "cows for milk, horses for power, pigs for food". Around the 1930s a young cook, aiming for a taste ordinary people could afford in a time of war and recession, took a hint from the eel bowl and created a soy-based flavour. The page cites a free magazine published by the Tokachi Mainichi newspaper.

A diner in one account, a young cook in another: neither names the person or the shop, and neither gives a year. Individual shops publish their own founding stories; official sources do not confirm them, so none is repeated here.

**Obihiro's claim.** The farm ministry says Obihiro is held to be the birthplace, and that 10 February is registered as Butadon Day. Obihiro city says simply that butadon was born in Obihiro.

**Two lists.** Butadon is on the farm ministry's list of Hokkaido's local dishes, one of 27. It is also one of the Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, in the "future" category, from fiscal 2023. The two lists are chosen separately; butadon is one of four Hokkaido dishes on both, with Ishikari-nabe, jingisukan and Muroran yakitori.

## What makes it butadon

- **The meat.** Mostly pork loin or belly, according to the ministry.
- **The cooking.** Grilled, ideally over charcoal, which the ministry says makes it more fragrant. The ministry's Hokkaido page describes the Tokachi way as pork grilled well with the sauce on it, then laid over rice.
- **The sauce.** Sugar and soy, in the style of eel *kabayaki*. The ministry's home recipe uses 2 tablespoons of soy sauce, 1 tablespoon of sugar and half a tablespoon of mirin.
- **On top.** Finely shredded white leek is common, the ministry says.

That is the whole dish: rice, grilled pork, sauce. The eel bowl it was modelled on is the same shape, with pork in place of the eel.

## The shops, and what they charge

Obihiro city says the city has many specialist butadon shops and publishes a butadon map. The farm ministry says butadon is widely served at restaurants across Tokachi.

No price is given here. The city's page does not list one, and no shop price with a tax-included label and a date was checked for this page.

## How to order it

- **Use the city's map.** Obihiro's butadon map is the city's own guide to its specialist shops.
- **Take the sauce home.** The farm ministry lists butadon sauce among the forms it is sold in, so the sauce travels even if the bowl does not.

## Not all of Japan's rice bowls are this

- **Gyūdon**, the beef bowl, is a chain-shop staple that began at a Tokyo fish market. See [gyūdon](/en/eat/donburi-gyudon/).
- **Katsudon** is a fried cutlet, usually set in egg, but dipped in sauce in Fukui and Komagane. See [katsudon](/en/eat/donburi-katsudon/).
- **Niigata's tare-katsudon** also uses a sweet soy sauce, but on fried cutlets rather than grilled pork. It is covered in [Hokuriku's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokuriku/).

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Obihiro.** The city is the birthplace and has the specialist shops. The Tokachigawa Onsen tourism association gives these journey times to Obihiro: from Sapporo, about 3 hours 50 minutes on the Potato Liner (ten round trips a day), or about 2 hours 13 minutes on the Tokachi Milky Liner by way of New Chitose Airport; by car, about 2 hours 45 minutes from the Sapporo interchanges to Obihiro interchange. From Tokachi-Obihiro Airport to Obihiro is about 35 minutes by car.

**Tokachigawa Onsen.** The tourism association's food page invites visitors to try "both butadon and ramen" at the spa and recommends Obihiro's butadon by name, listing it under a sushi restaurant too. From JR Obihiro station, Tokachi bus route 45 takes about 28 minutes; some runs are weekdays only and need booking. There is no direct bus from Sapporo. The farm ministry does not mention Tokachigawa, so the link is a Tokachi dish near a Tokachi onsen, not the onsen's invention. For the water itself, see [Hokkaido's onsen](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/).

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Butadon away from Obihiro

The word travels further than the dish. When Yoshinoya lost access to American beef in 2004, it switched to selling pork bowls and beef curry bowls until the beef bowl returned; that is a chain's pork bowl, not Tokachi's charcoal-grilled one, and the sources do not compare them. For pork in Japan more widely, including Kagoshima's black pork, see [Japanese pork](/en/eat/meat-pork/). For the rest of Hokkaido's local food, see [Hokkaido's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokkaido/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Butadon is just a cheap version of gyūdon."** Obihiro's is grilled pork in an eel-style sauce, with its own history in Tokachi.
- **"We know who invented it, and when."** The sources say a diner, or a young cook, in the early Shōwa era or around the 1930s. No official source names the person or the year.
- **"It was made to copy the beef bowl."** By the farm ministry's account, the model was the eel bowl.
- **"Tokachigawa Onsen invented it."** The onsen recommends it; the birthplace, by the official accounts, is Obihiro.
