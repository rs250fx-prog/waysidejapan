An *amamidokoro* (甘味処) is a sweet shop where you sit down: a bowl of bean soup in winter, shaved ice in summer, anmitsu or warabi-mochi in between. It is also surprisingly poorly documented. I could not find a public or trade body that defines the sweet shop as a type of business, dates it, or counts it. What can be said comes from individual shops and individual sweets, and that is how this page is built.

For sweets in Japan beyond wagashi in general, start from the sweets overview. For the confectionery itself — bean pastes, seasonal shapes, yōkan — start from the wagashi overview.

## Where it came from

**Anmitsu and Ginza.** The Chūō ward tourism association’s map of Tokyo firsts names Ginza Wakamatsu, in Ginza 5-chōme, as the shop where anmitsu began, devised by the second-generation owner, Mori Hanjirō, in 1930. The same page notes that the shop closed at the end of 2023. A Tokyo Shimbun article of May 2024 says the shop was founded in 1894, closed the previous December, and is *said* to be where anmitsu began. Those are the two sources. I could not open the shop’s own site, and I found no other origin story or explanation of the name that I could confirm. So: credited, not proven, and this site does not settle it.

What goes into anmitsu, the sources used here do not set out, so I have not described it.

**Mitsumame**, and the story of its origin at Ueno shops such as Funawa, I could only find in search summaries, not in a source I could read. It is left out.

**A sweet tea house in Kyoto.** Kyoto’s official tourism site introduces Bunnosuke Chaya in Higashiyama, a sweet tea house founded in 1909 (Meiji 42) by the rakugo storyteller Katsura Bunnosuke II. Its specialities are warabi-mochi and amazake. In summer it serves shaved ice; in winter, freshly grilled *mitarashi dango* and zenzai. That seasonal swap is the clearest picture I can give of what a sweet shop is.

## What makes it a sweet shop

Three ingredients carry most of the menu, and two of them are starches.

**Adzuki beans, and a word that swaps meaning.** The Toraya confectioners’ archive, in its 2014 exhibition on the sweets of east and west, explains that *zenzai* generally means, in the Kyoto–Osaka area, a sweet soup with whole adzuki beans in it, and in Tokyo, mochi or steamed millet with soupless bean paste poured over. What Kyoto and Osaka call zenzai, Tokyo calls *ogura-jiruko* (or *inaka-jiruko*). The same word, two dishes.

That is as far as the sources go. How *oshiruko* and other related words are used, or what zenzai means in Nagoya or Hokkaido, I could not confirm. Nor could I confirm the story that zenzai comes from Izumo’s *jinzai-mochi*, or anything about Okinawa’s zenzai.

**Kuzu.** The farm ministry, describing Nara’s *kuzu-mochi*, says *kuzu* starch is thought to take its name from the Kuzu people of the Yoshino area, who made and sold it. The starch is washed out of the root again and again in midwinter groundwater and dried for two to three months; when it is 100% kuzu starch, it is called *Yoshino hon-kuzu*. The method, *Yoshino-zarashi*, has continued since the Edo period, and the ministry says Yoshino’s winter cold and good water are what make good kuzu. *Kuzukiri*, the noodle-like kuzu sweet, appears in the same source only as one use of the starch, with no history.

**Warabi.** The farm ministry’s traditional-food database describes *warabi-mochi* as bracken-root starch (*warabi-ko*), water and sugar heated in a pan and then cooled to set. The name comes from the starch. Its origin legend: a monk of Tōdai-ji had bracken roots gathered on Mount Wakakusa, treated them like kuzu, found them good, and mixed in sugar to make a sweet for tea.

## The shops, and what they charge

I could not find sweet-shop menu prices from an official source. Bunnosuke Chaya’s tourism page gives none.

For reference only, Toraya sells instant *oshiruko* in winter — with wasanbon sugar, in three kinds of bean paste — at ¥2,149 for three packets and ¥4,082 for six, tax included, on its online shop. That is a product to take home, not a bowl at a sweet shop.

## How to order it

- **Read the season.** At the one shop documented here, the menu turns over: shaved ice in summer, zenzai and grilled dango in winter.
- **Ask what zenzai means here.** In a Kyoto or Osaka shop expect soup with beans; in Tokyo expect paste on mochi, with the soupy version under another name.
- **On eating kuzu sweets in summer**, the wagashi overview explains why they are eaten at room temperature.

## Not all of Japan’s sweet shops are this

The sources here are from Tokyo, Kyoto and Nara. I found no official description of sweet shops elsewhere. One small sign of how the sweet-shop menu travels: Seven-Eleven began selling a milk zenzai pudding, credited to the Izumo Zenzai Society, from 9 October 2026, in Tottori, Shimane, Hiroshima and Yamaguchi only. Who the society is, and how it relates to the Izumo origin story, I could not confirm.

## The sweet shop away from Kyoto, Nara and Ginza

I found no official source comparing zenzai, anmitsu, kuzukiri or warabi-mochi in their home places with Tokyo or anywhere else. What can be said:

- **The name changes between Kyoto–Osaka and Tokyo**, as above. That is a difference of meaning, not quality.
- **Kuzu is tied to Yoshino’s winter.** The ministry links its quality to the cold and the water there.
- **Warabi-mochi is sold differently.** In Nara, the farm ministry says, it is mostly sold at the prefecture’s confectioners, and it is popular at department stores elsewhere. Warabi-mochi sold from stalls I could not confirm from any official source.

## What people get wrong

- **"Zenzai and oshiruko are just two names for one thing."** Zenzai itself means different things in Kyoto–Osaka and Tokyo.
- **"Anmitsu was definitely invented in Ginza."** That is what a tourism association and a newspaper say; I could not read the shop’s own account or find another.
- **"You can still have anmitsu at the original shop."** Ginza Wakamatsu closed in December 2023.
- **"Kuzu and warabi-mochi are the same thing."** They are made from different starches — kuzu root and bracken root.
