Sōmen is a fine wheat noodle, served in summer on ice with a cold dipping sauce, or in winter in hot broth as *nyūmen*. The best of it is still stretched by hand, and the paradox of the trade is that this summer food is made in the coldest months and improves with age. This page explains the standard, the making, and how Japan cooks it. The lineage pages cover the two great producing areas, Miwa in Nara and Banshū in Hyōgo.

## What it is

**The national standard.** Japan's agricultural standard for hand-stretched dried noodles (*tenobe hoshi-men*) defines them as wheat flour kneaded with salt water, coated with edible vegetable oil or starch, stretched while being twisted into noodles, and dried. Every act of stretching, from the first pull (*kobiki*) to hanging the noodles on frames (*kadoboshi*), must be done by hand. Both the dough and the stretched strands must rest for set periods — the dough so that moisture evens out, the strands so that the gluten structure distorted by stretching recovers. The version I read is the 2019 standard as shown in a 2024 revision document; I have not confirmed how the revision took effect, or the resting times, which the document omits.

**The oil.** The Miwa cooperative says a little vegetable oil is used, so the noodles may smell slightly of it, but boiling and rinsing removes it.

I could not confirm the official thickness boundaries between sōmen and other noodles, so I do not give them.

## Why it is made in winter

The farm ministry says Miwa sōmen is made from December to the end of March, in two days — day one, dough kneaded with salt water is oiled and stretched thin; day two, it is dried and cut — because the Nara basin is cold and dry then. Hyōgo's Ibo no Ito is made from October to April. In Nara the industry spread to mountain villages from the late 1960s as winter work, the GI document says.

**Resting and ageing.** Miwa inserts a resting stage it calls *umashi* many times, which drives off moisture and gives a smooth noodle that does not go soft quickly. Ibo no Ito builds gluten by alternating rest with rope-like twisting and stretching. Both sell *hine*: noodles aged a year. Ibo no Ito says aged noodles improve through the hot, humid rainy season as enzymes in the flour act, a change it calls *yaku*; the Miwa cooperative says aged noodles are generally prized for firmness and smoothness, while new ones taste more of wheat.

## How to cook and eat it

- **Boiling.** Hyōgo's cooperative: about a litre of water to two bundles (100 g), 1 minute 30 seconds to 2 minutes, drain, then rub under running water. For nyūmen or stir-frying, boil 1 minute to 1 minute 30, because it will be heated again.
- **Cold sauce.** The Miwa cooperative's ratio: dashi 2, light soy ½, mirin ½, brought to the boil and cooled.
- **Nyūmen.** Dashi 5, light soy ½, mirin ⅓, with thin omelette, mitsuba and seasoned shiitake.
- **Other ways.** The farm ministry lists chilled, hot, stir-fried, peperoncino, thick-sauce and curry nyūmen; Ibo no Ito adds salads and dipping noodles.
- **Storage.** It absorbs smells; keep it away from strong ones and out of the fridge, or sealed if it must go in.

**Nyūmen Day** is 5 February, set by the national dried-noodle federation to encourage hot sōmen in winter.

## Sōmen and Tanabata

Two accounts, side by side:

- A Setagaya ward food leaflet (drawing on the farm ministry): sōmen is eaten at Tanabata likened to the Milky Way or the weaver princess's thread, and its forerunner *sakubei* was offered at Tanabata rites in the Heian period.
- The Miwa cooperative: from the Heian period sōmen, called *sakubei*, was prized at court rites and banquets, and in the Muromachi period court ladies called it *ozoro* and served it at Tanabata. It gives no sources.

## Summer food, winter work

No official source compares sōmen eaten at Miwa or Tatsuno with sōmen bought in Tokyo, so this page does not. What the sources show is a calendar: made in the cold, aged through summer, eaten in summer — or hot in February.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sōmen is a summer product."** It is made in winter and the best is aged a year.
- **"Machine-made and hand-stretched are the same."** The standard requires all stretching by hand.
- **"Sōmen is only eaten cold."** Nyūmen has its own day, in February.
