*Gibier* — *jibie* in Japanese — is the farm ministry’s word for the meat of wild birds and animals eaten as food. Its pages, and this one, are about deer and wild boar. The farm ministry explains why there is more of it: deer and boar damage crops across the country, more are being caught, and using the meat as food has spread nationwide. Almost everything official written about it concerns safety. This page sets out those rules, then the boar hotpots that have their own history.

For Japan’s other meats in general, start from the [meat overview](/en/eat/meat/).

## Where it came from

**A crop problem first.** The farm ministry’s page for consumers starts from damage, not cuisine. Crop damage by deer and boar has become a problem in many areas; as more animals are caught, the move to use their meat has spread across Japan.

**Not new to the table.** The farm ministry’s page on Kagoshima’s pork miso soup notes that the Satsuma domain ate pork, wild boar and deer in the Edo period.

**Boar in miso.** The best-documented wild-meat dish is *botan nabe*, wild boar simmered in miso, which Tamba-Sasayama in Hyōgo calls its own. Its tourism site dates it to an army regiment stationed there in 1908 and the name to a 1931 song. The full story is on the [botan nabe page](/en/eat/nabe-botan/).

## What makes it gibier

**It is wild.** The health ministry’s web magazine puts the difference simply. Unlike livestock, wild animals have no managed feed or health, so nobody knows what pathogens they carry. That one fact drives every rule below.

**It must come from a licensed facility.** Processing wild animals for food as a business falls under the Food Sanitation Act. Restaurants and shops must buy meat butchered at a facility that holds a licence for meat processing, the health ministry says. It has published hygiene guidelines for businesses, last revised on 26 June 2023, with a colour atlas revised on 19 March 2025.

**Some facilities are certified.** The farm ministry runs a national certification for game-processing facilities. It certifies those that follow hygiene standards, distribute meat to a set cutting chart, and label it so that it can be traced. When checked, its list ran from No. 001 to No. 043, the latest certified on 4 March 2026, with some numbers missing. Which animals the scheme covers, and the detail of its standards, are not on that page.

## How to eat it

**Cooked through.** The farm ministry warns that eating raw or undercooked wild deer or boar carries a risk of food poisoning from hepatitis E virus, enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli* and parasites. Its advice is to heat the meat until it is cooked to the centre, and to disinfect cooking utensils that touched it raw.

**The figure.** The health ministry’s web magazine gives the official wording: heat to 75°C at the centre for one minute or more. It calls eating game raw or underdone "very dangerous".

**Freezing does not make it safe.** Frozen game must still be cooked right through, the health ministry says, because the trichinella worm is resistant to freezing.

**The main pathogens**, in the health ministry’s list:

- hepatitis E virus, with an incubation period of 15 to 50 days;
- enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli*;
- trichinella, which in Japan has been reported from bear meat.

The health ministry adds that hepatitis E in pregnant women tends to become severe, with a fatality rate that can reach 20 percent.

## Not all raw meat is this

Japan does eat one meat raw as a regional dish: horse. On the [basashi page](/en/eat/meat-basashi/), the farm ministry describes Kumamoto horse butchered in hygiene-controlled plants and always frozen before distribution. Horse is livestock. Deer and boar are wild, and the official sources say the opposite for them — never raw, and frozen is not enough.

## Gibier away from the hills

**Boar hotpots are tied to places.** Botan nabe belongs to Tamba-Sasayama. Its tourism site describes wild boar, a blend of red and white miso that differs from shop to shop, and hand-cut meat at some shops. In Mie, the prefecture’s tourism federation describes *sōhei-nabe* at Yunoyama Onsen: a hotpot the warrior monks of the temple there are said to have eaten for stamina, with mountain food and meats such as boar simmered in several kinds of miso, served at inns and elsewhere in the town. It gives the route as a 10-minute Sanco bus ride from Yunoyama Onsen Station on the Kintetsu Yunoyama Line. That dish is also on [Kinki’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/).

**The rule travels.** Whether in a mountain town or a city, the national rules are the same: meat from licensed facilities, cooked to 75°C at the centre for a minute or more.

No official source checked for this page describes regional deer dishes, gives a figure for how much gibier is eaten, compares eating game where it is hunted with eating it in Tokyo, or gives a tax-included price, so none of these is stated. Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Game can be served pink, like beef."** The health ministry’s figure is 75°C at the centre for at least a minute.
- **"Freezing makes it safe."** Trichinella survives freezing.
- **"If horse can be eaten raw, so can deer."** Horse is livestock under its own controls; wild deer and boar are not.
- **"Any hunter’s meat can be sold."** Shops and restaurants must buy from licensed meat-processing facilities.
- **"Botan nabe is named after the peony plating."** The Tamba-Sasayama tourism site says the name came first.
