*Toriten* — "chicken tempura" — is chicken cut into bite-sized pieces and fried in tempura batter, and it belongs to Oita prefecture. The farm ministry lists it among Oita's local dishes and says it is eaten across the whole prefecture. It has a named restaurant in Beppu. What it does not have is one agreed date: the farm ministry and the Beppu tourism association put its start in different eras, and this page gives both.

For chicken in Japan generally — what *jidori* means and how the parts are named — start from the [chicken overview](/en/eat/chicken/).

## Where it came from

**The restaurant.** The farm ministry says toriten is held to have begun at Tōyōken, in Beppu, which it describes as the first restaurant in Oita prefecture. The Beppu tourism association also traces it to the prefecture's first restaurant, in Beppu.

**The date.** Here the two sources part.

- **Early Shōwa.** The farm ministry places the start in the early Shōwa era, which began in 1926.
- **Taishō.** The tourism association says it is held to have begun in the Taishō era (1912–26).

Neither source gives a year, and this site does not choose between them.

**The reason.** The ministry gives two accounts, side by side.

- **For diners who found bones awkward.** At Tōyōken, bone-in karaage was thought hard for women to eat, so boneless thigh was cut into bite-sized pieces and finished like tempura.
- **To make the chicken go further.** In a time when chicken was expensive, it was coated in plenty of batter to bulk it out.

**A third view.** Oita prefecture's web magazine, in a feature on Nakatsu karaage, mentions the view that toriten began as a variation on karaage.

## What makes it toriten

**Batter, not a dusting.** The Japan Karaage Association defines karaage as frying in oil after a light dusting of wheat flour or katakuri-ko starch. The farm ministry describes toriten as chicken fried in tempura batter. Set side by side, the difference between the two is the coating. That comparison is this site's; neither source draws it.

**Boneless thigh.** The ministry's account of Tōyōken is specific about the cut: thigh (*momo*), with the bone taken out, in bite-sized pieces.

**Dipped, not plain.** The ministry says toriten is eaten with vinegared soy sauce and *neri-karashi* (mustard paste), or with ponzu, or with *kabosu* citrus. Karaage in Nakatsu, by contrast, is seasoned in the marinade so that, in one shop owner's words, it needs nothing added.

## Oita and chicken

**The household figure.** The farm ministry says that in a 2019 government survey of annual chicken consumption per household, covering prefectural capitals and designated cities, Oita city came first in Japan. Karaage is the famous dish, it adds, and toriten is loved across the prefecture alongside it.

**More than one chicken dish.** Oita's entry in the farm ministry's list of local dishes has 30 items. Toriten is one; *torimeshi* (chicken rice) and *torijiru* (chicken soup) are others. The ministry's Oita story also describes households keeping chickens and killing one for guests or celebrations — a description that appears for Miyazaki and Kagoshima too.

**Soul food.** The ministry says Oita city treats toriten as its "soul food" and hands out maps of the shops that serve it.

## Toriten and the hot springs

Toriten is traced to Beppu, a hot-spring town, but neither the farm ministry nor the tourism association links it to the water. It is a Beppu dish by its restaurant, not by its springs. The ministry's Oita story heads its section on food with the phrase "the flavours of the onsen prefecture", but does not tie toriten to bathing.

Two Beppu foods do have a stated link to the town. Beppu city describes *jigoku-mushi*, food steamed over the strong vents of the hot springs — eggs, vegetables, rice — as part of staying in Kannawa. And the tourism association says Beppu *reimen*, cold noodles, took shape after the Second World War, when people returning from Manchuria settled in Beppu. Neither is in the farm ministry's list of Oita dishes; toriten is. For the baths themselves, see [Beppu](/en/soak/beppu/).

## The shops, and what they charge

The farm ministry says toriten is served everywhere from places for local people to places for tourists. None of the sources for this page gives a tax-included price for toriten, so none is given here.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Not all of Oita's fried chicken is this

- **Karaage (Nakatsu and Usa).** The other half of Oita's chicken. The Japan Karaage Association names Usa as the birthplace of the karaage specialist shop and Nakatsu as the town where the shops multiplied. Marinated, dusted, fried to order. See [karaage](/en/eat/chicken-karaage/).
- **Mizutaki (Fukuoka).** Chicken again, but simmered in a pot, not fried.
- **Other Kyushu dishes.** Oita's other local dishes, and those of its neighbours, are on the [Kyushu local food page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/).

## Toriten away from Oita

The farm ministry describes toriten as eaten across Oita prefecture; it does not say how widely it is served elsewhere. No source checked for this page compares toriten in Beppu with toriten outside Oita, in taste, method or price, and this page makes no such comparison. If a menu elsewhere offers toriten, the ministry's description is the one to measure it by: boneless thigh, tempura batter, and something to dip it in.

## What people get wrong

- **"Toriten is just karaage."** Karaage is dusted with flour or starch; toriten is fried in tempura batter.
- **"It is a hot-spring food."** It is traced to a Beppu restaurant. No source links it to the springs.
- **"It has a known start date."** The farm ministry says early Shōwa; the tourism association says Taishō.
- **"It is a Beppu dish only."** The ministry says it is eaten across the whole prefecture.
- **"It is eaten plain."** The ministry lists vinegared soy with mustard, ponzu or kabosu.
