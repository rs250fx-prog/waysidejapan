Soy sauce is made all over Japan, but a handful of towns carry its history: Yuasa in Wakayama, which calls itself the place of "the first drop"; Noda and Chōshi in Chiba, which supplied Edo; Tatsuno in Hyōgo, linked to light soy; and the island of Shōdoshima, with its wooden barrels. This page is about where soy sauce came from, how the regions differ, and how to visit a brewery.

For seasonings in general, and how soy sauce is brewed, start from the seasoning overview.

## Where it came from

The official accounts share an outline and disagree on details. This site gives them side by side and does not choose.

**The common ground.** The Japan Soy Sauce Association says soy sauce goes back to an ancient Chinese fermented paste, *jiang* (*hishio*), and that when it reached Japan is not clear. It and the farm ministry both say the Taihō Code of 701 records *hishio* made from soybeans at a court office.

**The monk and the miso.** Both say a Zen monk, Kakushin, brought the method for *kinzanji* miso back from China, and that the liquid gathering in its tubs is said to have been close to today’s tamari; the association dates this to 1254. They call him a monk of Shinshū. Yamasa, a soy company, calls him a monk of Kōkokuji in Yura, Kishū, and says, as "it is said", that something like tamari came about when Yuasa villagers got the recipe wrong. Yuasa’s Japan Heritage page, recognised in 2017, also keeps the form of a tradition: soy sauce is said to have been developed by Yuasa’s people from the liquid in a monk’s miso tubs.

**When it became soy sauce.** The association says something close to it was made by the mid-Muromachi period; the ministry says the late Muromachi. The association cites records of 1487 and 1559, and both it and the ministry say the word *shōyu* in its present characters first appears in 1597. Kikkoman’s history page, a company source, finds the word in a temple diary in 1568.

**From Kansai to Edo.** Kikkoman says production centres formed in Kinki — Sakai, Yuasa, Tatsuno — and that after 1603 soy was shipped from there to Edo. The association says dark soy production began in Kantō in 1640, and that Maruo Magoemon of Kansai developed light soy in 1666. By the 1800s, Kikkoman says, Kantō soy had pushed out the western shipments; an Edo ranking of 1840 put Noda and Chōshi on top. The farm ministry says soba sauce, eel sauce, the soy for nigiri sushi and *tsukudani* were Edo flavours born of the dark, salty soy Edo liked.

## What makes each region’s soy sauce

The five types (see the overview) do not spread evenly. Shares are of 2024 shipments, by where the factory is; the percentages of each type are my calculation.

- **Dark koikuchi — Kantō.** 84.9% nationally; Chiba ships about 41% of it. Kikkoman says Kantō’s dark soy is mostly fully brewed, without added sweeteners, and grew along the Tone river system from the mid-Edo period.
- **Light usukuchi — Kansai.** 11.5%; Hyōgo ships about 39% of it. Osaka prefecture’s column says most light soy is still used in Osaka, Kyoto and Hyōgo. The association says it uses about 10% more salt than dark soy to slow fermentation, and *amazake* made from rice.
- **Tamari — Chūbu.** 2.0%; Aichi ships about 57%. Made from miso-ball koji, matured nearly a year. Kikkoman says it goes into the sauce for *hitsumabushi* and the broth of *kishimen* and *Ise udon*.
- **Saishikomi.** 1.0%. The association says it was born around Yanai, Yamaguchi, and was made mostly from San’in to northern Kyūshū — but Gunma was the top shipper in 2024, with about 20%. Where a type was born and where it is shipped from are different things.
- **White shiro — Aichi.** 0.6%. Born in Hekinan; Aichi still ships the most. Kikkoman says it is used in clear soups and *chawanmushi*.
- **Sweet mixed soy — Kyūshū.** Kikkoman says it is common in Kyūshū and in Kōchi and Ehime, and is said to have spread in the 1955–64 period.

## The towns

**Noda, Chiba.** Noda city says the Iida family is told to have begun making tamari in the Eiroku era (1558–1570), the town’s oldest brewing, and that soy as a product is "thought" to date from 1661, when Takanashi Hyōzaemon began. In the 18th century Noda’s output replaced Kansai soy in Edo; in 1917 eight Mogi and Takanashi families merged into Noda Shōyu, the forerunner of Kikkoman. Kikkoman says raw materials and finished soy moved by river. The association’s 1640 date is a third account.

**Chōshi, Chiba.** Yamasa says its founder, Hamaguchi Gihei, came from Hiro village next to Yuasa and founded the company in 1645, and that Chōshi’s climate was much like Kishū’s. Yuasa’s town history says Yuasa merchants who went to the Kantō started brewing there, the root of Chōshi’s industry. Higeta says it marked its 400th anniversary in 2016; the pages I read give no founding year or founder.

**Tatsuno, Hyōgo.** Osaka prefecture’s column says light soy was born here in the mid-17th century, was carried by river boat to Osaka and Kyoto, and, with Hokkaidō kombu arriving at about the same time, helped build Osaka’s dashi culture (the dashi overview has more). Kikkoman lists local wheat, soybeans from Sayō, salt from Akō, water from the Ibo river, and sake-making skills. The association’s 1666 date names no place, so I do not tie it to Tatsuno.

**Shōdoshima, Kagawa.** The town calls the island a soy centre with 400 years of history and one of Japan’s four great soy regions, and gives three reasons: island salt, soybeans and wheat by sea, and a mild climate that suits koji. In the *Hishio no Sato* district many brewing sheds are registered cultural properties and still in use.

**Yuasa, Wakayama.** The town calls itself the home of soy sauce as a product, starting from *kinzanji* miso. In 2006 its brewing quarter became Japan’s first soy-brewing town chosen as a preservation district for traditional buildings, about 6.3 ha.

## Barrels and tanks

Shōdoshima town says its cedar barrels (*koga*) are about 2.2 m across and hold about 5,800 litres, and last 50 to over 100 years. Tanks, it says, finish soy in four to eight months; its barrel-brewed dark soy takes a year and saishikomi two. The association gives about six to eight months for fully brewed soy. I give both ranges. A brewer in Arida-gawa, Wakayama, quoted by the farm ministry’s Kinki office, said barrel brewing is "only 1%" of Japan’s soy sauce — his statement, not a statistic.

## How to visit

The association says you arrange visits with each factory, mostly by phone. From its lists:

- **Noda and Chōshi.** Kikkoman’s *Monoshiri Shōyu-kan* in Noda (booking required; web booking needs Kikkoman’s membership registration). Yamasa in Chōshi (booking required). Higeta’s Chōshi factory (groups of ten or more book; individuals need not).
- **Tatsuno and Yuasa.** Higashimaru and Nihon Maruten in Tatsuno, weekdays, booking required; Kadochō in Yuasa, booking required.
- **Shōdoshima.** Seven factories, all needing booking except Yamaroku, which is free and asks you not to eat natto beforehand.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Not all of Japan’s soy sauce is this

The association’s Q&A says eastern Japan developed fragrant dark soy, which suits the smell of red-fleshed fish, Kansai light soy for simmered dishes, and Kyūshū sweet soy. Kikkoman adds that Shōdoshima makes unsweetened dark soy like Kantō, while Sanuki udon broth uses mixed light soy. I found no official source explaining *why* each region took its soy.

## Soy sauce away from the brewery

**Production is concentrated; buying is not.** In 2024 Chiba shipped 37.6% of Japan’s soy sauce, Hyōgo 15.7%, Tokyo 350 kL. In the household survey’s 2023–25 city ranking, Yamagata bought the most (5,356 ml a household), and Chiba city, at 3,870 ml, was below the national 3,915 ml; Tokyo’s wards were 42nd and Osaka 48th. The survey counts home purchases only, does not split dark from light, and gives no reasons.

**What a brewery can offer.** Barrel-brewed soy and regional types such as saishikomi or tamari can be among what a brewery sells. I could not find an official comparison of brewery and supermarket soy, and the sources do not say one is better.

## What people get wrong

- **"Soy sauce has one birthplace and one date."** The accounts give 1254, 1487, 1559, 1568 and 1597 for different things, and Noda alone has three dates.
- **"Saishikomi comes from Gunma."** It was born around Yanai; Gunma ships the most today.
- **"Chiba buys the most soy sauce."** It makes the most.
- **"Brewery soy is barrel-brewed."** Most soy sauce is made in tanks.
