faq:
  - q: Are wanko soba and gyūtan on the farm ministry's list?
    a: No. Neither is in the farm ministry's list for Iwate or Miyagi, and nor are Morioka reimen, Morioka jajamen, Kitakata ramen or Yonezawa beef. Several of them are Cultural Agency 100-year foods instead. The two lists are chosen separately, so a dish can be on one and not the other.
  - q: Is imoni the same in Miyagi and Yamagata?
    a: No. The farm ministry says inland Yamagata makes it with beef and soy sauce, and Shōnai on the Japan Sea side with pork and miso. Miyagi's prefectural sites describe Miyagi imoni as pork with Sendai miso. So pork and miso is found in Shōnai as well as Miyagi. This page does not say which came first.
  - q: Which Tohoku hot spring towns have a dish tied to them?
    a: Four, where the same official page names both the spa and the food. Zao Onsen and Genghis Khan lamb, and Iizaka Onsen and radium tamago, from the Cultural Agency. Ginzan Onsen and its curry bread, from Yamagata's tourism site. Asamushi Onsen and kujira-mochi, from Aomori's tourism site.
  - q: When was kozuyu first eaten?
    a: The farm ministry says it has been eaten for more than 100 years and does not give a starting point. The Cultural Agency traces its root to the late Edo period, to a dish called jū eaten on the Aizu lord Matsudaira Katataka's journeys to Edo. This page gives both.

This page takes five prefectures of Tohoku, in the north-east of Honshu: Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Yamagata and Fukushima. Akita has its own page, [Akita's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-akita/), and is not repeated here. Several of the official descriptions come back to the same things: cold summers that failed the rice, dough torn by hand into soup, and salmon from the rivers in autumn. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Aomori

**Senbei-jiru in Hachinohe.** Soup with wheat crackers in it. The farm ministry says Nanbu *senbei*, wheat flour, salt and water baked in iron moulds, belong to the old Nanbu domain (south-east Aomori to northern Iwate), where cold summers often meant no rice; the crackers were a precious keeping food, and putting them in soup gave senbei-jiru. The ministry says there are several theories of the origin and gives one example: before the war, crackers went into a soup of *ugui*, a river fish. It says the name only settled in the Heisei era, when Hachinohe's tourism bodies promoted it. The Cultural Agency, which made it a 100-year food in fiscal 2022 (Reiwa 4, traditional category), says it has been eaten in the Hachinohe area for more than 200 years.

**Ichigo-ni.** A clear soup of sea urchin and abalone from the coast at Hachinohe and Hashikami, said to have begun with fishermen boiling their catch on the beach. The name comes from the urchin in the milky soup, which looked like wild strawberries (*ichigo*) in morning mist. Once everyday food, it is now for Obon, New Year and celebrations.

**Kaiyaki miso.** From Shimokita and Tsugaru, said to date from the Edo period: miso and fish grilled in a scallop shell about 20 cm across, later bound with egg. Eggs were then precious, the ministry says, so this was a special taste for the sick and expectant mothers.

**Hittsumi.** Dough pinched off by hand into soup, from the Nanbu area, where flour and millet dishes took root. The ministry says that in Hachinohe it is eaten at home about as often as senbei-jiru.

### Iwate

**Hittsumi.** Eaten across the prefecture. Cold summers sometimes ruined the rice of the Kitakami basin, and a culture of kneaded flour food, *shitonemono*, grew up; hittsumi, eaten in place of rice in bad years, is its best-known dish. The name is said to come from *hittsumamu*, dialect for tearing off by hand. Iwate's raw-noodle cooperative made 3 December "Hittsumi Day".

**Mamebu-jiru.** From Yamagata-chō in Kuji: a soy sauce soup with vegetables, grilled tofu and wheat dumplings with walnut inside. The ministry says it is thought that, in the bad harvests of the Edo period, dumplings stood in for noodles at celebrations. Two explanations for the name:

- **Size**: the dumplings are "bean-sized", or look like round wheat gluten.
- **A wish**: to live *mamemameshiku*, healthily and diligently.

This page gives both. Dumplings are made larger for happy occasions and smaller for funerals, it is said. It became a 100-year food in fiscal 2021.

**Morioka's three noodles.** Wanko soba, Morioka jajamen and Morioka reimen are not in the farm ministry's list. Together they are a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, "future" category). The agency says Morioka came first among prefectural capitals for spending on Chinese-style noodles in the national household survey (2009–2011 average), and the three grew into a regional brand once the media took them up. I found no official account of where jajamen or reimen began. Iwate's tourism site says that some 400 years ago the Nanbu lord Toshinao, stopping in Hanamaki on his way to Edo, was served soba in a bowl and asked for more again and again; Hanamaki's wanko soba is served without the chanting. For the dish, see [wanko soba](/en/eat/soba-wanko/).

### Miyagi

**Zunda-mochi.** Rice cake in crushed green soybean paste. The ministry says Miyagi eats rice cake at New Year, weddings, memorial services and funerals, and zunda-mochi is often served to guests. Three explanations for the name:

- **A farmer called Jinta** made it.
- **Date Masamune** crushed the beans with the hilt of his campaign sword (*jin-tachi*).
- **The sound of pounding beans**.

This page gives all three.

**Harako-meshi in Watari.** Rice topped with salmon and its roe (*harako*), eaten from September to November. It is known for being offered to Date Masamune when he inspected canal works at Arahama, but before that it was fishermen's food, made with salmon netted from the Abukuma river. A 100-year food since fiscal 2021.

**Sendai zōni.** From the late Edo period: a New Year soup with a large grilled goby, salmon roe and Sendai *seri*. Poor catches have made the gobies scarce and dear.

**Hatto-jiru.** Wheat dough in soup, from the north of the prefecture. In Tome, the ministry says, farmers had to sell the domain even the rice left after tax, so they kneaded wheat flour instead.

**Gyūtan-yaki.** Grilled beef tongue is not in the farm ministry's list. It is a 100-year food (fiscal 2025, future). The agency says a Sendai restaurant owner created it by trial and error in the Shōwa 20s (1945–1954), and that Sendai cuts it about 1 to 2 cm thick, against 2 to 3 mm in an ordinary *yakiniku* restaurant.

### Yamagata

**Imoni.** A taro stew eaten across the prefecture, which the ministry says may go back to the mid-1600s. At the end of the Mogami river boat route, near Nagasaki in Nakayama, boatmen waiting for consignees passed the time round a pot on the riverbank, cooking local taro with the dried cod they carried. That is given as the root. Beef came in around the start of the Shōwa era. The Cultural Agency (fiscal 2022, traditional) says a riverbank is "Yamagata style" and that the styles can turn into an "imoni debate".

**Tama-konnyaku.** Balls of konnyaku about 3 cm across, simmered in soy sauce, eaten with mustard. The ministry says the ball form is particular to Yamagata. Tradition holds that Ennin, who founded Yamadera, used konnyaku brought from China in the temple's vegetarian food.

**Dashi.** Chopped summer vegetables dressed with soy sauce, from Murayama and Okitama. Three explanations for the name: it brings out other foods like stock (*dashi*); the vegetables are cut fine and "put out"; it is quickly "put out" on the table. This page gives all three.

**Cold meat soba.** Not in the farm ministry's list. A 100-year food (fiscal 2023, modern category): cold soba in a hen-stock soy broth, topped with the hen's meat. The agency gives the local account that before the war, in Kahoku, a customer poured leftover simmered horse meat over soba, and that horse later gave way to the chickens households kept.

Yonezawa beef is not in the farm ministry's list either; see [Yonezawa wagyu](/en/eat/wagyu-yonezawa/).

### Fukushima

**Kozuyu.** From Aizu: wood ear, bracken, taro and more in dried-scallop stock, served in shallow lacquered *teshio-zara*. The name is said to be a corruption of *kojū no tsuyu*. It was a must at weddings, and has seven or nine ingredients, odd numbers being lucky. The sources give different depths:

- **The farm ministry**: eaten for more than 100 years.
- **The Cultural Agency** (fiscal 2021, traditional): rooted in the late Edo period, in a dish called *jū* eaten on the Aizu lord Matsudaira Katataka's journeys to Edo, combining local produce with sea foods brought by Kitamae ships.

This page gives both.

**Nishin no sanshō-zuke.** Dried herring pickled under sanshō leaves in vinegar, sake and soy sauce. In the Edo period, the ministry says, almost no fresh fish reached Fukushima, and dried Hokkaido herring carried to Aizu was protein that kept. It has its own dish, the *nishin-bachi* of Aizu Hongō ware.

**Enban gyōza.** Not in the farm ministry's list. A 100-year food (fiscal 2021, future): the agency says it began after the war when someone who had learned gyōza in Manchuria sold them at a stall, and a plate holds 20 to 30. Kitakata ramen is also a 100-year food and not in the farm ministry's list; see [Kitakata ramen](/en/eat/ramen-kitakata/).

## What makes it Tohoku

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Aomori 29, Iwate 30, Miyagi 29, Yamagata 29, Fukushima 30 — 147 in all. Among them:

- **Aomori**: *ke-no-shiru*, senbei-jiru, kaiyaki miso, ichigo-ni, hittsumi, squid *menchi*.
- **Iwate**: hittsumi, mamebu-jiru, *gandzuki*, *kurumi-zōni*.
- **Miyagi**: Sendai zōni, harako-meshi, zunda-mochi, hatto-jiru, *aburafu-don*.
- **Yamagata**: dashi, imoni, tama-konnyaku, *donga-jiru* of winter cod, *sasamaki*.
- **Fukushima**: *ika-ninjin*, kozuyu, nishin no sanshō-zuke, harako-meshi, basashi.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them on each prefecture's page: Aomori 4, Iwate 9, Miyagi 9, Yamagata 7, Fukushima 20 — 49 in all.

**What recurs.** Hittsumi is on the Aomori and Iwate lists; Miyagi has *hatto* and Fukushima *hattō*. Harako-meshi and *hokki-meshi* are on both the Miyagi and Fukushima lists, and *donko-jiru* on Iwate, Miyagi and Fukushima. Neither harako-meshi page says which came first.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: wanko soba, Morioka jajamen and reimen (Iwate); gyūtan and Miyagi-style imoni (Miyagi); cold meat soba and Yonezawa beef (Yamagata); Kitakata ramen and enban gyōza (Fukushima). That is all this says. Most are 100-year foods instead.

## Why the north

I found no public source that describes the food of Tohoku as a whole. What follows sets the prefectures' own descriptions side by side.

**Cold summers and flour.** The ministry's Aomori story says Nanbu takes the *yamase*, the cold summer wind, head on; rice did badly and flour and millet took root. Tsugaru, sheltered by mountains, grows rice; Shimokita grows potatoes. It quotes a researcher who divides Aomori into rice, flour and potato cultures. Iwate's story says the history of rice there is a fight with cold summers.

**Keeping food.** Iwate's snowy mountains gave salted and dried mountain vegetables and freeze-dried daikon and tofu. Fukushima makes freeze-dried rice cake and tofu. Yamagata dries and salts mountain vegetables.

**Rice cake.** Miyagi's story says the prefecture is said to have more than 50 kinds of mochi dish. The mochi culture of Ichinoseki and Hiraizumi in Iwate is a 100-year food.

**The Kitamae ships.** Sakata in Yamagata was a Kitamae port, and Aizu's kozuyu and herring were built on dried sea food from Hokkaido.

I found no public source that calls Tohoku food salty or sweet, or that describes a "hot-spring cure food" here, so I say neither.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Senbei-jiru**: about 200 restaurants in Hachinohe.
- **Ichigo-ni**: a festival in Hashikami every July; tinned since 1980.
- **Mamebu**: more restaurants in Kuji now serve it.
- **Kozuyu**: inns and restaurants in the prefecture; pouches and kits.
- **Tama-konnyaku**: festivals, sightseeing spots, the approach to Yamadera.
- **Harako-meshi**: restaurants at Arahama, September to November.
- **Imoni**: a riverbank festival in Yamagata city in mid-September, with a pot 6.5 m across.

**Prices.** The only official tax-inclusive prices I found are for soba in Obanazawa, Yamagata: the city's page lists shops on its soba road at ¥700 to ¥1,500, tax included (checked 11 October 2026). The page gives no update date, so they may not be current. I found none for the dishes above. For the noodle, see [soba](/en/eat/soba/).

## How it is eaten

- **Senbei-jiru**: chicken stock and soy sauce most often.
- **Hittsumi in Iwate**: mostly soy sauce, with sweet *azuki* and *zunda* versions.
- **Imoni**: finished with udon or curry roux in some homes.

**When.** Ichigo-ni urchin around July. Harako-meshi September to November. Fukushima's hokki-meshi June to November. Imoni in autumn and winter; the *imoni-kai* party is a fixed event of the year. Dashi in summer. Herring in sanshō in spring and early summer.

## Not all of Tohoku is this

**Imoni across the border.** The farm ministry: inland Yamagata uses beef and soy sauce, Shōnai on the Japan Sea side pork and miso. Miyagi's tourism federation and produce site call pork and Sendai miso "Miyagi imoni". So pork and miso belongs to Shōnai as much as to Miyagi. This page does not say which came first.

**Two kujira-mochi.** Yamagata's list has *kujira-mochi*, a rice-cake sweet with no whale in it. Asamushi's kujira-mochi, written differently, is a separate sweet (see below).

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Zao Onsen and Genghis Khan lamb.** Zao Onsen jingisukan is a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2022, future), not in the farm ministry's Yamagata list. The agency's page says that after the war, synthetic fibres crashed wool prices and farmers who kept sheep needed help. Iron pans modelled on a Mongolian helmet grill were made at a Yamagata foundry, and the dish was devised as a speciality for Zao Onsen, chosen first in the mountain section of a "100 new tourist sites" list in 1949. It used fresh meat from the start. Six shops in Zao Onsen serve it today, the page says. For the baths, see [Zao Onsen](/en/soak/zao/); for the dish, [jingisukan](/en/eat/meat-jingisukan/).

**[Iizaka Onsen](/en/soak/iizaka/) and radium tamago.** A Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2022, modern), not in the farm ministry's Fukushima list. The agency calls it a hot-spring egg from Iizaka, where it says radium was first confirmed in Japan. Iizaka's inns have always served it at breakfast, and Fukushima people say "radium tamago", not *onsen tamago*.

**Ginzan Onsen and curry bread.** Yamagata's tourism site names *Haikara-san no karī-pan* as a Ginzan speciality, sold at Meiyūan and Taishō Roman-kan. It is not in the farm ministry's list. [Ginzan](/en/soak/ginzan/) is about 40 minutes by bus from Ōishida station on the Yamagata shinkansen, by Yamagata's tourism site; the environment ministry's plan for Ginzan says about 30 minutes.

**[Asamushi Onsen](/en/soak/asamushi/) and kujira-mochi.** Aomori's tourism site names kujira-mochi as an Asamushi speciality: two long-established shops steam it from rice flour with bean paste and sugar. From Aomori station the Aoimori Railway takes about 20 to 30 minutes, depending on the source: about 25 minutes says the Asamushi tourism association, while Aomori's tourism site gives 30 minutes in a feature and about 20 on its spot page.

**No link found.** Miyagi's story calls [Naruko](/en/soak/naruko/) a famous spring but ties it to no dish. Official pages do not tie [Hanamaki Onsen](/en/soak/hanamaki/) to wanko soba, Tsunagi Onsen to Morioka's noodles, or [Higashiyama Onsen](/en/soak/higashiyama/) to kozuyu. For the region's springs, see [onsen in Honshu](/en/soak/onsen-honshu/).

**Getting there.** Official tourism sites; check times before you travel.

- **Sendai**: about 1 hour 30 minutes from Tokyo by shinkansen, not counting changes (Miyagi's figures, as of December 2023).
- **Morioka**: Iwate's site gives 2 hours 10 minutes from Tokyo; Morioka's convention bureau gives about 2 hours 30 minutes by *Hayate* and 3 hours 10 minutes by *Yamabiko*. The two differ.
- **Yamagata**: about 2 hours 35 minutes from Tokyo by Yamagata shinkansen, as I read the prefecture's diagram; about 1 hour 5 minutes by air from Haneda, then about 35 minutes by shuttle bus.
- **Aomori**: about 1 hour 20 minutes by air from Haneda; the shinkansen runs direct to Hachinohe and Shin-Aomori, but the site gives no time.
- **Fukushima and Aizu**: I could not read clear official times, so I give none.

## Tohoku food away from Tohoku

- **Aomori.** The prefecture announced that Aomori Hokusaikan's Tokyo shop closed on 31 July 2025. I could not confirm a current permanent Aomori shop in Tokyo.
- **Iwate.** Iwate Ginga Plaza, 5-15-1 Ginza. The prefecture says its shops stock soba, Nanbu senbei, ironware and lacquer. I could not confirm opening hours.
- **Miyagi.** Miyagi Furusato Plaza reopened on 8 August 2025 in the Tokyo Kōtsū Kaikan in Yūrakuchō, after crowdfunding. Its old Ikebukuro shop is closed, but two prefectural pages date that differently: one says it ran until December 2024, the other that its lease ended at the end of February 2025. Zunda shakes and Shiroishi *ūmen* noodles sell well.
- **Yamagata.** Oishii Yamagata Plaza in Ginza 1-chōme, with produce and the restaurant YAMAGATA San-Dan-Delo. Hours are 10:00 to 20:00, but the site says they are shortened for the time being (checked 11 October 2026).
- **Fukushima.** Nihonbashi Fukushima-kan MIDETTE in Nihonbashi Muromachi, 10:30 to 19:00 (checked 11 October 2026), with nishin no sanshō-zuke and ika-ninjin among its products.

**What changes.** At the source these dishes have seasons and settings. The Tokyo shops sell mainly products all year: tinned ichigo-ni, kozuyu in pouches, frozen sea squirt. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Wanko soba and gyūtan are official local dishes."** Neither is in the farm ministry's list; both are Cultural Agency 100-year foods.
- **"Imoni is beef and soy sauce."** Inland Yamagata's is; Shōnai's and Miyagi's are pork and miso.
- **"Senbei-jiru is an old name."** The ministry says the name settled only in the Heisei era, though the agency says the food goes back more than 200 years.
- **"Tohoku is one cuisine."** The sources speak prefecture by prefecture; even Aomori splits into rice, flour and potato.
