In 2026 the chains' sweet-potato and moon-viewing products went on sale in early September, while it was still hot. New rice and new soba are named as such, and two autumn foods, matsutake and saury, have become scarce enough that official reports explain why. Winter turns to root vegetables, Chinese cabbage, citrus and the fish of the cold months. This page goes through the second half of the year using the farm ministry's lists and the few rules and figures the official sources give.

For what *shun* means and how chains sell the season, start from the shun overview.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's washoku pages describe *shun* as the peak of an ingredient, with a first taste (*hashiri*) before it and a last taste (*nagori*) after. Autumn gives the ministry its example of *nagori*: autumn aubergine, a vegetable whose flavour and sweetness are said to deepen as the season ends.

## What is in season

As with spring and summer, the ministry's lists overlap without matching.

**Autumn.**

- **The children's food-education page:** sweet potatoes, persimmons, Pacific saury (*sanma*) and chestnuts.
- **The washoku page** calls autumn the season of colour and harvest. Sweet potatoes and other tubers, it says, store up nutrients from the summer sun and are rich in starch and vitamin C. Its fruit are grapes, Asian pears, chestnuts, persimmons and apples; its mushrooms matsutake, shimeji, enoki and maitake; its fish salmon and saury, which carry more fat in autumn.
- **The ministry's English-language calendar** for restaurants serving overseas visitors lists barracuda, salmon, salmon roe, mackerel, saury, autumn bonito, chestnuts, pomegranates, persimmons, Asian pears, grapes, apples, sweet potatoes, taro, ginkgo nuts, shimeji, buckwheat, yamaimo yam, maitake and matsutake, with a note that moon-viewing dumplings are offered at the mid-autumn full moon. Its month ranges could not be read.

**Winter.**

- **The children's page:** Chinese cabbage, mandarin oranges (*mikan*), yellowtail (*buri*) and daikon.
- **The washoku page:** root vegetables (daikon, turnip, carrot, burdock), Chinese cabbage and Japanese leek, which it says grow sweeter in the cold; mikan, lemons and other citrus; and among seafood yellowtail, monkfish, crab, oysters and fugu.
- **The English-language calendar** lists monkfish, spiny lobster, blackthroat seaperch, oysters, red bream, snow crab, cod, flounder, puffer fish, yellowtail, scallops, pond smelt, mandarin oranges, yuzu, Japanese mustard, spinach, burdock, garland chrysanthemum, daikon, Chinese cabbage and lotus root, noting that people eat pumpkin and take a yuzu bath at the winter solstice.

Yellowtail and sashimi, oysters, crab, fugu and hot pots each have their own overview on this site. The winter solstice and New Year's Eve soba belong to the overview of food for annual events.

## What makes it autumn

**Chestnuts.** The ministry's *aff* magazine (October 2019) gives the season of Japanese chestnuts as September to October, varying by variety: Tanzawa in early to mid-September, Porotan in mid to late September, Tsukuba and Ginyose in late September to early October. It says Tsukuba suits boiled chestnuts and *shibukawa-ni* (chestnuts simmered in their inner skins), Tanzawa suits confectionery, and Porotan peels easily. The dishes it shows are chestnut rice, Mont Blanc, roast chestnuts and chestnut buns. I could not get national harvest figures.

**Matsutake.** A farm ministry page on Kyoto's matsutake rice gives the season as September to early November, and says the prefecture's production fell from over 1,200 tonnes a year before the war to a few tonnes in recent years. Its reasons: the change of fuels meant pine branches and needles were no longer gathered and pine woods were left alone; matsutake prefers dry, poor ground and struggles once other trees grow up; and pine wilt killed pines. It cannot be cultivated, so the hills need tending. Matsutake rice, the page says, is an autumn dish of traditional restaurants, and the mushrooms are wiped with a cloth rather than washed. Nationally, the Forestry Agency gives 2025 production as 15 tonnes, down 70.6% on the year before, with Nagano, Iwate and Okayama as the main producers, and says that because there is no practical cultivation, output depends heavily on the weather.

**Saury.** The Fisheries Agency's white paper put the 2021 catch at about 18,000 tonnes, a poor catch at a record-low level. It gives two possible causes: a weaker Oyashio current that moved the fish's migration routes and breeding grounds offshore where food is scarce, reducing the stock; and catches by foreign vessels. The agency's long-range forecast for August to December 2025 expected the run to be at last year's low level overall (below it early in the season, above it later), with the share of one-year-old fish (29 cm and over) similar to last year and the fish heavier. In April 2026 the agency announced that the North Pacific Fisheries Commission had agreed to cut the 2026 high-seas catch limit by 5% to 115,425 tonnes; a 10% cut was not applied because members could not agree on the stock assessment. I could not confirm Japan's catch for 2022 to 2025, so I do not say whether it has recovered.

**New rice.** The ministry's consumer advice page sets out who may call rice *shinmai*. Only brown rice packed by 31 December of the year it was grown, or polished rice milled and packed by that date, may carry the word. Anything else falls under the food labelling standard's list of banned indications. In short, new rice is new only within its harvest year.

**New soba.** A ministry page on Niigata's *hegi-soba* says buckwheat is sown in summer and harvested in autumn, that the autumn crop is called *aki-shin*, and that soba at this time is excellent in colour, taste and aroma. A page on Nagano's hand-made soba says buckwheat has two harvest seasons a year, spring and autumn; that freshly harvested new soba is good; that the summer new soba is *natsu-shin* and the autumn one *aki-shin*; and that Shinshū's new soba appears from about mid-October to early November. Ibaraki's *Hitachi aki-soba*, in the ministry's traditional-food guide, is sown in mid-August and harvested in late October; that page does not use the phrase "new soba". I found no labelling rule for new soba like the one for new rice, and no official source on new-soba festivals.

## What makes it winter: mikan

The ministry's *aff* magazine (January 2017) calls mikan a fruit whose season was originally winter, and says breeding aimed at earlier shipping followed: early varieties (*wase*) start harvesting before October and are popular for their balance of sweet and sour. In the Edo period, it says, *Kishū mikan* was the main kind, and the seedless *unshū mikan* was avoided because the continuation of the family name mattered. (The page does not spell out the link between seedlessness and the family line.) In the Meiji era the unshū's ease of eating and larger fruit made it popular and production grew. I could not get the latest harvest figures.

## The shops, and what they charge

Prices are tax included as posted in the companies' releases. Starbucks gives takeaway / eat-in prices, which follow the consumption tax rates of 8% and 10% as of 2026-10-10.

- **McDonald's Japan, Tsukimi family** (on sale from 2 September 2026, nationwide except some shops). Tsukimi Burger from ¥460, value set from ¥760; Cheese Tsukimi from ¥490; Tsukimi Muffin from ¥420. End dates differ: the Tsukimi Burger until late October (planned), the new *Aburi gyū sukiyaki-fū Tsukimi* until late September, the Nagano Shine Muscat Tsukimi McShake until mid-October. The release notes that prices may differ in some shops. The burger dates from 1991; the origin of its name I could not confirm.
- **Starbucks Japan, sweet potato** (from 2 September 2026, no end date given). *Torori mitsu-imo beni haruka* Frappuccino, Tall ¥687 / ¥700; mousse tea latte ¥628 / ¥640; a baked sweet-potato cake of beni haruka and *annō-imo* ¥530 / ¥540. The release says domestic beni haruka is coming into season, and that because September is still hot it chose a chilled taste rather than hot baked potato.
- **Seven-Eleven Japan.** Its 30-product autumn fair was announced in September 2025; I could not find a 2026 version, so no prices are given.

I could not find company sources for yuzu or matcha as limited seasonal flavours.

## How to eat it

Two practical points from the sources: *shinmai* on a rice bag means it was packed within its harvest year, and the Kyoto page describes wiping matsutake with a cloth rather than washing them.

## Not all of Japan's autumn is this

The season shifts with the variety and the place: chestnuts ripen from early September to early October by variety, Ibaraki's Hitachi aki-soba is harvested in late October while Shinshū's new soba appears from mid-October, and mikan, originally winter fruit, now starts before October with early varieties. Matsutake's main producers are now Nagano, Iwate and Okayama, not Kyoto.

## Autumn away from where it grows

Tokyo's central wholesale market shows how the season is stretched on its way to the city. Its children's page (figures from 2008) says apples harvested from September to November are stored until about June or July of the next year, and that growers use greenhouses and other methods to ship when the market has little and prices are high. Produce arrives at night and goes to auction from about 7 a.m. The farm ministry's children's page adds that many foods on sale all year are greenhouse-grown or imported.

What I could not find: how much later, or at what price, a producing area's autumn foods reach Tokyo shops, and how supermarkets handle autumn foods out of season. I have not said that anything tastes better where it is grown.

## What people get wrong

- **"Any rice from this year can be called new rice."** Only rice packed by 31 December of its harvest year may be labelled *shinmai*.
- **"New soba has one season."** Ministry pages describe *natsu-shin* and *aki-shin*.
- **"Matsutake is rare because it is farmed badly."** The Forestry Agency says it cannot be cultivated at all, and the Kyoto page links the decline to the state of the pine woods.
- **"The saury collapse has one cause."** The Fisheries Agency gives two possible causes, ocean conditions and foreign catches, without choosing.
- **"Autumn products appear in autumn."** The chains' autumn products in 2026 went on sale on 2 September.
