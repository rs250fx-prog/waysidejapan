In Chōfu, on the western side of Tokyo, an old temple has a cluster of soba shops at its gate. The temple's own website opens with a four-part motto — the water and greenery of Musashino, the temple, and soba — and the local soba union says more than twenty shops line the approach today. Jindaiji soba is not a cut, a flour or a way of serving. It is soba with a place and a story attached, and the story comes in several versions, which is where this page starts. For the city's other soba — the old houses, the dark sauce, the half-dip — see [Edo soba](/en/eat/soba-edo/); for the noodle in general, [soba](/en/eat/soba/).

## Where it came from

**The temple.** The soba union's page on the temple says Jindaiji was founded in 733, in the Nara period, to enshrine Jinja Daiō, a water deity. The priest who opened it, Mankū, is said to have been born to parents brought together by that deity's blessing, which is why the temple is visited for matchmaking. Its Shakadō hall keeps a bronze figure of the Buddha from the late Asuka (Hakuhō) period, a national treasure — the only one held by a temple in Tokyo, and said to be the oldest national-treasure Buddha in eastern Japan. I could not find the founding year on the temple's own website, so the date here is the union's.

**The soba.** Several sources tell the origin, and they overlap without matching. The union says plainly that there are various theories.

- The *Edo Meisho Zue*, the illustrated guide to Edo published in 1834–36, in the copy the Tokyo Metropolitan Library puts online: the land around Jindaiji was poor for rice, so people grew buckwheat and paid the temple in buckwheat flour instead of rice, and the temple made soba to entertain its visitors. That, it says, is the traditional account of how Jindaiji soba began.
- The agriculture ministry tells the same story — the land around Chōfu was unsuited to rice, so tenant farmers made buckwheat flour and offered it to the temple — and adds that the flour was stone-milled and fragrant, and that the shogun Iemitsu ate it on a hawking trip and praised it, after which buckwheat growing took hold.
- The soba union: in the Edo period, flour paid to the temple by nearby farmers was made into noodles in the temple's lodgings and won a name among worshippers; the third shogun, Iemitsu, stopped there on a hawking trip and praised it highly. The union also records that the soba was presented to a prince-abbot of Kan'ei-ji in Ueno and admired.
- The Chōfu tourism association (2022): the plateau north of the temple suited buckwheat, tenant farmers grew it and paid the temple in flour, and the temple served soba to guests. It dates the presentation to the prince-abbot of Kan'ei-ji to the Genroku era, and says the soba was then widely recommended to the shogun's house and the lords.

The union and the tourism association name the prince-abbot differently. This page does not choose between them, and does not choose between Iemitsu and the prince-abbot either.

**Its reputation.** The union quotes the gazetteer *Shinpen Musashi Fudoki-kō* calling the soba superb, and says that in the Bunka and Bunsei eras (1804–30) Ōta Shokusanjin, a shogunate official inspecting the Tama River, ate it and spread its fame. It also says the soba was known as *kenjō soba*, "presented soba", eaten only by the upper classes. I could not confirm when the gazetteer was compiled.

**The modern trade.** The union says the area drew attention in 1961, when the Jindai botanical garden opened. The ministry says the place became a sightseeing spot as bus routes developed and visitors to the garden grew.

## What makes it Jindaiji soba

**The land.** The union says the soil around the temple is *kuroboku*, a black soil that suited buckwheat. The tourism association points to the plateau north of the temple; the *Edo Meisho Zue* and the ministry say the land was poor for rice. Every version starts from farmland that grew buckwheat better than rice.

**The water.** The union says local spring water was used to rinse the noodles. Beyond that and the temple's motto, I found no official description of the springs — where they are, how much they give, or what the water is like.

**The flour.** Stone-milled and fragrant, in the ministry's account of the origin. How much of the flour used at Jindaiji today is grown locally, I could not find out.

**The menu.** The union's menu page lists *mori* soba (some shops call it *seiro*; with nori on top it becomes *zaru*), *oroshi* with grated daikon, *tanuki* with tempura crumbs, *sansai* with mountain vegetables, *yamakake* with grated yam, and tempura soba — which, it says, at most Jindaiji shops means prawn tempura. The tourism association adds that shops have their own dishes, such as buns made with buckwheat flour.

**The temple's hours.** The union says the temple normally closes its gate at 5 p.m. and many shops close with it. Over the New Year most shops serve until the soba runs out.

## Why Jindaiji

Put the versions side by side and the shape is the same: farmland better for buckwheat than for rice, a temple that received the flour and fed its visitors, praise from someone of rank — a shogun or a prince-abbot — and, in the twentieth century, buses and a botanical garden that brought crowds. The ministry's account of Edo soba is a different shape: a city food that ran from street stalls to shops with their own premises, with Sunaba, Sarashina and Yabu as its three great names. Jindaiji's begins at a temple gate. That difference in origin is what the official sources support. I found no official comparison of the noodle or the sauce at Jindaiji with central Tokyo's, so this page makes none.

## How to eat it

The union's own menu page is the best local guide.

1. **Go early.** Many shops close with the temple gate at about 5 p.m.
2. **Choose the plate.** *Mori* is the plain cold soba to dip; at some shops it is called *seiro*, and with nori it is *zaru*. A cold plate with tempura alongside is *tenzaru*; tempura soba, at most shops here, comes with prawn.
3. **Condiments.** Spring onion and wasabi are the basics, the union says, and shichimi pepper is up to you.
4. **Dip a little at a time.** The union says dip the soba into the sauce a little at a time. The national noodle federation says dip about half, to enjoy the flavour of the soba itself.
5. **Soba-yu.** With dipped soba, the cooking water arrives after the meal. You pour it into the leftover sauce to thin it and drink it like a soup. The federation's dictionary says the habit began in Shinshū and is thought to have reached Edo in the Kan'en era (1748–51), and that why people started drinking it is not clear.

## Jindaiji soba away from Jindaiji

The name belongs to the place, and I found no official source on Jindaiji soba served anywhere else. What a visitor eats at the gate is ordered and eaten in the same way as soba in the rest of Tokyo — the ministry describes the city's soba as *mori* to dip and *kake* with hot broth poured over, with spring onion or chopped nori to change the taste — and the union's menu is in the same words. What is Jindaiji's own is the story, the temple and the setting.

## Getting there

The temple is at 5-15-1 Jindaiji-motomachi, Chōfu. Its gate opens at 6:00 and closes at about 17:00. There is no convenience store or cash machine near the shops — the union puts the nearest a 15 to 20 minute walk from the main gate — so bring what you need. I could not confirm bus routes from an official page for this update. On 3 and 4 March the temple holds its Ganzan Daishi festival, with a daruma market of about 300 stalls. The union announces a soba festival in the new-soba season; I did not confirm its dates.

## What people get wrong

- **"Iemitsu started it."** It is one of several stories, and the union itself says there are various theories.
- **"The buckwheat is grown at the temple."** That is the historical account. How much of today's flour is local, I could not find.
- **"The shops stay open into the evening."** Many close with the temple gate, at about 5 p.m.
- **"There will be a cash machine."** Not near the shops, by the union's own notice.
- **"It tastes different from other Tokyo soba in a known way."** No official source compares them. The difference on record is the origin.
