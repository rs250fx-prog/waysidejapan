  - q: Is it safe to eat raw horse in Japan?
    a: Horse sashimi is a regional dish in Kumamoto, Yamanashi and Aizu. Wagyu gets the attention, and it has its own page on this site, starting from the wagyu overview. This page is about the rest: the pork that Kagoshima turned into a brand, the grilled lamb that Hokkaido claims as its own, the raw horse eaten in three regions far apart, and the wild deer and boar that the government asks you to cook all the way through. They have little in common except that each has a story, a rule or a statistic that explains what is on the plate. The lineage pages go deeper on each.

## What it is

Four kinds of meat, each tied to a place.

- **Pork.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says more than 400 pork brands are said to exist in Japan, and a Kagoshima research report gives the same figure from a 2018 handbook. The best known is Kagoshima’s black pork, *kurobuta*. Pork also anchors two regional dishes with official write-ups: the grilled pork bowl of Obihiro, *butadon*, and the sweet simmered pork belly of Nagasaki, *kakuni*.
- **Lamb and mutton.** *Jingisukan* is lamb or mutton grilled on a domed pan with vegetables, and the farm ministry calls it a Hokkaido dish.
- **Horse.** *Basashi*, raw horse, has ministry entries for Kumamoto, Yamanashi and Aizu in Fukushima, each with its own history and its own way of eating it.
- **Deer and wild boar.** Wild game, *gibier* in Japanese usage, is being hunted more because deer and boar damage crops, and the meat is being used as food. Here the official writing is mostly about safety.

Pork cutlets are covered on the tonkatsu pages, and Okinawa’s braised pork *rafute* on the Okinawa local-food page. They are mentioned here, not repeated.

## Why it is the way it is

**Pork brands are local standards, not one national one.** The *aff* article explains that brands differ by bloodline, feed and so on, and gives no common definition of a pork brand. I could not confirm a legal definition of "brand pork", or the legal conditions for labelling pork as *kurobuta*, from the original documents, so I do not state them. What can be stated is one brand’s own rules: Kagoshima’s producers’ council requires Berkshire pigs raised apart from other breeds, fed 10 to 20 percent sweet potato for at least 60 days at the end of fattening.

**Jingisukan follows wool policy.** The farm ministry links it to the Taishō era, when the First World War made imported wool hard to get and the state encouraged sheep farming. Hokkaido took it up, mutton began to be eaten, and after the war, as imported wool and synthetic fibre spread, sheep farming shifted toward meat. Today Hokkaido has 58 percent of Japan’s sheep, by the ministry’s 2023 count — and yet in FY2022 domestic lamb and mutton was 98 tonnes against 20,187 tonnes imported, a domestic share of 0.5 percent. Most jingisukan lamb is imported, mainly from Australia and New Zealand.

**Raw horse is eaten where horses were kept.** The ministry ties Yamanashi’s horse dishes to the old province of Kai, which supplied horses to the court and kept many for carrying loads up Mount Fuji. Kumamoto’s story starts with a warlord, Aizu’s with a war. All three are below and on the horse page.

**Wild game is ruled by hygiene.** The health ministry explains that wild animals, unlike livestock, have no controlled feed or health, so nobody knows what pathogens they carry. That is why the rules for deer and boar look nothing like the rules for horse.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Kurobuta was a brand before brands.** Kagoshima’s research report says that in the Shōwa 30s (1955–64) black pigs fattened on sweet potato were shipped alive to Tokyo, won over the city, and became Japan’s first pork brand. The same pig nearly disappeared: by 1975 black pork was 1.6 percent of Kagoshima’s pig shipments.
- **Butadon is filed under rice bowls.** The donburi overview has Obihiro’s pork bowl on its list; the pork page here covers its background.
- **Deer and boar are on the horse page for now.** They are there as a contrast: raw horse is a regional dish with its own controls, and raw wild game is something the government tells you not to eat.

## Where to eat it

- **Kagoshima** — kagoshima kurobuta, the brand with written standards.
- **Obihiro** — butadon. The city calls itself the birthplace and publishes a map of specialist shops.
- **Nagasaki** — kakuni of skin-on pork belly, close to its Chinese original, according to the ministry.
- **Hokkaido** — jingisukan: Sapporo for the grill-then-dip style, Takikawa for the marinated style.
- **Kumamoto, Yamanashi, Aizu-Wakamatsu** — basashi, each eaten its own way.

## The same name, a different place

I could not find an official source comparing any of these dishes in their home region with the same dish in Tokyo or elsewhere. What the sources do show are differences inside the home regions, and one case of a name travelling ahead of the food:

- **Kurobuta became famous in Tokyo first.** The Kagoshima report’s account is that the brand was made by live pigs sold in Tokyo. After a carcass trading standard came in in 1961, the pigs were slaughtered near the farms and the meat went out across Japan.
- **Nagasaki’s kakuni is not the national kakuni.** The farm ministry calls the two "similar but different": Nagasaki still cooks close to the original, simmering skin-on belly sweet.
- **Jingisukan differs inside Hokkaido.** Takikawa marinates the meat; Sapporo grills it plain and dips it.
- **Horse differs between regions.** Kumamoto eats it with sweet soy; Aizu with soy and *karashi-miso*, a mustard-miso.

## What people get wrong

- **"Jingisukan is Mongolian."** The farm ministry says it is often thought so, and that it was born in Hokkaido.
- **"Jingisukan lamb is Hokkaido lamb."** Hokkaido has most of Japan’s sheep, but domestic meat was 0.5 percent of supply in FY2022.
- **"Black pork is any dark pig."** In Japan *kurobuta* means the Berkshire breed, and Kagoshima’s brand adds feed and breeding rules on top.
- **"If horse can be eaten raw, so can venison."** Horse sold for basashi in Kumamoto is frozen before distribution as a parasite measure. For deer and boar, the health ministry’s rule is to cook to 75°C at the centre for a minute or more, and freezing does not kill the trichinella worm.
- **"Kakuni is kakuni."** Nagasaki’s version comes from Chinese *dongpo* pork by way of the city’s *shippoku* banquet cooking, and the ministry treats it as its own dish.
