A rice ball is the simplest thing you can do with cooked rice: press it into a shape you can hold. That simplicity is why its history is hard to pin down. There are three different "first" onigiri, depending on whether you count an archaeological find, a word in old documents, or the first time someone sold one. There are two names, and an old dictionary's map of who used which. And there are versions you will only meet in one region. This page sets those out, side by side.

For rice in general, start from the rice overview. For the convenience-store rice ball — how the film keeps the nori crisp, how to store it, what it costs — see the convenience-store onigiri page.

## Where it came from

There are three accounts, and they are answering different questions. This site does not choose between them.

**1. An archaeological find in Ishikawa.** Nakanoto town's official page says that in 1987 a carbonised lump of rice shaped like a *chimaki* was excavated at the Sugitani Chanobatake site, a Yayoi-period settlement, and dates from the middle Yayoi period, about 2,000 years ago. The town calls it the find that "is said to be" Japan's oldest onigiri; it does not claim more than that. A public library's reference answer adds that rice-ball-like carbonised finds come from both Yayoi and Heian-period sites.

**2. *Tonjiki* at Heian banquets.** The farm ministry describes *tonjiki* as food set out on stands in the gardens of aristocratic houses during banquets and given to people of low rank. It was *kowaii*, steamed rice, pressed by hand, and the ministry says it is called the prototype of onigiri. It notes that *tonjiki* appears in *The Tale of Genji*, at a coming-of-age ceremony. Library reference answers, citing Japanese food dictionaries, say the old word was *tonjiki* and that the *tonjiki* in Nara and Heian documents is presumed to mean a rice ball. Presumed is the word.

**3. The first rice ball for sale.** A library reference answer, citing a book on onigiri across Japan, says rice balls were first sold commercially as Japan's first station bento, at Utsunomiya station in 1885. Station-bento origins are themselves disputed, so treat this as one claim among several.

**In between.** The farm ministry quotes the Edo-period encyclopaedia *Morisada Mankō* on what went into a *makunouchi* bento: ten rice balls, konnyaku, grilled tofu, simmered gourd strips and taro, kamaboko and rolled omelette. One library answer cites a source saying the rice ball became an ordinary packed lunch only after the Meiji era.

Stories about pickled plum in rice balls in the Kamakura period, or rice balls as army rations, circulate widely. I could not confirm them from a source I opened, so they are not given here.

## What makes it onigiri

**The name.** Two library reference services answer this. Tochigi Prefectural Library (2004) says both *onigiri* and *omusubi* are polite words for *nigirimeshi*, pressed rice. Citing the *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* (2002), it says *onigiri* was originally a word used by women and children; that by region *onigiri* was more common in western Japan and *omusubi* in eastern Japan; and that *onigiri* was "becoming the general term". It adds that *nigirimeshi* appears in a 1698 book, *omusubi* in a 1833 collection of comic verse, and *onigiri* in a 1946 story by Dazai Osamu — as far as the library could tell. Tokyo Metropolitan Library (2003) says dictionaries describe *omusubi* as a polite form of *nigirimeshi*, and as a word of the old court ladies.

That regional split is a dictionary's description from 2002. I could not find a current survey, so I would not tell anyone which word people in Osaka or Tokyo use today.

**The shape and the nori.** I looked for a ministry or industry source that sets a standard way to shape a rice ball or wrap it in nori and did not find one. What I did find is an engineering curiosity: the farm ministry's magazine *aff* (November 2020) describes Panasonic's "OniRobot", which presses rice from five directions to reproduce the pressure of a rice-ball maker's hands and make a soft-textured ball. The article says it was not commercialised.

**The rice is traceable.** Under the rice traceability law, rice balls are listed among the cooked-rice products whose rice must be recorded and whose origin must be passed on, and restaurants must tell customers where the rice in rice dishes, rice balls included, was grown.

## The two onigiri days

Both come from Nakanoto town, where the rice lump was found.

- **18 June.** The town says the date combines the 6 from the name of the former Rokusei town and the 18th, the monthly rice day. The page says the day was recognised by the Japan Anniversary Association; it does not say when, and I could not confirm the year.
- **18 November.** On 19 June 2015 the town passed an ordinance on a Nakanoto "onigiri day" and added 18 November, the month of the find.

Other "onigiri" or "omusubi" days are mentioned online. I could not confirm them from a primary source.

## How to eat it

No official source I found sets rules for eating a rice ball, and this page does not invent any. Two things are worth knowing:

- **Fillings.** I could not find a national statistic on the standard fillings. The examples the big convenience-store chains and the national tourism organisation give — salmon, pickled plum, cod roe, tuna mayonnaise, kombu, pickles — are on the convenience-store onigiri page.
- **Size varies.** One regional rice ball described by the farm ministry uses 200 g of rice (below), and the ministry says *mehari-zushi* was once the size of a softball.

## Not all onigiri is this

The farm ministry's *aff* (November 2020) went looking for rice balls sold only by local convenience-store chains, not the national ones:

- **"Kuma"** at Tateyama Thunderbird in Toyama — bear meat from local hunters, simmered in soy sauce.
- **Pork-and-egg onigiri** at Kanehide Zipmart in Okinawa, with luncheon meat.
- **Hot Chef large salmon onigiri** at Seicomart in Hokkaido.
- **Scallop-miso onigiri** at Orange Heart in Aomori.
- **Miso chicken-cutlet "bomb" onigiri** at MG in Mie, with 200 g of rice.

The same article says that in spring 2020 Lawson ran regional rice-ball contests, and a "Sendai flavour" (beef tongue with miso mayonnaise) went on sale nationwide. It adds that it has since been discontinued.

**Mehari-zushi, Wakayama.** The farm ministry's children's food-education site describes a large rice ball wrapped in salted *takana* leaves, made as a packed lunch for work in the mountains and between busy fishing trips. It gives two explanations of the name: that you open your mouth as wide as your eyes to eat it, or that it is so good your eyes go wide. It says the dish appears in a mid-Edo kabuki play, *Ise Ondo Koi no Netaba*.

## Onigiri away from home

The rice ball has no single home, so the comparison is between national and local. The national version is the big chains’ convenience-store rice ball. The local version is a regional chain's own creation or a regional dish like *mehari-zushi*, and the *aff* article's point is that you meet these only by going to the region. The name may change too: *onigiri* in the west, *omusubi* in the east, according to a 2002 dictionary.

I could not find an official comparison of how rice balls differ in price or size between a region and Tokyo, and nothing official says the local one is better.

## What people get wrong

- **"Onigiri has a known origin."** There are three, answering three different questions — oldest find, oldest word, first sale — and none of them is settled.
- **"Onigiri is the western word and omusubi the eastern one, full stop."** That is how one dictionary described it in 2002, while also saying *onigiri* was becoming the general term.
- **"The oldest rice ball is proven to be 2,000 years old."** The town that found it says it is *said* to be the oldest.
- **"There is a proper way to shape or wrap it."** I found no official source that sets one.
