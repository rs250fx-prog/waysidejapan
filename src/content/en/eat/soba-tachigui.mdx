There is a kind of soba you eat on your feet: a counter on a station platform or a narrow shop near the ticket gates, a bowl in front of you, a train or an office to get to afterwards. The agriculture ministry, in its page on Tokyo soba, calls the station standing-soba counter a scene particular to Japan — and says nothing about where it came from. Neither, as far as I could find, does any other official source. What follows is what the records do say: two theories about the first station soba, one chain's dates, one platform caterer's century, and the prices one chain publishes. The rest of Tokyo soba — the old houses, the half-dip, the cooking water — is on the [Edo soba](/en/eat/soba-edo/) page.

## Where it came from

**Station soba.** The national noodle federation's soba dictionary, which cites a soba dictionary and a history of the station lunch box as its references, says that catering inside stations — dining rooms, tea rooms, kiosks — began almost as soon as the railways did. The oldest recorded eating place it gives is a Western-food shop opened by Ueda Toranosuke inside Shinbashi station in June 1872. For soba it offers a theory rather than a date: that the first station soba was at Oshamanbe or at Mori, both on the Hakodate main line in Hokkaido. What made station soba widely known, it says, seems to have been Karuizawa, around 1897, a few years after the Shin'etsu line opened in 1894. The climb between Yokokawa and Karuizawa was so steep that trains stopped there about fifteen minutes to change locomotives, and passengers could eat freshly made soba while they waited. The federation says "there is a theory" and "it seems", and this page keeps both. Other stations named as the first appear only in sources I could not verify.

**Fast soba before the railway.** Edo had quick soba long before it had stations. The federation's account of the night vendors begins with the Meireki fire of 1657: rebuilding drew labourers into the city, demand for eating out grew, and vendors began selling noodles at night from stalls, mostly shouldered rather than wheeled. At first the main item was udon, not soba, and everything was sold hot, with the broth poured over. *Fūrin* soba vendors sold bowls with toppings such as chikuwa or wheat gluten. The selling season ran from the ninth day of the ninth month to the third day of the third month of the old calendar. The prices it records are ten *mon* for *yotaka* soba (one account), twelve for fūrin soba, and, in the late-Edo encyclopaedia *Morisada Mankō*, sixteen *mon* a bowl, with toppings at twenty-four or thirty-two.

It is tempting to draw a line from those stalls to today's counters. The federation's page on Edo stalls does not mention eating standing up, and I found no official source connecting the two, so this page does not. The ministry says only that soba was among the stall foods of Edo.

**The chains.** Fuji Soba's own history is precise. Its founder, Dan Michio, opened Soba Kiyo, a standing-soba shop and the forerunner of Fuji Soba, in 1966. In 1972 he founded Daitan Food to concentrate on standing soba, and that year ran 24-hour shops in Shibuya, Shinjuku, Ikebukuro and Nishi-Ogikubo. The company does not claim to have been the first standing-soba business, and neither does this page.

**A platform caterer.** JR East's own magazine traces the soba counters on Shinagawa's platforms to Tokiwaken, which began selling boxed lunches there in 1923, marked its centenary in 2023, and opened as a standing-soba counter in 1964. It is one example of a station caterer moving from boxed lunches to noodles; whether others followed the same path I could not confirm.

## What makes it standing soba

**The counter.** You stand. JR East's article sells its Shinagawa counters as quick and filling, something to eat while waiting for a train.

**The words.** The menu uses the same words as any soba shop. In the federation's glossary, *kake* is soba with hot broth poured over it; *mori*, soba on a bamboo tray, to dip; *tanuki*, soba topped with tempura crumbs; *oroshi*, soba with grated daikon; *tenzaru*, cold soba with prawn tempura alongside. Fuji Soba's menu lists kake and mori at the same price, a tempura soba, and a morning soba with an *onsen tamago*, a soft-cooked egg.

**Tempura on top.** The federation's dictionary says the origin of tempura soba is unclear, but a *senryū* poem of 1827 mentions it, so soba shops sold it by then at least. *Morisada Mankō* describes it as soba with three or four small shiba prawns. Tokyo soba shops kept to shiba prawns after the Meiji era; when they grew scarce in Tokyo Bay in the Shōwa era, kuruma prawns took over.

**The broth.** The ministry's page on Osaka says that the Kanto region mostly uses a stock based on bonito, while Osaka prefers kombu as the base, combined with bonito flakes. It gives two reasons that "are said": large quantities of kombu reached Osaka by the western sea route in the Edo period, and Osaka's soft water suited kombu stock. The [dashi east and west](/en/eat/dashi-east-west/) page covers this in full. I found no official source saying that Kanto soba broth is darker or that Kansai counters lead with udon. The ministry does describe the Yabu school's broth as salty in the Edo manner. And at Shinagawa, JR East notes that one of Tokiwaken's two platform counters makes its broth darker than the other's — two counters, one operator, two broths.

## Why the station

No official source I found explains why the standing-soba shop came about, so this page gives no reason of its own. What the sources do show is where it has been put: in the Karuizawa story, a fifteen-minute stop; at Shinagawa, a platform where people wait for trains; in 1972, Fuji Soba's 24-hour shops in four of Tokyo's busiest districts. Read together they describe food fitted around travel and work — my reading, not the sources' words.

## How to eat it

1. **Look for a machine by the door.** If there is one, buy a ticket first and hand it over. If there is not, order at the counter. Neither Fuji Soba's menu nor the Shinagawa article describes how ordering works, and the [meal ticket machine](/en/column/how-meal-ticket-machines-work/) column notes that not every shop uses one.
2. **Hot or cold.** *Kake* is the hot bowl, *mori* the cold one to dip. At Fuji Soba they cost the same.
3. **Slurp, or don't.** In the federation's own dialogue a shop owner says you draw soba in with a *zuzuzu* sound and that eating it in one go is stylish. It gives no reason; the [slurping](/en/column/slurping-noodles/) column sets out the explanations people offer.
4. **For mori, dip about half.** The federation says dip the noodle about halfway, to enjoy the flavour of the soba itself, and that it is fine to swallow a mouthful in about three bites — soba digests easily and is enjoyed going down.
5. **If you avoid buckwheat, ask.** Buckwheat is one of eight allergens that Japanese law requires on packaged food, chosen with peanuts because the reactions can be severe, and the consumer agency notes it can turn up in seasonings. Dishes served in restaurants are outside that requirement; the agency asks restaurants to show allergens on menus or websites and to train staff, but it is not compulsory. What any given counter does is for you to ask. See [eating in Japan with allergies](/en/column/eating-in-japan-allergies/).

## Standing soba away from Tokyo

By the federation's account, station soba was not a Tokyo invention: the stations it names for the earliest soba are in Hokkaido, and the one that made it known is Karuizawa, in Nagano prefecture. The chains in this article are Tokyo's. The one regional difference the ministry describes is the stock, as above; how counters elsewhere season their broth I could not confirm.

## Getting there

The Shinagawa counters are on the platforms themselves — platform 1, and platforms 13 and 14 — so you need to be inside the ticket gates. Check the hours before relying on them: the official figures date from August 2024. Fuji Soba's first 24-hour shops, in 1972, were in Shibuya, Shinjuku, Ikebukuro and Nishi-Ogikubo; I did not check current shop addresses for this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Standing soba goes back to the Edo night stalls."** Edo did have night soba stalls. No official source I found connects them to today's counters.
- **"There is one known first station soba."** There are theories — Oshamanbe or Mori, then Karuizawa — and the federation puts them as theories.
- **"Every counter has a ticket machine."** Some do. Not every shop uses one, and the official sources here do not say how many.
- **"It costs the same everywhere."** Fuji Soba itself says prices vary by area.
- **"Kanto broth is darker, everyone knows."** The ministry's difference is bonito against kombu. Two counters at one Shinagawa caterer already differ.
- **"Restaurants must label buckwheat."** Packaged food must. Restaurant dishes are outside the rule; ask.
