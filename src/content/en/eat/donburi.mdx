*Donburi* is first the name of a bowl, and then of everything served in it: a deep bowl of rice with something on top and a sauce soaking down. It runs from the beef-bowl chains to the cutlet bowls of soba shops. It is also a lesson in how much a word can change between towns. Order *katsudon* in Tokyo and you get a cutlet simmered with egg; order it in Fukui and you get one dipped in sauce, no egg at all. This page is the map; the lineage pages cover the beef bowl and the cutlet bowl.

## What it is

**The bowl.** A Japanese dictionary defines *donburi* as a thick, deep ceramic bowl for serving food, and also as short for *donburi-mono*, dishes served in one. The bowl appears in an essay collection of 1784–1814, served with hot simmered tofu. The word *don*, as in *unadon* and *tendon*, attached to a shortened food name, is first recorded in a dictionary of new words in 1919.

**Where the word came from** is unclear. One dictionary guesses at the sound of something dropped into a well; an encyclopedia mentions the sound of something thrown into water and says the origin is not certain. The same encyclopedia records the word from the late 17th century and a "Nanking blue-and-white donburi" in a 1781 menu book, and says that as rice topped with eel or tempura (*donburi-meshi*) spread, *donburi-mono* became the general name. I could not find an official date for when the rice-bowl dish itself took shape.

**Why on rice.** The farm ministry's account of the eel bowl gives one reason: eel and rice were originally served separately, and the eel bowl spread because placing the eel on hot rice kept it warm for delivery.

## Why it is the way it is

Every donburi is rice + a topping + a way of seasoning it, and the seasoning is where regions split. From the farm ministry's local-food database:

- **Set with egg.** The common katsudon. In Yamanashi this is called *ni-katsudon* and is treated as a separate dish.
- **Sauce poured on.** Yamanashi's own katsudon: a cutlet on shredded cabbage, with sauce added just before eating. The ministry says it began at a Meiji-era soba shop in Kōfu whose owner had tasted a cutlet in Tokyo; since soba shops mostly delivered, everything went in one bowl.
- **Dipped in sauce.** Fukui and Komagane (see the katsudon page).
- **Dipped in sweet soy tare.** Niigata's *tare-katsudon*, with two or more cutlets and no egg, said to date from the early Shōwa era.
- **Glazed with sugar-and-soy tare.** Obihiro's *butadon*, pork loin or belly with shredded leek. The ministry says an Obihiro diner devised it in the early Shōwa era as stamina food for settlers and farmers; it wanted to use eel, found it too dear, and grilled pork over charcoal with an eel-style sauce instead.

## How ordering works

- **Size has names.** Sukiya sells mini, regular, *tokumori* and mega. Katsuya's ume, take and matsu step up the meat, with "light" for less rice. A Fukui sauce-katsudon shop offers small, medium and large.
- **Extras have names.** Yoshinoya lists *negi-daku* (extra onion) and *niku-daku* (extra meat) as separate menu items. Its history says *tsuyu-daku*, extra sauce, began as a request from regulars at the Tsukiji fish market.
- **Soup is often separate.** Katsuya sells pork miso soup as a side (small ¥165, large ¥220). Sukiya offers sets on top of the bowl.

## Where to eat it

- **Tokyo** — where Yoshinoya's beef bowl began, at the fish market, and where the katsudon origin claims cluster, around Waseda. Toyosu market has 38 restaurants inside, open to visitors.
- **Fukui** — sauce-katsudon.
- **Komagane, Nagano** — sauce-katsudon on cabbage, under a registered regional trademark.
- **Niigata, Kōfu, Obihiro** — the regional bowls above.

## The same name, a different bowl

- **"Katsudon" is a different order in different towns.** In Fukui and Komagane it means sauce-katsudon; for egg, say so. In Yamanashi the egg version has its own name. In Niigata the cutlet comes in soy tare.
- **Even sauce-katsudon differs.** Fukui serves rice, thin cutlets and sauce with no cabbage; Komagane lays cabbage under the cutlet and sets rules for it; Yamanashi pours sauce over cutlet on cabbage.
- **A national chain lets you choose.** Katsuya sells egg-set katsudon and sauce-katsudon side by side at the same price.

## What people get wrong

- **"Donburi is a dish."** It is a bowl first.
- **"Katsudon always has egg."** Not in Fukui, Komagane, Yamanashi's local version, or Niigata.
- **"The beef bowl is a modern chain invention."** Yoshinoya dates itself to 1899, at the Nihonbashi fish market.
- **"Ume is the smallest everywhere."** Each chain names its own sizes.
