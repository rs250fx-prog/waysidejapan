At a Japanese festival the food comes from stalls: takoyaki, fried chicken, oden, shaved ice. Those stalls are assembled for the day, licensed for food and for the road, and — at least in Osaka — limited by rule to the kind of food that can be cooked simply and heated just before serving. That rule goes a long way to explaining the menu. This page covers the rules, the temporary-stall exception, and the traders.

For food stalls in general, start from the yatai overview.

## What a festival stall is

Osaka city describes a *roten* as a stall assembled each time it trades — unlike Fukuoka's yatai, which set up at a fixed place every night.

## The rules

- **Food permit.** In Osaka, a stall that cooks and serves food needs a restaurant business permit (the category since 1 June 2021). A permit from Osaka prefecture since then is valid across the prefecture. HACCP-style hygiene and a food hygiene supervisor are required.
- **What it may sell (Osaka).** In principle, food simple to prepare on site and heated just before serving: oden, takoyaki, fried chicken, shūmai. Exceptions: shaved ice, warabi-mochi, preparing drinks. Not allowed: raw food such as sashimi and salad, cutting after cooking, and first-stage preparation such as chopping vegetables or cleaning fish on site. Other cities may differ, and I could not confirm the rules for yakisoba, candied apples or candy floss specifically.
- **Road.** A stall set up on a road without moving needs a police road-use permit (category 3), and sometimes a road-occupancy permit too.
- **The local-festival exception (Osaka).** A festival run by a neighbourhood association, children's group or school, held temporarily (in principle up to about five times a year), not mainly for profit, with stalls run by the organiser or people it chooses, can file a temporary-stall notice with the health centre and receive hygiene guidance instead of a permit. Food and equipment follow the stall rules. Trading as a business needs a permit.

So the takoyaki and fried chicken at a festival are not only tradition: they are the kind of food the rules allow a stall to sell. Takoyaki itself, the farm ministry says, grew out of snacks sold at sweet shops and festival stalls in the late Meiji and Taishō eras.

## Who runs them

A reference answer from the National Diet Library sets out the two words:

- **Yashi** (香具師) and **tekiya** (的屋) both mean stall traders and their associations. *Yashi* is the older word; *tekiya* became common from the Meiji era. One source traces the shift to an 1872 Tokyo order abolishing the name yashi.
- Most stallholders were small travelling traders. To manage dealings with local people and friction with other traders on the road, they needed organisation, and regional tekiya groups formed.
- *Yashi* tended to mean an individual and *tekiya* a group (*ikka*), and *yashi* was more used in rural areas, *tekiya* in cities.

I found no official account of today's tekiya organisations, or of how pitch fees work.

## Older than festivals

Street-stall food is far older than modern festivals. In Edo, fire rules confined cooking with fire and oil to outdoors; stall shops appeared in the 1770s and lined streets by the 1780s, selling tempura, eel, sushi, oden and sweets. See the yatai overview.

## The same stall, a different place

- **The rules are local.** Osaka's list of allowed foods is Osaka's; other cities may set different ones.
- **A festival stall is not a Fukuoka yatai.** One is assembled for an event, the other trades nightly on licensed pitches.

## What people get wrong

- **"Festival stalls need no licence."** They need food and road permits, unless a local organiser files a temporary notice under conditions like Osaka's.
- **"Stalls can sell sashimi."** Not in Osaka; raw food is excluded.
- **"Tekiya and yashi are different trades."** They mean the same, with different histories and nuances.
