The Inland Sea, *Seto Naikai*, gives five prefectures a shared shoreline: Okayama, Hiroshima and Yamaguchi on the Honshu side, Kagawa and Ehime on Shikoku. The same things keep turning up in the official descriptions of their dishes: fast tides, sea bream, and festival sushi. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Okayama

**Matsuri-zushi, or bara-zushi.** A scattered sushi packed with Inland Sea fish and vegetables, made in the Bizen Okayama area for festivals, celebrations and guests. The farm ministry says that in the Edo period the lord of Okayama, Ikeda Mitsumasa, ordered commoners to save money by eating one soup and one side dish. People are said to have reasoned that fish and vegetables mixed into sushi rice were no longer a side dish, and so ate rice tossed with ten or more ingredients from a wide tub.

**Mamakari.** A small fish called *sappa* in the Kantō area, caught mainly in the Inland Sea and best around October. The name is said to mean it is so good you go to borrow (*kari*) rice (*mama*) from the neighbours, the ministry says. Pickled in vinegar, the bones soften enough to eat whole. Families once made it themselves; now most people buy it ready-made, and a "Mamakari Promotion Committee" was set up in 2008.

**Tako-meshi in Kurashiki.** Octopus rice, mainly from Kojima. The ministry says Okayama's octopus catch is among the highest in Japan and that octopus from the fast-flowing waters off Shimotsui is firm. It is said to have begun with fishermen eating it on their boats.

### Hiroshima

**Oyster dote-nabe.** Three explanations for the name, all from the farm ministry: miso spread round the inside of the pot like a riverbank (*dote*); a man called Dote Chōkichi who devised it; or the Edo-period oyster boats that carried Hiroshima oysters to Osaka and served hotpot on the riverbank. This page gives all three.

**Tai-meshi.** Along the coast at Fukuyama, Onomichi and Ōsakikamijima, a whole sea bream is cooked in the pot with the rice. Fishermen are said to have started making it in the Edo period, when the headman of Hashirijima began net fishing for sea bream.

**Anago-meshi on Miyajima.** Grilled conger eel laid on rice cooked in stock. The ministry dates its start to 1901 (Meiji 34), four years after the Sanyō Railway opened, when it went on sale as a station lunch box near Miyajimaguchi station. In the 1955–64 period (the Shōwa 30s) it became famous as a "bus lunch" on sightseeing and school-trip coaches.

**Okonomiyaki.** On the ministry's Hiroshima list. It traces the dish to the pre-war *issen yōshoku* and says it changed shape after the war. For the dish itself, see the [okonomiyaki page](/en/eat/konamono-okonomiyaki/).

### Yamaguchi

**Iwakuni-zushi.** A pressed sushi for celebrations in the castle town known for the Kintai bridge. It is also called "lord's sushi", from a tradition that it was presented to and pleased the Kikkawa lord of Iwakuni.

**Fuku-sashi in Shimonoseki.** Fugu sashimi. The ministry says fugu eating was banned after soldiers died from it during Toyotomi Hideyoshi's campaign in Korea, and that Itō Hirobumi, impressed by the fish, had it legalised in Yamaguchi alone in 1888 (Meiji 21); the first licensed restaurant was Shunpanrō. The ministry's page frames the ban as "said". In Shimonoseki the fish is called *fuku*, for luck. For the rules on eating fugu, see the [fugu page](/en/eat/seafood-fugu/).

**Kawara soba.** Green-tea soba, beef and shredded omelette on a hot roof tile, from Kawatana Onsen in Shimonoseki. The sources differ on the year:

- **The farm ministry**: born in 1961 (Shōwa 36).
- **Yamaguchi's tourism site**: born in 1962 (Shōwa 37).

Both give the same inspiration, the story of Satsuma troops in the Satsuma Rebellion grilling meat and wild greens on roof tiles. The ministry adds that an inn-keeping business devised it to give Kawatana Onsen a speciality, and calls it "a dish with a short history". This page gives both years and does not choose.

### Kagawa

**Anmochi zōni.** New Year soup of white miso with round rice cakes filled with sweet bean paste, daikon rounds and *kintoki* carrot. In the Edo period sugar cane growing was encouraged in Sanuki and white sugar became one of the "three whites of Sanuki" — cotton, salt and sugar. The ministry says the dish is said to have begun around the Meiji era, when people put sugar, used once a year, into the New Year soup. White miso is said to have come from Kyoto to the retired emperor Sutoku, exiled to Sanuki after the Hōgen Disturbance.

**Shōyu-mame.** Roasted broad beans dropped hot into soy sauce, sugar and chilli, thought to date from the Edo period. Two origins:

- **Shōdoshima**, where soy brewing began in the Bunroku era.
- **The pilgrims' route**: broad beans roasted as gifts for *henro* pilgrims rolled into a soy sauce jar.

This page gives both. The ministry adds that the dried beans are now mostly imported.

**Oshinuki-zushi.** The centre of *haruio*, a farmers' spring celebration held when Spanish mackerel (*sawara*) come into the Inland Sea. Sushi rice, cooked vegetables, sawara and broad beans are pressed in a mould.

### Ehime

**Tai-meshi.** Two different dishes under one name (see "Not all of the Setouchi is this" below). The southern version is said to have begun as a quick dish made by fishermen on boats where they could not use fire. They often used horse mackerel, and the showier sea bream version was the one called *tai-meshi*.

**Jakoten.** Small fish ground with skin and bone and fried, from Yawatahama and Uwajima in the south. Two theories for the name: a corruption of *zako-ten*, "small-fry tempura", or the name of the fish used, *haranbo* (*hotaru-jako*). According to the history of the Uwajima domain, it is said to go back to 1615, when the first lord, Date Hidemune, brought fish-paste makers from Sendai.

**Matsuyama-zushi (moburi-zushi).** Matsuyama's scattered sushi, with conger eel and a stock of small Inland Sea fish. The ministry says that in 1892 (Meiji 25) Natsume Sōseki was served it at the home of the poet Masaoka Shiki, who wrote a haiku praising "the sushi of Matsuyama". *Moburu* is local dialect for mixing in. In 2006 a fish-market council revived it and put up a stone with Shiki's poem.
## What makes it Setouchi

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Okayama 28, Hiroshima 30, Yamaguchi 30, Kagawa 30, Ehime 25 — 143 in all. Among them:

- **Okayama**: matsuri-zushi, mackerel sushi, pickled mamakari, tako-meshi, offal udon, *demi-katsu-don*.
- **Hiroshima**: oyster dote-nabe, okonomiyaki, tai-meshi, Kure's *nikujaga*, anago-meshi.
- **Yamaguchi**: Iwakuni-zushi, fuku-sashi, fried fugu, whale in *nanban* sauce, kawara soba.
- **Kagawa**: anmochi zōni, shōyu-mame, shippoku udon, oshinuki-zushi, toshiake udon.
- **Ehime**: tai-meshi, Matsuyama-zushi, jakoten, Imabari yakitori, *taruto*.

**What recurs.** Sea bream is a local dish in both Hiroshima and Ehime, and sea bream noodles appear on the Hiroshima, Kagawa and Ehime lists. Celebration scattered sushi appears in Okayama (matsuri-zushi), Ehime (Matsuyama-zushi) and Kagawa (bara-zushi). *Igisu* seaweed tofu is on both the Hiroshima and Ehime lists.

**What is missing.** Kagawa's list has no item called Sanuki udon; it has shippoku udon and toshiake udon. Sanuki udon is not in the farm ministry's list. For the noodle itself, see [Sanuki udon](/en/eat/udon-sanuki/).

**Recognition.** The ministry's mamakari sushi page says that mamakari sushi and bara-zushi were chosen in the 2007 "100 local dishes of farming, mountain and fishing villages". I did not open the Cultural Agency's 100-year food pages for these five prefectures, so I give no count.

## Why the Inland Sea

**The tides.** The farm ministry gives fast currents as the reason for quality again and again, across prefecture lines: the firm octopus off Shimotsui, the fatty "Seto anago" of the fast Ōno strait by Miyajima, the sea bream of Hiroshima's rocky, tidal waters, the firm fish of Ehime's Kurushima strait, and Kagawa's fish "toughened by fast tides".

**Hiroshima.** A mild climate with little rain or snow. The prefecture produces more than half of Japan's oysters, the most of any prefecture (2020 fisheries statistics), which the ministry puts down to calm water, a less salty layer where rivers flow in, and nutrients from the Chūgoku mountains. Its island slopes give Japan's largest lemon harvest.

**Kagawa.** The ministry calls the Inland Sea a "natural fish farm". Salt was made on the shallow shores, anchovies became *iriko* (dried sardines for stock), and Shōdoshima made soy sauce.

**Ehime.** The calm Inland Sea on one side and the ria coast of the Uwa Sea on the other; citrus on the terraced fields of the south, earning the name "citrus kingdom".

**Okayama.** The Okayama plain in the south was shallow sea, made into land by silt and reclamation. Inland Mimasaka, which does not face the sea, made mackerel sushi with salted fish carried from the San'in coast.

**Yamaguchi.** The prefecture divides into three — the Inland Sea coast, the inland mountains and the Japan Sea coast. Its fugu and whale belong to the Japan Sea and Kanmon side rather than to the Inland Sea.

I found no public source that names salt or citrus as features of the whole Inland Sea; the sources speak prefecture by prefecture.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Okayama bara-zushi**: Japanese restaurants and sushi shops across the prefecture, supermarket delis, and the *Momotarō no Matsuri-zushi* station lunch box, sold at Okayama station for more than 50 years.
- **Miyajima anago-meshi**: many restaurants around Miyajima, and mail order.
- **Hiroshima tai-meshi**: mostly restaurants; Tomo-no-ura in Fukuyama has a cluster of sea bream restaurants.
- **Iwakuni-zushi**: eating places and inns, especially around the Kintai bridge.
- **Kawara soba**: restaurants and inns in the Kawatana Onsen area.
- **Kagawa oshinuki-zushi**: supermarkets and restaurants.
- **Matsuyama-zushi**: a *ryōtei* tops it with sea urchin and prawns, an *izakaya* with sashimi, a canteen with jakoten and vinegared mackerel.
- **Jakoten**: Uwajima's fish-paste cooperative publishes a map of where to buy it.

**Prices.** I found no official dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten

- **Kawara soba**: dipped in noodle sauce, with lemon and grated daikon with chilli (*momiji-oroshi*). At home it is made on a hotplate.
- **Southern Ehime tai-meshi**: raw sea bream marinated in soy sauce, mirin, egg, sesame and stock, then spooned over hot rice.
- **Mamakari**: vinegared until the bones are soft, and eaten bones and all.
- **Bara-zushi**: sometimes steamed and served warm, as *nuku-zushi*.
- **Shōyu-mame**: a side dish kept in stock, or a snack with drink; some families use it instead of black beans in the New Year *osechi*.
- **Fuku-sashi**: a luxury for celebrations and special days. The ministry's Yamaguchi story sets it apart from cheap fugu, which is fried. Wild tiger fugu is caught from September to March.

**When.** Bara-zushi is for spring and autumn festivals, Obon, memorial services and guests, and its fillings change with the season — sawara, butterbur and bamboo shoot in spring, matsutake in autumn. Oyster dote-nabe is a winter dish, best in January and February, and appears in school lunches. Oshinuki-zushi is made from late April to June. Anmochi zōni is made at home for New Year, with sweet white miso prepared around December. Tako-meshi in Kurashiki is everyday food.

## Not all of the Setouchi is this

**Ehime's two tai-meshi.** In Imabari and the rest of eastern Ehime, a whole sea bream is cooked with the rice and the flesh flaked through it. In Uwajima, Seiyo and the south, raw sea bream goes over hot rice in a sauce with egg.

**Yamaguchi's other coast.** Fugu and whale are Japan Sea and Kanmon-strait food. The ministry adds that fugu longline fishing began in Yamaguchi. In 2016 "Shimonoseki fuku" became a Geographical Indication, the first for a marine product.

**Okonomiyaki by town.** The ministry names local variants within Hiroshima, among them Kure-yaki and Onomichi-yaki, and says Hiroshima has the most okonomiyaki shops per 100,000 people in Japan (2014 economic census).
## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Kawatana Onsen for kawara soba.** The farm ministry names Kawatana Onsen as the dish's home, and Yamaguchi's tourism site calls it the birthplace. It is about 50 minutes by bus from JR Shimonoseki station, or about 5 minutes by taxi from JR Kawatana-Onsen station. The tourism site's onsen feature also says kawara soba can be had at Nagato-Yumoto Onsen, which it calls the prefecture's oldest spring.

**The Mimasaka springs for mackerel sushi.** The ministry's Okayama story mentions Mimasaka's three springs — Okutsu, Yubara and Yunogō — in the same piece as the inland mackerel sushi. It does not tie a particular dish to a particular spring.

**Dōgo and Matsuyama.** Matsuyama-zushi and Dōgo Onsen share a city, but no single official source I read connects them. For the baths, see [Dōgo Onsen](/en/soak/dogo/). Bathing only (Kami-no-yu) at the Dōgo Onsen main building costs ¥700 for adults and ¥350 for children, tax included (checked 11 October 2026).

**Getting there:**

- **Okayama**: about 3 hours 20 minutes from Tokyo by *Nozomi* shinkansen.
- **Takamatsu**: about 1 hour 20 minutes by air from Haneda, then about 45 minutes by airport bus to JR Takamatsu station; about 1 hour from Okayama by the Marine Liner train.
- **Matsuyama and Dōgo**: about 43 minutes by airport bus from Matsuyama airport to Dōgo-Onsen station; about 25 minutes by tram from JR Matsuyama station.
- **Yamaguchi**: about 1 hour 40 minutes from Haneda, about 4 hours 30 minutes by shinkansen from Tokyo, about 30 minutes from Hiroshima or Hakata (prefectural figures). From Shin-Yamaguchi to Yuda Onsen station on the JR Yamaguchi line, the site of Kitsune no Ashiato, the footbath centre in [Yuda](/en/soak/yuda/), gives 18 minutes by local train and 9 by limited express, then about 10 minutes on foot to the onsen streets. The prefecture's Yuda feature says about 20 minutes by train, and its onsen feature about 15.
- **Hiroshima**: I found no official journey times, so I give none.

## Setouchi food away from the Setouchi

- **Kagawa and Ehime together.** The prefectures share a Tokyo shop, the Kagawa–Ehime Setouchi Shunsaikan, in the Shinbashi Marine Building, 2-19-10 Shinbashi, Minato-ku, with the shop on the ground floor. ## What people get wrong

- **"Sanuki udon is Kagawa's official local dish."** Sanuki udon is not in the farm ministry's list; shippoku udon and toshiake udon are.
- **"The Setouchi is one cuisine."** Yamaguchi faces three ways, and its fugu and whale come from the Japan Sea and Kanmon side.
- **"Tai-meshi is one dish."** Ehime alone has two.
- **"Kawara soba is an old tradition."** The ministry calls it a dish with a short history, born in 1961 or 1962 depending on the source.
- **"Salt and citrus define the whole Inland Sea."** The sources describe Kagawa's salt, Hiroshima's lemons and Ehime's citrus prefecture by prefecture, and none generalises.
