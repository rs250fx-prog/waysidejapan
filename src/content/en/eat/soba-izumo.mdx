Izumo is where the gods gather in the tenth month, and where Japanese soba breaks its own rules. The noodle is nearly black. It comes in a stack of three round red dishes. And you do not dip it — you pour the sauce over it, eat, and pour the remainder into the dish below. Everything a Tokyo soba shop would correct, Izumo does on purpose, and the agriculture ministry lists it with Togakushi and wanko as one of the three great soba traditions of Japan.

## Where it came from

The ministry's record and the city's are the same story with two dates. In the early Edo period a lord named Matsudaira Naomasa was transferred from the Matsumoto domain in Shinano — Nagano, the home of the soba noodle — to the Matsue domain in Izumo, and brought Shinano soba craftsmen with him. The city's materials give his arrival as 1638; a prefectural report gives 1666 for the transfer of the technique. This site records both and chooses neither. The ministry adds that the two Izumo serving styles have different homes: *wariko*, the stacked dishes, grew up in the castle town of Matsue; *kama-age*, served hot in its own cooking water, grew up around Izumo Grand Shrine and the other shrines, where it was sold from stalls during the Kamiari festival when the gods are in residence.

In 2022 the Agency for Cultural Affairs recognised Izumo soba as a "100-year food" with a special award, and the local trade registered 11 February — the day Naomasa was ordered to Matsue — as Izumo Soba Day.

## What makes it Izumo soba

**The flour.** *Hikigurumi*: the whole grain, husk and all, milled together. The noodle is dark grey to black, coarse, and tastes strongly of buckwheat; the wheat content is about 20%. It is the opposite philosophy to Tokyo's white *sarashina*, which uses only the pale centre of the kernel.

**Wariko.** Three round, red-lacquered dishes, stacked, one serving. The word *wariko* once meant a samurai's lunch box; the dishes were square until the Meiji era, when round ones replaced them because square corners were hard to wash. You pour sauce and condiments — spring onion, nori, bonito flakes, and the local grated daikon with chilli (*momiji-oroshi*) — onto the top dish, eat it, then tip the leftover sauce and condiments into the next dish and repeat.

**Kama-age.** Noodles lifted from the pot and served in their own hot cooking water, with the sauce added to the bowl by the eater. The *soba-yu* that Tokyo drinks at the end is here part of the dish from the start.

**The sauce.** Poured on, not dipped. This is the single thing that most surprises other Japanese, and it is simply how Izumo eats.

## Why Izumo

A lord from the soba country arrived with his cooks, and a shrine town supplied festival crowds. Shimane itself grows very little buckwheat — 137 tonnes in 2025, against Hokkaido's 16,800 — so the tradition is one of method and manners rather than farming. The ministry notes that the shrine-side kama-age began as festival stall food for pilgrims to the Grand Shrine during Kamiari-zuki, the month when, in Izumo's calendar, all the gods of Japan are in town.

## The shops, and what they charge

In Izumo:

- **Kenjō Soba Haneya**, Imaichi, Izumo city. Founded in the late Edo period; in May 1907 it served its soba to the Crown Prince (later Emperor Taishō) and was permitted the name *kenjō*, "presented". Prices tax included: wariko (three dishes) ¥1,050, one extra dish ¥350, three-colour wariko ¥1,200, tempura wariko ¥2,100, kama-age ¥950.
- **Arakiya**, five minutes' walk from the Grand Shrine. Founded in the Tenmei era (1780s). Wariko ¥1,080 (tax not stated), 11:00–16:00 or until sold out, closed Wednesdays. A prefectural report counts about twenty soba shops around the shrine.

Matsue households rank 32nd of 52 cities for noodles eaten out, at ¥7,331 a year — just under the national average, which given the size of the city is a lot of soba.

## How to eat it

1. Order *wariko* for the Izumo experience; *kama-age* in cold weather.
2. Put the condiments on the top dish, pour a little sauce from the pot — less than you think — and eat.
3. Tip what is left into the second dish. Repeat. The third dish gets the most seasoned sauce; that is intended.
4. For kama-age, add the sauce to the bowl of noodles and cooking water, taste, add more.
5. Nobody will mind how you slurp.

## Izumo soba away from Izumo

What travels is the flour; what does not is the table. A shop elsewhere can mill with the husk — several Tokyo shops do, and the dark noodle has a following — but the stack of red dishes and the pouring are a local habit that reads as an error anywhere else, so shops outside Shimane mostly serve the dark noodle Tokyo-style, on a tray with a dipping cup. A Shimane antenna shop and a handful of Izumo-named restaurants exist in Tokyo; I could not confirm their menus from an official page, so none are listed. The simplest test is the dishes: if it does not come in a stack of three, it is Izumo flour in someone else's manners.

## Getting there

Izumo Enmusubi Airport has bus connections to the Grand Shrine (about 35 minutes), JR Izumoshi station (about 25) and Matsue station (about 30), by the prefecture's timetable map; current fares were not available from an official page. From Okayama, the Yakumo limited express runs to Izumoshi via Matsue. The Grand Shrine is on the Ichibata electric railway from Izumoshi and Matsue Shinjiko-Onsen; Arakiya is five minutes from the shrine, Haneya in the city centre.

## What people get wrong

- **"Pour the sauce on? That is wrong."** In Izumo it is right, and it has been for three centuries.
- **"Dark soba is low quality."** It is whole-grain by design. The white Tokyo noodle is the refined one; which is better is a matter of city.
- **"Izumo grows the buckwheat."** 137 tonnes a year. The tradition is the milling and the dishes.
- **"The three dishes are three portions."** They are one.
