*Yaki-imo* (焼き芋) is a whole sweet potato roasted until soft. Of the street sweets on these pages, it has the oldest date in a public source: the farm ministry places its popularity in late Edo. This is a short page, because the sources are short.

For sweets in Japan beyond wagashi in general, start from the sweets overview.

## Where it came from

**Late Edo.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says roast sweet potatoes became popular in the late Edo period, and that this is when sweet potatoes came to be grown as a cash crop.

**Thirteen ri.** The ministry’s page for children explains the sellers’ sign. Sweet potatoes, which it says Aoki Kon’yō helped spread, came from around Kawagoe, thirteen *ri* from Edo. So roast-potato shops were called *jūsan-ri*, "thirteen ri", and sold with the line *kuri yori umai jūsan-ri* — "thirteen ri, tastier than chestnuts (*kuri*, which sounds like nine ri) plus four (*yori*, which sounds like four ri)". Nine and four make thirteen.

**Kawagoe.** Kawagoe city says "Kawagoe potato" means sweet potatoes grown around the Kawagoe domain on the Musashino plateau, and that the image took hold in the Kansei era (the late 1700s), when roast potatoes were a hit in Edo.

**Stone-roasting.** How and when *ishi-yaki-imo*, potatoes roasted in hot stones, began and spread, I could not find in an official source.

## What makes it yaki-imo

**The variety.** The ministry’s *aff* says 100 sweet-potato varieties are registered. The one it profiles is *beni haruka* (written べにはるか in the article): selected in 1996, named in 2007, well-shaped with a refined sweetness, reddish-purple skin, yellowish-white flesh, a slightly floury texture and strong sweetness.

**The storage.** The same article says beni haruka gets sweeter when stored for two to three months after harvest at the right temperature and humidity. It does not explain why. A Chiba prefecture study of the storage conditions exists, but I could not read it, so I say no more.

## The shops, and what they charge

I could not find a price for a roast sweet potato from an official source, so none is given.

## How to eat it

The sources here say nothing about how roast potatoes are sold or eaten today, so I have not described it.

## Not all of Japan’s street sweets are this

The crepe has its own page; taiyaki and imagawayaki are with wagashi.

## Yaki-imo away from Kawagoe

Kawagoe made its name as the potato supply for Edo’s roast-potato trade. The city says it was a major producing area until the 1950s–60s, but that today, pushed out by leafy vegetables, it does not grow many. I found no source comparing a roast potato in Kawagoe with one in Tokyo.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kawagoe still grows Tokyo’s sweet potatoes."** The city says it grows far fewer now.
- **"A fresh-dug potato is the sweetest."** For beni haruka, the ministry says two to three months of storage make it sweeter.
- **"Thirteen ri is only a distance."** It is also a pun: nine (*kuri*) plus four (*yori*).
