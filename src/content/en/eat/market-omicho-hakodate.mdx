Outside Tokyo, the market where a visitor eats a seafood bowl is usually not a wholesale market at all. It is a town market: a warren of fishmongers, greengrocers and small restaurants where local people shop, and where the seafood-bowl restaurants have gathered. Kanazawa's Ōmichō and Hakodate's morning market are the two on this page. They have different origins — one a merchants' quarter centuries old, one a postwar farmers' pitch by a station — and they share a menu vocabulary worth learning before you sit down.

For how a wholesale market works, and why it is different, start from the markets overview.

## Where they came from

**Ōmichō, Kanazawa.** The market's own site introduces it as the kitchen of Kanazawa's people for about 300 years, since the days of the Kaga domain, with about 170 shops crowded along narrow lanes near Musashi-ga-tsuji in the city centre, selling fresh seafood, vegetables, meat and more. Locals call it *Omichō*.

**Hakodate morning market.** The market federation tells its history year by year. In 1945, after the war, farmers began selling vegetables in front of Hakodate station. In 1946 they moved onto the street; by 1947 it was an open-air market where producers and consumers traded. In 1956 the producers bought 1,997 m² of city land and formed a cooperative. In 1987 six cooperatives formed the federation, and in 2006 "Hakodate Asaichi" was recognised as a trademark. Today the market counts about 250 shops — about 150 federation members and 100 others.

Whether either market is a wholesale market in the legal sense, I could not confirm, and I have not found an official definition of "town market". What they say about themselves is the description used here.

## What makes them different from Toyosu

- **Anyone can buy.** At a wholesale market such as Toyosu, the farm ministry and Tokyo both say the general public cannot buy on the trading floor. Ōmichō and Hakodate exist to sell to the public.
- **They keep shop hours, not auction hours.** Hakodate's morning market trades all year. Each shop sets its own hours: from 06:00 between January and April, from 05:00 between May and December, closing a little after 14:00. The Hakodate Asaichi Hiroba building, run by a local farm cooperative, is open 05:00–13:30. At Ōmichō, hours are set shop by shop and listed on each shop's page.
- **They name the fish's origin as a selling point.** Ōmichō's Kaisendon Ichiba describes bowls built on seasonal fish bought daily at the market and on Hokuriku specialities such as *nodoguro* (blackthroat seaperch) and white shrimp. Ikiikitei says its fish comes straight from Kanazawa port. These are the shops' own descriptions.

## The seafood bowl, as the menus describe it

Ōmichō's list of restaurants includes several with *kaisendon* in the name — Kaisendon Ichiba, Uouma, Hirai (two branches), Hikariya and others. Hakodate's Donburi Yokochō market, a minute's walk from JR Hakodate station, lists 19 shops.

**Named by how many and who chooses.** Hirai offers about 20 bowls. Its most popular, by its own account, is a special bowl with tuna, crab, sweet shrimp and sea urchin; it also has a mini bowl, a tempura bowl and an eel bowl, and names others after the region — "Noto Kiwami", "Kanazawa Zanmai". In Donburi Yokochō the names describe the deal: "three-colour, choose any three toppings", "ten colours", "seven-colour morning market bowl", "chef's choice", "all salmon".

**Some have no price.** Hirai lists its sea urchin and salmon roe bowl as *jika*, market price, between bowls with fixed prices. A Donburi Yokochō restaurant does the same for live squid sashimi. My reading, not an official one, is that these are the items whose cost moves with the day's catch. Either way, ask before ordering.

## What a bowl costs

Not given here, on purpose. The shop pages for Hirai, Kaisendon Ichiba, Ikiikitei, Inoya and five or six Donburi Yokochō restaurants all post prices in yen, but none says whether tax is included, and Ōmichō's restaurant list does not say either. Hakodate's federation runs a "Select Asaichi" scheme whose page says all featured prices include tax, but the menu file was offline when checked. This site only gives tax-included prices it can verify, so read the menu at the door.

## Getting there

Donburi Yokochō is, by its own site, one minute on foot from JR Hakodate station, and the morning market grew up in front of the station. Ōmichō is near Musashi-ga-tsuji in central Kanazawa. Fares and travel times are not on this page yet; they will be added from the operators' own fare pages.

## The town market and the Tokyo market

- **Origins.** Toyosu is where fish from across Japan is auctioned to licensed buyers. Hakodate's market began with farmers selling vegetables by the station, and Ōmichō calls itself the city's kitchen.
- **Days.** Toyosu's inner market closes on Sundays, holidays and market holidays. Hakodate's morning market trades all year.
- **Who chose the fish.** Kanazawa shops point to Hokuriku fish and Kanazawa port. In Tokyo, the city says Senkyaku Banrai's restaurants cook with fish chosen by the market's intermediate wholesalers. No official source compares the two, and neither claim proves anything about quality.
- **Price.** I could not verify tax-included bowl prices in either place, so I cannot say which is cheaper.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hakodate's morning market is a fish market."** It began as a farmers' vegetable market in 1945.
- **"It's open all morning."** Shops open at 05:00 or 06:00 depending on the season and close a little after 14:00, each to its own hours.
- **"Ōmichō is for tourists."** By its own account it has been Kanazawa's kitchen for about 300 years, and it still sells vegetables and meat as well as fish.
- **"Market price means expensive."** It means the price is not printed. Ask.
- **"Local fish" is a guarantee.** It is the shop's own description. Read it as a claim.
