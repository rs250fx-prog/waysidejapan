Yakiniku is how most Japanese people eat beef out: at a table with a grill in the middle, cooking small pieces themselves, with rice and a cold beer and a timer running on an all-you-can-eat course. It is also the one great Japanese beef dish that Japan did not invent alone. It came out of the Korean communities of the postwar black markets, and its menu still speaks a little Korean.

## Where it came from

The Osaka tourism board's history of Tsuruhashi is the standard origin. After the war some 3,000 street traders gathered around Tsuruhashi station and formed a black market; the traders' federation was founded in 1947; the neighbouring Korean district, long known as the "Chōsen market", was a Korean residents' grocery market that became a tourist destination after the 2002 World Cup and the Korean-culture boom. Tsuruhashi is today, in the board's words, "the mecca of yakiniku". The industry's own account, given to the regulator in 2010, is that yakiniku is "a postwar food" whose first menus listed only *rōsu* and *karubi*. The national Yakiniku Association was founded in 1992, became a cooperative in 1998, and declared 29 August — *ya-ki-ni-ku* in Japanese number-puns — Yakiniku Day.

The format then changed three times. In the 1970s Jojoen, opened in Roppongi in 1976, served beef tongue with salt and lemon; the trade press credits its chairman Arai Taidō with inventing *tan-shio*, now the first thing ordered at every table in Japan. In March 1980 a Nagoya company, Shinpo, began selling the *mu-en* smokeless roaster — a grill with a downdraught — which took yakiniku out of smoke-filled rooms and into shopping centres. And in January 1996 a small shop opened at the wrong end of a Sangenjaya shopping street in Tokyo, selling yakiniku cheaply enough to eat on a weekday; it became Gyūkaku, and the all-you-can-eat course became the genre's default. Yakiniku King followed in 2007 (first shop in Nonoichi, Ishikawa), and in August 2018 Yakiniku Like opened in Shinbashi with one grill per seat — "yakiniku fast food" for one — reaching 72 shops by 2026.

## What makes it yakiniku

**You cook.** Thin pieces of beef, offal and vegetables, on a gas or charcoal grill set into the table, dipped in a sweet soy *tare* or eaten with salt and lemon, with rice and *kimchi* and *namul* on the side.

**The words are menu words.** In 2010 the Consumer Affairs Agency asked the association to stop shops selling other cuts as "wagyu rōsu" after finding one chain's "rōsu" was another cut about a third of the time. The association's explanation is the key to reading a yakiniku menu: *rōsu* (lean, from "roast") and *karubi* (fatty, Korean for rib) were the only two items on the first postwar menus and became categories rather than anatomy; retail cut names were regulated in 1977 but restaurants were not covered. *Horumon*, offal, has a folk etymology — *hōru-mon*, "the stuff you throw away" — that a ministry regional document records as a local tradition and that nobody has proved. The menu is honest now about what is loin and what is not, but it is still a menu.

**The ID on the wall.** Restaurants serving yakiniku, sukiyaki, shabu-shabu or steak must display the ten-digit traceability number of the beef. It is usually on a small board by the till.

**Tan-shio first.** Tongue, salt, lemon, then the fatty cuts, then the lean, then rice — the order every Japanese table follows, and a 1970s invention.

## Why Tsuruhashi — and everywhere

A postwar market, a Korean community with a grilling culture and access to the offal that Japanese butchers discarded, and a city that eats standing up. The smokeless grill and the buffet are what carried it from Tsuruhashi to every suburb: Teikoku Databank credits yakiniku and heavy-pork-bone chains with most of the growth in restaurant numbers in 2024. Yakiniku is now, by count, the way Japan eats beef.

- **Gyūkaku** (Colowide): all-you-can-eat ¥3,058 (cheapest, 90 minutes) to ¥3,718 (standard) and ¥4,158 (top). Founded 1996, Sangenjaya.
- **Yakiniku King** (Monogatari Corp.): ¥3,278 to ¥3,828 "and up", 100 minutes, prices varying by shop. Founded 2007.
- **Jojoen**: the upmarket chain, 77 directly run shops, 52 in greater Tokyo. Lunch set ¥3,200 at its Shinjuku branch; weekday standard course ¥8,000 with service included; service charge at dinner otherwise.
- **Yakiniku Like**: one grill per seat, 72 shops; opened at ¥1,210 before tax for a rib-and-skirt set in 2018.
- **Tsuruhashi**: hundreds of independent shops around the station; prices are by the plate and not published centrally.

Drinks are extra everywhere, and the course timer is enforced.

## How to do it

1. Choose the course, or order by plate if you know what you want. Rice and soup are usually separate.
2. Tan-shio first, while the grill is clean. Salt and lemon, not sauce.
3. Fatty cuts (*karubi*) next; they flare, so lift them early. Lean (*rōsu*, *harami*) after.
4. Use the tongs for raw meat and your chopsticks for cooked; shops give you both for a reason.
5. Offal last, and well done.
6. The all-you-can-eat clock starts at your first order. Last orders ten to fifteen minutes before the end.

## Yakiniku away from Tsuruhashi

The dish homogenised as it spread. Tsuruhashi's shops are small, smoky, by-the-plate and often run by families who have been there since the market; the suburban chains are smokeless, timed, and the same from Hokkaido to Kyushu. Tokyo's Korean quarter, Shin-Ōkubo, is a later formation and is as much about Korean food as about yakiniku. Abroad, "Japanese BBQ" is the chain form exported, and Korean barbecue is a different lineage of the same idea; in Japan the two are distinguished by the beef (Japanese wagyu, cut small), the *tare*, and the rice. What stays constant everywhere is the first order: tongue, salt, lemon, a Roppongi idea from the 1970s.

## Getting there

Tsuruhashi is on the Osaka Loop line and the Kintetsu line from Namba; the shops begin inside the station's arcades. Jojoen's head shop is in Roppongi; the buffet chains are at every suburban junction. Shin-Ōkubo is one stop from Shinjuku on the Yamanote line.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yakiniku is Korean barbecue."** It descends from it and speaks some of its words; the dish Japan eats is a Japanese restaurant genre with wagyu, a smokeless grill and a timer.
- **"Rōsu means loin."** It means the lean category on a yakiniku menu, by the trade's own account to the regulator.
- **"Tan-shio is traditional."** 1970s, Roppongi.
- **"All-you-can-eat is low quality."** It is standardised, and it is how the country eats beef; the ID number on the wall is the same law as at the steakhouse.
