Oden is a pot of items simmered in stock. It did not start that way. It started as tofu on a skewer, grilled and spread with miso, and the farm ministry’s pages show that some regions still keep a bit of that, while others went their own way with the broth, the items and what goes on the side. This page follows the split: Tokyo, Kansai and Aichi, then Shizuoka, Kanazawa and Himeji.

For soups and hotpots in general, start from the soups and hotpots overview. Convenience-store oden, with its regional broths, has its own konbini oden page and is not repeated here.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry traces oden to *tofu dengaku*: tofu cut into blocks, put on bamboo skewers and grilled. The word "oden", it says, is court women’s language — the polite *o* added to *dengaku*, with the *-gaku* dropped. In the Edo period, dengaku spread with ordinary people as a side dish, grilled with miso spread on it. The simmered oden became common from the modern era on.

A second ministry feature adds the next steps: the range of ingredients grew in the Meiji era, oden spread in Kansai in the Taishō era, and regional flavours and local products turned it into home cooking across the country.

The ministry’s Aichi page puts the split in one line: Edo developed oden simmered in soy, while Aichi kept the habit of putting miso on it.

## What makes each one what it is

**Tokyo.** Stock of katsuobushi and kombu, seasoned with dark soy sauce and other things, the ministry says. What marks it out is *chikuwabu*, a tube of wheat dough, and mustard on the side. It is mostly an autumn-and-winter food, but some long-established Tokyo shops serve it all year. Other Kanto items the ministry names are *hanpen*, beef tendon and fish balls (*tsumire*), in a dark broth. Tokyo is described here as the representative of the Kanto style, not as its birthplace.

**Kansai.** In Kansai the simmered kind has been called *kantō-daki* to tell it apart from the original oden, the grilled dengaku, the ministry says. Its oden feature describes Osaka’s broth as light in colour, with kantō-daki simmered in katsuobushi and soy sauce. Kyoto likes tofu products, Kyoto vegetables and ginkgo nuts, and uses kombu with light soy sauce or a white dashi. Where Kanto style ends and Kansai style begins, and the history of the name kantō-daki beyond that explanation, I could not find in the sources.

**Aichi.** *Miso oden* is items simmered on skewers or in a pot, then dipped in a miso sauce. The ministry says it is either dipped in the sauce from an earthenware miso pot or has the sauce poured over it. One of the two Hatcho miso specifications lists miso oden among the dishes the miso typically seasons.

**Shizuoka.** The ministry places Shizuoka oden in Shizuoka city. The items — black *hanpen*, beef tendon, radish, konnyaku, egg, kombu — go on skewers and simmer in a dark broth made from beef tendon. They are finished with green laver and *dashiko* (fish powder) and served with mustard. It is said to have begun in the Taishō era, when beef tendon and pork offal that had been thrown away were used as items and became popular, and to have spread after the Second World War. Fish from Suruga Bay made fish-paste products a natural ingredient. What black hanpen is made from is not in the ministry’s page, so I have not said.

**Kanazawa.** The ministry’s oden feature only names Toyama and Kanazawa as places with *kurumafu* (wheel-shaped wheat gluten) among their items. The detail comes from Ishikawa’s tourism site, *Hot Ishikawa* (the operator is not named on the page I read):

- **Kurumafu** — fresh gluten wrapped round a rod and baked, a traditional Kanazawa food; long-running shops soak it in a stock they keep topping up.
- **Bai-gai** — a whelk, served in its shell at some shops, a signature item.
- **Akamaki** — a fish cake with a red spiral.
- **Kani-men** — the shell of a female snow crab (*kōbako-gani*) packed with its meat and both kinds of roe. Only in winter, from about early November to around 29 December, and only on days when there is fishing.

The site is clear that shops differ: in the stock (kombu, katsuobushi, dried sardines) and in how they use soy sauce, and some serve a dark Kanto-style broth.

**Himeji.** Around Himeji city in Hyōgo, the ministry says, oden is eaten not with mustard but with ginger-soy — grated ginger mixed into soy sauce, in a small dish on the side. Himeji has both a sweet, dark kantō-daki style and a light style; either is "Himeji oden" if eaten with ginger-soy. The habit is thought to come from the area having produced ginger and soy sauce, which the ministry gives as a likely background, not a certainty. The name "Himeji oden" was coined in 2006 by local volunteers. As of 2021 more than 100 shops in the city served it, and it appears in school lunches.

## How to eat it

What the sources describe, by place:

- **Tokyo** — with mustard.
- **Aichi** — dipped in miso sauce from a pot, or with the sauce poured over.
- **Shizuoka** — sprinkled with green laver and fish powder, with mustard; bought one skewer at a time at cheap-sweet shops (*dagashiya*) and delicatessens.
- **Himeji** — with ginger-soy instead of mustard.

## Not all of Japan’s oden is this

The ministry’s feature names many regional styles; this page has covered the six with the most detail in the sources. The Toyama items it mentions, beyond kurumafu, are not described. Oden in convenience stores also changes by region; that is on the konbini oden page.

## Oden away from its town

I could not find a public source that compares eating these odens where they come from with eating them elsewhere on the same terms, and this page does not say which is better. What the sources show is how each place serves its own:

- **Aichi** — the ministry notes that convenience-store oden in the prefecture often comes with miso sauce, and miso oden is served in school lunches.
- **Shizuoka** — sold by the single skewer at cheap-sweet shops, which the ministry says is how children grow up with it, and there are oden streets in the city centre.
- **Himeji** — over 100 shops in the city as of 2021.
- **Kanazawa** — the kani-men is seasonal and depends on the day’s fishing; the long-running shops’ topped-up stock is what they are known for.

None of the official sources I used gave tax-included prices, so there are none here. Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## What people get wrong

- **"Oden was always simmered."** It began as tofu grilled with miso; the simmered form spread in the modern era.
- **"Kantō-daki is a different dish."** It is Kansai’s name for simmered oden, used to keep it apart from the grilled original.
- **"Oden comes with mustard."** In Himeji it comes with ginger-soy, in Aichi with miso sauce.
- **"Kanazawa oden has one broth."** The tourism site says each shop does it differently, and some use a dark Kanto-style broth.
- **"Shizuoka oden is a restaurant dish."** The ministry describes it bought by the skewer at cheap-sweet shops.
