*Himono* is the Japanese word for dried fish, and it covers a wide range: split horse mackerel, dried squid, dried sardines, salmon strips, *karasumi*. What they share is a reason. Fish spoil fast, and drying slows that down. This page covers why drying works, the main methods, how the fish is split, and how dried fish changed once refrigeration arrived. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods defines himono as seafood dried whole, or after the guts and gills are removed, to make it keep. It does not give a date for when the practice began, and I did not find one in an official source, so this page gives none.

One specific origin is given. The ministry says the Ainu cut salmon into thin lengthwise strips, washed them in seawater and hung them under the eaves, and that this is said to be the origin of *sake-toba*, dried salmon strips.

## What makes it himono

**Why drying works.** Seafood holds a lot of water, the ministry says, so its own digestive enzymes and bacteria change and spoil it easily. Drying reduces the water, and that holds both back.

**Four ways to dry.**

- **Sun-drying** (*tenpi kansō*) — the simplest. Freshness, temperature, humidity and time all have to be judged, which takes skill.
- **Salt-drying** (*shio-boshi*) — the fish is salted, or soaked in brine of 10–20% salt (*tatejio*), before drying. The strength and time depend on the species and size. The ministry says the fish matures while it is in the salt, which gives it a particular flavour.
- **Boil-drying** (*nie-boshi*) — boiled first, then dried. Boiling stops the enzymes and kills the bacteria, so it dries more easily.
- **Grill-drying** (*yaki-boshi*) — grilled first, then dried.

**The kinds, with the ministry's examples.**

- **Plain-dried** (*su-boshi*): *surume* (squid), *bōdara* (cod), *migaki-nishin* (herring), dried nori.
- **Salt-dried** (*shio-boshi*): whole dried round herring (*urume maru-boshi*), split horse mackerel (*aji hiraki-boshi*), *karasumi*.
- **Seasoned and dried**: *mirin-boshi*.
- **Boil-dried** (*nie-boshi*): dried sardines (*niboshi*), *shirasu-boshi*, dried scallop adductors.
- **Grill-dried** (*yaki-boshi*): *yaki-ago*, grilled flying fish.
- **Smoked**, and **freeze-dried** (*tōkan*): *mentai* and *kanten* are the ministry's examples of the latter.

**How it is split.** Most dried fish today is split open from the belly (*hara-biraki*). The ministry notes a custom for *kamasu* and *sayori* of leaving the head on and splitting from the back, called *Odawara-biraki*.

## How it changed

The ministry says that today, freezing, refrigeration and packaging are used alongside drying, which allows a lower-salt, higher-moisture dried fish called *ichiya-boshi*, "dried overnight". Machine drying also means producers no longer depend on the weather.

Whether ichiya-boshi needs to be kept in the fridge, and how that compares with fully dried goods, I could not confirm from an official source, so I do not give storage advice.

## Sake-toba, step by step

The farm ministry gives the method for salmon toba:

1. Cut half a salmon into long thin strips.
2. Soak them in brine.
3. Lay them on a drying net for about a week.

It is made when the air is below 10°C, from late autumn to before spring.

## Not all of Japan's dried sea food is fish

The same ministry guide puts dried seaweed in the same family. It says Japan's coastal waters have about 1,500 kinds of seaweed, about 50 of them eaten, and that seaweed was listed as a tax item under the Taihō Code. In the Edo period kombu, nori, wakame, tengusa, hijiki and funori spread across the country, helped by being light once dried. Seaweed is processed mainly by plain drying, ash drying, boil-drying, sheet drying and salting.

## Himono away from the coast

I could not find an official source comparing dried fish where it is made with dried fish in Tokyo, so this page does not compare them. The sources show salt-drying and belly-split fish as common across Japan, and the Odawara split as a local custom.

I could not verify a tax-included price for any dried fish.

## What people get wrong

- **"Himono is just salted fish."** Salt-drying is one method; fish is also sun-dried plain, boiled then dried, or grilled then dried.
- **"All dried fish is hard and keeps forever."** The ministry describes modern ichiya-boshi as lower in salt and higher in water, made possible by cold storage.
- **"Dried fish is always split from the back."** Most is split from the belly; the back split with the head on is a custom for certain fish.
