*Sara udon* — "plate noodles" — is champon's soupless sibling. It comes from the same restaurant in Nagasaki, and from the same man, and today it comes in two forms that people in Nagasaki keep apart by name. The two main sources on it, the farm ministry and the restaurant, agree on where it started and describe the second form differently. This page sets out both.

For champon in general, start from the champon overview.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry says sara udon is held to have been made in 1899 by Chin Heijun of Shikairou, the man who devised champon, as "champon without soup". Shikairou's own pages say the same: Chin Heijun created it as a variation on champon.

**The name.** The ministry says that in an age when noodles were always served in a deep bowl, serving them on a plate (*sara*) surprised people, and the name is said to come from that. Shikairou says the name came from serving a soupless noodle dish on a flat plate.

## What makes it sara udon

There are two kinds, and the ministry says people in Nagasaki distinguish them as *futomen* (thick-noodle) and *hosomen* (thin-noodle) sara udon.

**Thick-noodle sara udon.** The original. The ministry describes it as the same thick noodles as champon, fried, and cooked until no soup is left. Shikairou describes the method: the champon noodles are first fried on their own, then cooked with the toppings so that a small amount of soup is absorbed into the noodles.

**Thin-noodle sara udon.** Thin noodles topped with a thickened sauce of mixed ingredients. The ministry says this kind has become common in recent years. Shikairou says that later, to make it simpler to cook, the noodle was made thinner and deep-fried, with a thickened sauce of vegetables, pork and seafood poured over it, and that because it was easy to make it spread through Nagasaki's homes and then across Japan.

**Two descriptions.** The ministry calls the thin-noodle kind a different side of the dish; Shikairou presents it as a development made for simplicity. The two accounts are not the same, and this site does not choose.

So the three ways of cooking a noodle in this family are visible on one restaurant's menu: champon is stewed in soup, thick-noodle sara udon is fried until the soup is gone, and thin-noodle sara udon is deep-fried and covered with a thickened sauce.

## How to eat it

The ministry says both kinds are customarily eaten with Nagasaki's own Worcestershire sauce, or with vinegar. It does not say one is preferred.

It also records a household habit. At the Bon festival, at New Year and when guests come, families order a large platter of sara udon delivered from a nearby Chinese restaurant, and some families like to eat what is left the next day.

## Not all of Nagasaki's noodles are this

Champon is the stewed dish; sara udon is the fried one. Within champon itself, the ministry names two main kinds in the prefecture — the rich, pork-bone Nagasaki champon and the light, chicken-bone Obama champon. Sara udon is described as coming from Nagasaki champon's restaurant; I found nothing on an Obama version.

## Sara udon away from Nagasaki

The ministry says sara udon is also widely eaten in other prefectures. Given that people in Nagasaki distinguish thick and thin noodles, and Shikairou says the thin kind is the one that spread across Japan, the sara udon you meet outside Nagasaki may often be the thin kind. I found no figures on how often, so treat that as a possibility, not a finding. When you order, look at which noodle the menu names.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sara udon is made with udon."** It is made with champon's noodles — thick and fried, or thin and deep-fried.
- **"There is one sara udon."** People in Nagasaki, the ministry says, name two.
- **"The deep-fried one is the original."** Both sources say the original was the thick noodle, fried until the soup was gone.
- **"Sauce on noodles is strange."** The ministry records Worcestershire sauce or vinegar as the custom for both kinds.
