Anago, the conger eel, and hamo, the pike conger, are long, eel-like fish that Japanese cooking treats in completely different ways depending on the city. In Tokyo, anago is simmered and brushed with a thick sauce for sushi. On Miyajima, it is grilled and laid over rice. In Kyoto, hamo is the summer fish, and it cannot be eaten until a cook has cut through its small bones. This page covers all three. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Where it came from

**Edomae anago.** The farm ministry says that in the Edo period, Tokyo Bay was fed by several rivers carrying nutrient-rich water, a good place for fish to grow. Anago, together with *kisu* and *megochi*, took root in Edo food culture as sushi and tempura.

**Miyajima's anago-meshi.** Miyajima and Hiroshima city are good anago grounds, and the fish has long been eaten there. A Hiroshima domain gazetteer of the Bunsei era of the Edo period, the *Geihan Tsūshi*, records anago as a local product with a good flavour. The dish itself dates from 1901 (Meiji 34), four years after the Sanyō Railway opened, when it was sold as a station lunch near Miyajimaguchi station. In the Shōwa 30s (1955–64), many tourist and school-trip buses came to Miyajimaguchi and it became known as a "bus lunch". Today it is a Miyajima specialty.

**Kyoto's hamo.** The farm ministry lists Kyoto city and the Chūtan and Tango areas as where its hamo dishes are handed down. It calls hamo indispensable to Kyoto cuisine. A late-Edo cookbook, *Hamu Hyakuchin*, carries more than 100 hamo dishes.

## What makes each one

**Edomae anago: simmered, with tsume.** The farm ministry describes the traditional method:

1. Live anago is killed and filleted, and the slime is removed with salt and hot water to suppress the smell.
2. It is simmered gently in sake, sugar and soy, then cut to the size of a sushi topping.
3. The head and bones are made into stock, seasoned, and reduced to make *tsume*.
4. The anago goes on the rice and is brushed with tsume.

The ministry calls removing the slime, simmering and finishing the tsume a craftsman's skill, and quotes the saying that you can tell a good sushi shop by its anago — because freshness matters so much.

**Miyajima anago: grilled.** The guts and backbone are removed, and the anago is grilled *kabayaki*-style while being brushed with soy, mirin and sake. It is cut into angled slices and laid over rice cooked in stock. Sometimes the anago is steamed instead. The anago caught around Ōno Seto in Hatsukaichi, where the current is fast, is fatty, flavourful and tender, the ministry says, and is called "Seto anago".

So the difference between the two is in the sources themselves: Edomae simmers and brushes with tsume; Miyajima grills and lays it on rice.

**Kyoto hamo: honekiri.** Hamo has many small bones, so it needs a technique called *honekiri*, bone-cutting. The farm ministry says that in Kyoto a cook is considered fully trained once they have learned it. How many cuts are made, and to what spacing, I could not confirm from an official source, so this page gives no number. The Kyoto governor, in a 2022 message, wrote that carefully bone-cut hamo, briefly blanched, is as beautiful as a white flower — his own impression.

## When to eat them

- **Edomae anago.** Traditionally late spring to summer, especially July and August. With modern distribution, anago arrives from all over Japan and is eaten year-round.
- **Miyajima anago.** Year-round, with July–August and November, when it is fatty, considered especially good.
- **Hamo.** Early summer to autumn.

## How to eat them

**Anago.** At a sushi shop, as simmered anago brushed with tsume — a standard Edomae topping. On Miyajima, as *anago-meshi*, slices of grilled anago over rice cooked in stock. For sushi itself, start from the sushi overview.

**Hamo.** Because it takes so much work, the farm ministry says, hamo is rarely cooked at home and is usually eaten at *ryōtei*, *kappō* and other restaurants.

**The Gion Festival.** Two sources describe the link, in different ways:

- The farm ministry says the Gion Festival is also known as the "hamo festival", and hamo dishes are served as festival food.
- The Kyoto governor's 2022 message says, as something he has heard, that in the "Hamo Dōchū" organised by the Awaji Island tourism association hamo is sometimes offered at Yasaka Shrine, and that because the festival coincides with the season when hamo is good, it is sometimes called the hamo festival.

Neither source explains how hamo first came to be linked to the festival, so I do not.

I could not verify a tax-included price for anago or hamo at any shop.

## Not all of Japan's anago and hamo is this

The farm ministry's pages describe Tokyo, Miyajima and Kyoto. I did not read an official source on hamo dishes in Osaka or elsewhere, so other regions are left out here.

## Away from the sea, and away from the source

**Kyoto is not a hamo fishing ground.** The farm ministry says most of the hamo eaten in Kyoto is caught in the Seto Inland Sea or the Genkai Sea. Hamo is hardy and could be carried alive over long distances, and that is given as the reason it took root in Kyoto. Kyoto's part is the bone-cutting.

**Tokyo Bay anago is scarce.** The ministry says the fish caught in Tokyo Bay today are limited and declining, but anago is still landed there and highly rated nationally. Most anago now comes from all over Japan. Where the anago served in Tokyo restaurants comes from, I could not find in an official source.

**Miyajima has a name for its own.** "Seto anago" is the local name for the fish from Ōno Seto, and the dish spread as a station lunch.

## What people get wrong

- **"Anago is a cheap unagi."** The ministry describes a different fish — less fatty, lighter — and one not yet farmed. For unagi, start from the unagi overview.
- **"Anago is always grilled like unagi."** In Edomae sushi it is simmered and brushed with tsume.
- **"Kyoto’s hamo is caught near Kyoto."** Most of it is brought in alive from the Seto Inland Sea and Genkai Sea.
- **"Hamo can be served like any fillet."** Its small bones have to be cut first.
