The packet is where instant noodles began: a dried block of noodles in a bag, finished with hot water or a minute in a pan. In Japan it is now the smaller half of the market — about one serving in three, by the industry association's fiscal 2025 figures — but it is the older half, and every cup on a convenience-store shelf descends from it.

For instant noodles in general — the definition, the hot water, the prices and the bin — start from the [instant noodles overview](/en/eat/instant-noodles/).

## Where it came from

The story as Nissin tells it, in its chronicle of its founder, Momofuku Ando: in 1957 the credit union Ando chaired collapsed and he lost his assets. Near Osaka station he had seen a long queue at a black-market stall for a single bowl of ramen, and he became convinced of the demand. In a shed in the back garden of his home in Ikeda, Osaka, he spent about a year, alone, sleeping four hours a night on average, working on a ramen that could be eaten at home as soon as there was hot water. One day he watched his wife frying tempura and arrived at the idea of frying the noodles — "instant oil-heat drying", in the company's phrase. Frying drives the water out, so the noodle keeps for half a year; hot water then soaks back in through the holes the water left.

He set himself five goals. The industry association (JIFFA) lists them as tasty, keeps well, easy to cook, fairly priced and safe; Nissin's own words are a taste you do not tire of, keeping well enough to stock in a home kitchen, no effort, cheap, and safe and hygienic. Different words, same five points.

Chicken Ramen went on sale on **25 August 1958**. JIFFA, Nissin, WINA and the Cup Noodles Museum in Ikeda all give that date. It cost ¥35 a packet at a time when a ball of udon cost ¥6, and wholesalers were reluctant to stock it; both JIFFA and Nissin say it sold out soon after launch. Pour on hot water, wait two minutes: JIFFA says people called it "magic ramen".

Nissin calls it the world's first instant ramen, and JIFFA says it was the first to establish the basic industrial process and succeed in mass production. I did not find a competing claim, from Japan or elsewhere, in the official sources I read, and I do not try to settle the question here.

## What makes it a packet noodle

**The process.** JIFFA names four steps that Chicken Ramen established: making the noodle, steaming it, seasoning it, and frying it dry.

**The strand.** JIFFA's trivia: a packet holds about 80 strands averaging about 65 cm, which would stretch to about 51 m end to end.

**Seasoned, then a sachet.** The first packets were pre-seasoned. In April 1962, JIFFA says, Myojo (with its bamboo-shoot Myojo Ramen) and Toyo Suisan (with Maruchan Hi Ramen) switched entirely to a separate soup sachet, and sales took off. It was more work than the seasoned kind, but the soup could be deeper and you could add your own ingredients; JIFFA says it was popular with housewives in the cities.

**Fried or not.** From September 1968, beginning with Diamond Foods' Sapporo Yanagi-men (Diamond was a Myojo subsidiary), makers added non-fried noodles, and in 1969 Myojo, Nissin, Sanyo, Toyo Suisan and Acecook all followed. JIFFA says the non-fried noodle has no oil to go stale and a texture closer to fresh noodles.

**The white specks.** Nissin's FAQ says the palm oil used to fry the noodle sets white at room temperature and can occasionally show on the surface, and that this is not a quality problem.

## Why it spread

JIFFA puts it down partly to the moment. "Instant" was the great buzzword of 1960–61; Morinaga launched its instant coffee in 1960. JIFFA also credits Chicken Ramen with making the word *rāmen* replace *chūka soba* in everyday speech, a story the [ramen page](/en/eat/ramen/) tells from the ramen side.

The numbers moved fast. By JIFFA's account there were said to be more than 70 makers in 1961, over 100 by the end of 1963 and 360 by around the turn of 1964–65. Production went from 550 million servings in 1961 to 2 billion in 1963 and 2.5 billion in 1965.

## The brands that followed

Each company's own history, with JIFFA's dates where they add something:

- **Acecook:** began making instant noodles in 1959 with Peking Ramen; Wantanmen (instant wonton noodles) in 1963; Ekimae Ramen, a large 100 g block, in 1967 (JIFFA).
- **Myojo:** started development in 1959, launched Myojo Ajitsuke Ramen in 1960, a separate-soup Myojo Ramen in 1962 and Myojo Charumera in 1966 (September, says JIFFA). It joined the Nissin group in 2006.
- **Toyo Suisan:** Jirushi Ramen in 1961 and Hi Ramen in 1962, the year the "Maruchan" mark was born.
- **Sanyo Foods:** began instant noodles alongside dried noodles in 1961; Piyo Piyo Ramen in 1963; Nagasaki Tanmen in 1964, which the company calls the industry's first salt-flavoured and first regional ramen; Sapporo Ichiban (soy) in January 1966, then miso in 1968 and shio in 1971. JIFFA describes the first Sapporo Ichiban as garlicky, with dried spring onion.
- **Nissin:** Demae Iccho in 1968, with sesame chilli oil (JIFFA).

## What it costs

Makers' prices are published before tax, and shops set their own, so this page gives no current packet prices. The history has two numbers that do not agree. JIFFA says the ¥35 launch price later fell to ¥30 under fierce competition, and a 1970 rise took the suggested price back to ¥35. Nissin, describing 1971, says packet noodles were ¥25. Neither explains the gap — a list price against a shop price is one possibility — and I leave both standing.

The one current price I can give is the museum's. At the Cup Noodles Museum in Ikeda, the Chicken Ramen Factory costs ¥600 for elementary-school children and ¥1,200 from junior-high age up, tax included (checked 13 October 2026).

## How it is eaten

Nissin's current Chicken Ramen is an 85 g block. Its page gives two methods: pour on about 400 ml of hot water and wait three minutes, or simmer for one minute. In 1958 it was two minutes. Nissin's FAQ says "hot water" means water that has boiled.

The separate-soup packets, JIFFA says, left room to add your own ingredients. JIFFA's labelling rules say a photograph of a cooked packet must be marked "serving suggestion" (調理例), so what you see on the front is not always what is in the bag. Nissin also sells packets meant to be made in a small bowl with 160 ml of hot water.

JIFFA gives a rough shelf life of about eight months for packets, longer than for cups.

## Nearby, and different

JIFFA says the "area" product began in 1979 with House Foods' Umakacchan, a Kyushu pork-bone flavour sold only in that region, and that other makers' Kyushu packets followed, along with Sukiyanen for Kansai and Umaissho for Hokkaido. In 1983 Myojo launched Ramen Kikō in four editions: Sapporo, Tokyo, Osaka and Hakata.

JIFFA's summary of the industry's fair-labelling code says a product may carry a place name such as "Sapporo ramen" or "Hakata ramen" only if it reproduces that style's features or uses at least 50% main ingredients from that area. I could not open the code itself, so this is JIFFA's account of it.

## Getting there

The Cup Noodles Museum in Ikeda, Osaka, stands in the town where Nissin says Chicken Ramen was invented. Entry is free; it opens 9:30–16:30 (last entry 15:30) and is closed on Tuesdays. The Chicken Ramen Factory prices are above, and the My Cup Noodles Factory is ¥500 a cup. The Yokohama museum is on the [cup noodles page](/en/eat/instant-noodles-cup/). I have not confirmed the route from an official page, so none is given here.

## Packet noodles away from Japan

JIFFA says Japanese makers began expanding abroad in earnest from 1970, and that Japanese technology moved to the United States, Asia and Europe. Toyo Suisan founded Maruchan, Inc. in Irvine, California in 1972 and built a ramen plant there in 1977. Myojo set up an American company in 1978 and licensed its technology to Campbell Soup in 1986.

World consumption, by JIFFA's account, was around 15 billion servings in 1990, 50 billion in 2001 and 105.6 billion in 2013; WINA puts 2025 at 124.2 billion. JIFFA thinks the reason is that noodles blend easily with each country's own flavours.

## What people get wrong

- **"Instant noodles started as a cup."** The packet came 13 years earlier, in 1958.
- **"The packet in the picture is what you get."** The industry's rule is that a cooked photograph is a serving suggestion.
- **"The soup sachet was always there."** The 1958 packet was pre-seasoned; JIFFA dates the switch to separate soup to the rival brands of 1962.
- **"Instant ramen was always cheap."** It launched at ¥35 when a ball of udon was ¥6, and wholesalers hesitated.
