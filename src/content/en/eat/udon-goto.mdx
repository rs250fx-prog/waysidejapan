The Gotō islands lie off the far western edge of Nagasaki, and their udon is the opposite of the thick, square noodle most of Japan now thinks of as udon. It is dried, hand-stretched, round and thin — about 1.7 mm, says the Japan Tourism Agency — and it is brushed with camellia oil as it is stretched. On the islands it is eaten as *jigoku-daki*: boiled in an iron pot on the table and dipped, hot, into flying-fish stock with egg. The farm ministry says about 80% of what is made now leaves the islands, so most of it is eaten somewhere else.

## Where it came from

The story is the same everywhere; how firmly it is told is not.

- **The farm ministry**: Gotō was a port of call for Japan's envoy ships to Tang China, and the envoys, who studied many things in China, are *said* to have brought the original form. Recent research has produced the theory that its roots lie in a *suomian* noodle of Yantan, in Yongjia county, Zhejiang, made in a very similar way.
- **The Cultural Agency**, which named it a 100-year food in fiscal 2024, in its "traditional" category: about 1,200 years ago Kami-Gotō, known as a port of call for the envoy ships, received culture from the continent, and Gotō udon too is *said* to have come with the envoys.
- **Shin-Kamigotō town**: "there are various theories" about its origin; because Kami-Gotō was a port for the envoy ships, it is said to have been brought back from Tang. Nagasaki prefecture's tourism site likewise says the continent's noodle-making first reached Kami-Gotō in the envoys' time, adding "there are various theories".
- **The Gotō te-nobe udon cooperative**: its origin is becoming clear as going back to the Nara and Heian periods, and because the Yantan *suomian* is made in a very similar way, the theory that the envoys brought the method to Kami-Gotō first is "regarded as the most likely". It says over a thousand years.

None of the pages I read cites a document for the envoy story, and none offers a different one. This page does not choose.

**The phantom noodle.** The town says that because it was all made by hand, it was called the "phantom udon" and eaten only at celebrations. The cooperative says it was called that because so little was made. The town's tourism association says the Funasaki district, which some call its birthplace, still makes it by hand.

**The cooperative.** Founded on 31 January 1984 by twelve udon makers from the five towns of Kami-Gotō; it registered the shared brand *Nami no Ito* in 1986.

## What makes it Gotō udon

**The noodle.** The patent office's description, written by the trademark holder, says it is thicker than *hiyamugi* and thin for an udon, and that in Gotō "udon" once meant this noodle. The tourism agency says it is about 1.7 mm and round, thinner than ordinary square-cut udon. Under Japan's labelling standard, a dried noodle 1.7 mm or more across may be called udon; Gotō's packets say *te-nobe udon*, hand-stretched udon.

**The making**, as the cooperative describes it:

1. Flour is kneaded with the island's water and sea salt from the Gotō-nada, the salt adjusted to the day's temperature and humidity.
2. The dough is trodden underfoot about 500 times, on a stand made of *sendan* wood among others; this, it says, is where the bite comes from.
3. The disc of dough is cut in a spiral into a rope and coiled in a tub, with camellia oil brushed on each layer. A rope about 40 mm across is called *ōmaki*.
4. It is worked down by hand to about 10 mm, oiled again so it does not stick, and rested.
5. It is hung in a figure of eight on two bamboo poles and rested again in a *muro* box.
6. It is stretched and hung on a *hata* frame to dry. The northwesterly wind that blows over Kami-Gotō from late autumn to early spring was essential to the drying.
7. It is cut into eight and bagged.

The cooperative adds that part of the process is now done by machine.

**The camellia oil.** The cooperative says the oil stops the strands sticking as they are stretched, and that camellia oil, rich in oleic acid, is also said to slow oxidation. The tourism association says noodles coated in it stay firm when boiled. The tourism agency says the step is called *migaki*, that the oil keeps the noodle from breaking and gives a silky bite with spring, and that the noodles are finally dried in the sea wind.

**But not only camellia.** The cooperative's own standard label lists flour (made in Japan), salt, rapeseed oil, soybean oil and camellia oil. Per 100 g dry, 338 kcal and 3.7 g salt equivalent (estimated from a sample). Best before one year from manufacture. Wheat and soy are the declared allergens.

This is how [Inaniwa udon](/en/eat/udon-inaniwa/) differs most: Inaniwa uses no oil and is pressed flat. Both hang the noodle in a figure of eight on two poles. For hand-stretched sōmen, see [sōmen](/en/eat/somen/).

## Jigoku-daki and other ways to eat it

**From the pot.** The farm ministry calls *jigoku-daki* the standard local way. The dried noodles go into a bubbling iron pot and are eaten with *ago* stock from flying fish caught off Gotō; for a change, they are dipped into egg beaten with spring onion, bonito flakes and soy sauce. A scoop stick (*udon-sukui-bō*) lifts the noodles, because chopsticks find them hard to catch. It is also food for gatherings round a big pot. The ministry's recipe makes the dip from water, granulated ago stock, mirin, soy sauce and tamari, with *yuzu-koshō*, grated radish with chilli, ginger, spring onion and raw egg on the side. For ago, see [iriko and ago](/en/eat/dashi-iriko-ago/).

**Hot or cold.** The tourism agency says it can be eaten hot or cold, and that flying-fish stock is the usual dip locally. The town says chilled *zaru* udon in the hot months, jigoku-daki once the weather turns cool, and that some locals dip it in the liquid from simmered fish.

**The name.** The cooperative gives one story, and calls it something that is said: a traveller tasting it for the first time praised it as *shigoku oishii*, "utterly delicious", and was misheard as *jigoku oishii*, "hellishly delicious". The farm ministry's page gives no origin for the name. No source I read connects it with the volcanic "hells" of [Unzen](/en/soak/unzen/).

**Boiling.** The cooperative's jigoku-daki recipe boils 80–100 g a person in plenty of water for six to seven minutes. It also sells a thinner "three-minute" noodle, whose developer, the product page says, prefers it cold.

I found no official source for adding camellia oil at the table.

## On the islands

The cooperative runs Udon Chaya Yūmen Zanmai, five minutes' walk from Arikawa port, with 52 seats, open 11:00–14:30, closed irregularly. Its menu page did not open, so I have no prices. At the Funasaki Udon Denshōkan in Shin-Kamigotō you can try three stages — *kakeba*, *kobiki* and *hatakake* — and the Japan Heritage site says to book a week ahead.

## Gotō udon away from the islands

**The name is protected; the dish is not a GI.** "Gotō udon" (registration no. 5079155, for Gotō-made udon noodles) and "Gotō te-nobe udon" (no. 5079156, for Gotō-made hand-stretched udon noodles) are regional collective trademarks held by the cooperative, filed in May 2006 and registered on 21 September 2007, still in force. It is not among the farm ministry's geographical indications; Nagasaki's two are Taishū soba and Nagasaki karasumi. This is the reverse of Inaniwa, which has neither.

**Most of it leaves.** About 80% is shipped off the islands, the farm ministry says.

**In Tokyo.** Nagasaki's showroom, Nihonbashi Nagasaki-kan, did not mention Gotō udon on its home page when I checked, so I do not know whether it stocks it.

**The lists.** *Jigoku-daki (Gotō udon and Shimabara sōmen)* is one of the 30 dishes on the farm ministry's Nagasaki list. Gotō udon is a 100-year food (fiscal 2024), and Shin-Kamigotō's Gotō udon appears among the cultural properties of the Japan Heritage story "Border islands Iki, Tsushima and Gotō". The cooperative says it was added in 2025; I could not confirm the year on the Heritage site. For Nagasaki's other dishes, see [Kyushu's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/).

## What people get wrong

- **"The envoy story is proven."** Every source calls it said, a theory, or the most likely; none cites a record.
- **"It is made only with camellia oil."** The label lists rapeseed and soybean oil too.
- **"Jigoku means the hot springs."** No source ties it to Unzen; the cooperative's one story is a misheard compliment.
- **"It is one of the three great udon."** Nagasaki's tourism site and the cooperative say so; the farm ministry's Gunma page names Mizusawa as the third instead.
