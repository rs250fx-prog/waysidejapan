Milk is the least foreign-looking of the Western foods Japan adopted, and the one with the most official paperwork. It was promoted by the Meiji government, written into school lunches, and is now sold under labelling rules detailed enough that the category name on the carton tells you exactly what is inside. Most of the information on this page comes from J-Milk, the dairy industry’s association, and from the farm ministry.

For the wider group of Western foods made Japanese, start from the Western foods overview.

## Where it came from

J-Milk’s fact book on the history of milk in Japan sets out the Meiji beginnings:

- **1860.** Fukuzawa Yukichi’s English–Chinese word book, *Zōtei Ka-Ei Tsūgo*, lists three dairy words: butter, cheese and cream.
- **1867.** The physician Matsumoto Ryōjun submitted a proposal to the shogunate to promote raising cattle and sheep.
- **1870.** Matsumoto persuaded a relative by marriage to open Sakagawa Milk Shop in Akasaka, the first milk shop in Tokyo. At first its customers were foreigners only.
- **1870.** A cattle and horse company produced and distributed *Nikushoku no Setsu*, a pamphlet written by Fukuzawa praising milk, among other things as a medicine for many ills.
- **1871.** A newspaper reported in November that the Emperor Meiji, then 19, drank milk twice a day.
- **1872.** The scholar Kondō Yoshiki wrote *Gyūnyū-kō*, which J-Milk calls Japan’s first essay on milk.

The fact book says the new government campaigned for meat-eating and milk-drinking from its first days, that older people were reported to dislike animal milk, and that milk, with the beef hotpot, became a symbol of the new civilisation.

**Dairy farming moved north.** J-Milk explains that after the Meiji era, with no refrigeration or modern transport, fresh milk could only be had near the farm, so dairying grew up around the cities. As refrigeration and transport improved, dairy farmers spread to places further from the cities, and from the 1970s raw milk production grew in Hokkaido, whose climate and land suited it. In the other prefectures farms declined with urban development; their output peaked in 1992 and fell, and in 2010 Hokkaido’s production overtook that of all other prefectures combined.

## What makes it milk, in Japan

**Strict category names.** J-Milk and the farm ministry both explain that the kinds of milk, their composition standards, manufacturing, storage and labelling are set by a ministerial ordinance under the Food Sanitation Act and by an industry code on milk labelling recognised by the Fair Trade Commission. I could not open the text of the ordinance itself. On J-Milk’s account:

- **Gyūnyū (milk)** — 100% raw milk, at least 3.0% milk fat and 8.0% non-fat milk solids.
- **Low-fat milk** — milk fat from 0.5% to 1.5%; **non-fat milk** — under 0.5%. Both at least 8.0% non-fat milk solids.
- **Processed milk** (*kakō-nyū*) — raw milk with dairy products such as skimmed milk powder or butter added.
- **Milk beverage** (*nyū-inryō*) — mainly raw milk or dairy products, with non-dairy ingredients added, and at least 3.0% milk solids.

The farm ministry adds that anything in the category "milk" is unadjusted, and that only processed milk and milk beverages meeting conditions such as 8.0% non-fat milk solids may use the words "milk" or "乳" in a product name. Its example label reads 8.3% non-fat solids and 3.5% milk fat; that is an example, not the minimum.

**Special milk.** The ministry says *tokubetsu gyūnyū* (special milk) can only be made at facilities with a specific licence, and may only be pasteurised at 63–65°C for 30 minutes.

**The label.** J-Milk lists what must be shown: the category name, product name, non-fat milk solids, milk fat, ingredients, how it was sterilised, volume, date, storage, and the maker and plant. Milk made only from raw milk says "raw milk 100% (domestic)". A fair-trade mark on the label box shows it passed the code’s review, and the figures are checked three times a year by approved laboratories. J-Milk says milk sterilised at high temperature generally carries a best-before date, and low-temperature pasteurised milk, which spoils faster, a use-by date; each maker sets the date.

**The notch.** Gable-top cartons of 500 ml or more have a notch at the top of the roof so that "milk" can be identified by touch, for people with visual impairments.

**The litre that is a quart box.** J-Milk says the inside of a 1-litre carton measures 70 × 70 × 194 mm, about 950.6 ml on paper; it bulges when filled and takes 1,000 ml. The 70 mm base came from the United States, where paper cartons were first used, and matched the crates for milk bottles, 16 to a crate. The American carton holds a quart, 946 ml; the base stays the same and the height changes to fit each country’s unit. In Okinawa, J-Milk says, the 946 ml carton is the usual size, attributed to American influence. Why Japan chose the litre, I could not find.

**From tin can to carton.** J-Milk’s history of containers: in the early Meiji era milk was ladled from large tin cans, then delivered in small 180 ml cans — the start of home delivery. Narrow-necked glass bottles came in 1889. A 1928 regulation required pasteurisation, banned coloured bottles and brought in the clear wide-mouthed bottle with a paper cap, the start of today’s milk bottle. The first Tetra Classic container was sold in Japan in 1956, and paper cartons spread fast after the Tokyo Olympics with supermarkets and school lunches.

## School lunch

J-Milk dates the first school lunch in Japan to 1889, when monks at a private primary school in Tsuruoka, Yamagata, gave rice balls, grilled fish and pickles free to poor children. The first milk in a school lunch, it says, was at Kōjimachi primary school in Tokyo in 1920.

After the war, at the suggestion of the occupation authorities, aid of milk containing animal protein was chosen over wheat flour, and skimmed milk powder was donated by American private groups and by UNICEF. Schools served 22 g dissolved in 180 ml of hot water. At first the milk was carried to the classroom in large aluminium buckets and poured into bowls; then came 180 ml bottles, from 1970 200 ml bottles, and then paper cartons, partly because the bottles were heavy for younger children. In fiscal 2011, J-Milk says, citing government statistics, 25.6% of school-lunch milk came in bottles and 74.3% in cartons.

The farm ministry’s magazine says the drink changed from skimmed milk powder to milk. The sources I opened do not agree on the year, so this page does not give one.

J-Milk also notes that Japan’s demand for drinking milk peaks in June and September — June because it has no public holidays, so school milk is not interrupted — and is lowest at the year-end holidays and the end of the school year.

## Hokkaido and the rest

**Who makes it.** The farm ministry’s 2025 overview of Hokkaido puts its share of national raw milk at 58%, first in Japan; its 2024 table gives 4,233,050 t for Hokkaido out of 7,357,451 t nationally. Hokkaido farms average 158.9 dairy cows each, about 2.2 times the figure for other prefectures.

**What it becomes.** J-Milk says Hokkaido’s raw milk goes mainly into dairy products, and the other prefectures’ mainly into drinking milk, because Hokkaido is far from the main consuming areas and dairy products keep. More than half of Japan’s raw milk is used for drinking milk, yoghurt and the like; J-Milk puts Japan’s drinking share at 53% and calls it a drinking-milk country.

**How it travels.** Raw milk and milk packed at source are shipped from Hokkaido, Tōhoku, Kyushu and elsewhere to Kanto and Kansai, mostly by sea. From Hokkaido to Kanto the main carrier is the *Hokuren Maru*, two dedicated ships that run almost daily between Kushiro and Hitachi port in Ibaraki.

**Seasons.** Dairy cows like cool weather, so output falls in the heat. J-Milk says the other prefectures peak around April and bottom out in August–September; Hokkaido peaks from late May to June and bottoms out in late October to November. In summer, when drinking demand rises and other regions run short, more milk is shipped from Hokkaido; in winter, factories turn the surplus into dairy products. Hokkaido balances supply with butter and skimmed milk powder, which keep well. From 100 kg of raw milk, J-Milk calculates, you get about 3.8 kg of butter and about 8.8 kg of skimmed milk powder, so making butter yields about twice as much powder.

## Cheese, butter and soft-serve

**Cheese.** The farm ministry’s cheese supply table for fiscal 2025, published 17 July 2026, puts total cheese consumption at 315,198 t, down 3.4% on the year: 191,645 t natural cheese and 123,553 t processed cheese. Japan made 45,532 t of natural cheese and imported 241,644 t. Domestic cheese was 15.5% of all cheese consumed.

A page of the Agriculture and Livestock Industries Corporation, citing a cheese professionals’ association textbook, gives the history in outline: a dairy product called *so* appears in ancient texts around 700; the first domestic cheese was made at a facility in Hokkaido in 1875; full-scale production began in the early Shōwa period, with domestic processed cheese made and sold from about 1934–36; production stopped in the war and cheese reached ordinary homes after it. The page was too garbled for me to read the name of the 1875 facility or product.

**Butter.** What is on record is how butter fits the supply system above. I could not find official figures for the history of butter in Japan or how much is eaten.

**Soft-serve.** I could not find the start of soft-serve in Japan in any source I could open.

## Milk away from Hokkaido

The difference that is on record is one of use: Hokkaido milk mostly goes into dairy products, and milk from other regions is mostly drunk. J-Milk says milk sent from Hokkaido to Kanto goes mainly by sea. I found no official source on whether milk tastes different in Hokkaido and in Tokyo. The farm ministry says that when a product name includes a place of origin, such as "Hokkaido milk", it uses 100% raw milk from that place.

## What people get wrong

- **"Milk in Japan is all the same."** The category on the label — milk, low-fat, processed, milk beverage — is defined by law and an industry code.
- **"A milk drink is milk."** A *nyū-inryō* needs only 3.0% milk solids and can contain non-dairy ingredients.
- **"Milk came to Japan with the occupation."** Tokyo’s first milk shop opened in 1870, and school milk began in 1920. The occupation era brought skimmed milk powder to school lunches.
- **"A 1-litre carton is a litre box."** On paper it holds about 950.6 ml; it bulges to take a litre.
- **"Japanese cheese is mostly Japanese."** Domestic cheese was 15.5% of consumption in fiscal 2025.
