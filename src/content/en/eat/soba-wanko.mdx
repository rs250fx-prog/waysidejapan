Wanko soba is the only noodle dish in Japan with a referee. You sit; a server in an apron stands at your shoulder with a tray of small lacquer bowls, each holding a single mouthful of soba; the instant your bowl is empty, another mouthful drops into it with a cry of *hai, jan-jan!* and it does not stop until you put the lid on. It is Iwate's idea of hospitality, and it has become Iwate's idea of a sport.

## Where it came from

Two towns tell two stories, and the agriculture ministry files wanko soba with Izumo and Togakushi as one of Japan's three great soba traditions without settling the question. Hanamaki's version, told by Yabuya, the restaurant that hosts the town's contest: in the Keichō era (around 1600) the Nambu lord Toshinao stopped at Hanamaki castle, was served the local soba a little at a time in lacquer bowls so as not to offend a lord with a labourer's heap, and kept asking for more. *Wanko* is the local diminutive for a bowl. The Tōhoku tourism body's version is less specific and more plausible as a mechanism: a way of feeding a large banquet from one pot, born of the region's tradition of hospitality, which then spread from Hanamaki's soba shops in the Meiji era to households in the Taishō. Morioka's claim rests on its shops and its contest rather than a founding legend this site could source.

Both towns institutionalised it. Hanamaki's "Original Wanko Soba All-Japan Tournament" began in December 1957 as a sumo-style bout, took its present name from the eighteenth edition, and has been held every 11 February since the twenty-second; the day was registered as Wanko Soba Day in 2015. Morioka's "All-Japan Wanko Soba Championship" began in 1986. In 2022 the Agency for Cultural Affairs listed wanko soba as a "100-year food" and Morioka's three noodles — wanko soba, cold *reimen* and *jajamen* — as one of its "future" foods.

## What makes it wanko soba

**The portion.** One mouthful per bowl; the contest standard is ten grams. Azumaya in Morioka and the Tōhoku tourism body both say about fifteen bowls make a normal serving of soba; a prefectural comparison puts it at ten to fifteen.

**The rules.** Azumaya's are the ones you will hear at the table: the first bowl is eaten without condiments, to taste the soba; when the sauce builds up in your bowl you pour it into the slop bucket; condiments — tuna sashimi, grated daikon, nori, spring onion, pickles — are there to change the taste as you go; and you cannot stop until you have put the lid on while the server is watching. Lid on, contest over. Eat a hundred and you get a wooden tally plaque.

**The noodle.** Ordinary soba, pre-sauced so that it can be eaten without a dipping cup. This is not a dish about the buckwheat; it is a dish about the counting.

## Why Iwate

A cold northern province where buckwheat grew (Iwate harvested about 1,050 tonnes in 2025, in the national top ten), a castle-town culture of banquets, and a habit of serving guests more than they can eat. The tourism body's phrase is *omotenashi no kokoro*, the hospitality of the ancestors; the Hanamaki story is the same idea with a lord in it.

## The shops, and what they charge

Wanko soba is priced as a session, not a bowl:

- **Azumaya**, Morioka, founded 1907. Wanko soba ¥4,500 tax included; ¥700 off for primary and secondary school children, ¥1,800 for pre-schoolers.
- **Yabuya Hanamaki Sōhonten**, founded 1923 and once a haunt of the poet Miyazawa Kenji. Wanko soba ¥4,300 for middle-school age and up, ¥3,300 for primary, ¥1,300 for pre-schoolers (the menu page says tax included; the history page says otherwise, so ask). Yabuya's branch inside Morioka station sells a ten-bowl taster for ¥1,980.
- **Chokurian**, the other Morioka name, publishes no prices this site could verify.

Morioka households rank 38th of 52 cities for noodles eaten out (¥6,927 a year) — wanko soba is for guests and visitors, not Tuesday lunch.

## How to eat it

1. Tie the apron. Decide whether you are eating or competing; the price is the same.
2. First bowl plain. Then use the condiments to keep your palate interested — tuna, daikon, nori, the pickles.
3. Pour the accumulating sauce into the bucket between bowls, or you will be drinking it.
4. Count, or let the server count with matchsticks. Fifteen is lunch; fifty is a good effort; a hundred gets the plaque.
5. To stop, put the lid on your bowl while the server is watching. Hesitate and another mouthful lands.

## The records

Morioka's contest runs fifteen minutes per person. The record is 632 bowls, set in 2018 by Suzuki Takamasa; the women's record is 411, the same year; before the time limit was introduced in 1997, the mark was 559 with no clock. Hanamaki's contest is five minutes (three for children) and its official record, as the host restaurant publishes it, is 258 bowls. Results after 2019 for Morioka and after 2025 for Hanamaki were not available from an official page.

## Wanko soba away from Iwate

It does not travel, because the dish is the server. Soba restaurants elsewhere occasionally stage wanko-style events, and department-store fairs sell "wanko soba sets" of small bowls, but without someone standing at your shoulder refilling the bowl it is a plate of soba in instalments. The two chains that own the tradition have stayed home: Azumaya is in Morioka, Yabuya in Hanamaki and Morioka station. If you want it, you go to Iwate — which is, for a tourism body, the entire point.

## Getting there

Morioka and Shin-Hanamaki are both on the Tōhoku Shinkansen from Tokyo. Azumaya's main shop is in central Morioka; Yabuya's main shop is near Hanamaki station on the Tōhoku main line, and its station branch is inside Morioka station's Fesan building.

## What people get wrong

- **"It is all-you-can-eat soba."** It is a fixed-price session that ends when you close the lid; the price does not depend on how many you eat.
- **"Fifteen bowls is a lot."** Fifteen bowls is one plate of soba.
- **"The sauce is a soup."** Pour it out between bowls.
- **"It is Morioka's."** Hanamaki has the legend and the older contest; Morioka has the record. Both are right to claim it.
