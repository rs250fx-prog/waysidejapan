Most visitors meet Japanese fish as sushi or sashimi. A great deal of it never arrives raw. It is salted in the port where it lands, dried in the cold, ground into paste, simmered and brushed with a reduced sauce, or carried alive to a city that has no sea. And one fish may only be cut by someone holding a prefectural licence. This page is the map of that side of Japanese seafood. Sushi, sashimi, unagi and the fish markets each have their own overview; this page mentions them but does not repeat them.

## What it is

There is no single dish here, so there is no single definition. The pages under this overview cover:

- **Salmon and its roe** — salted salmon, *sujiko* and *ikura*, and the northern dishes that use the whole fish.
- **Fugu** — pufferfish, the fish with the strictest rules on who may prepare it.
- **Anago and hamo** — conger eel and pike conger, two long fish with two very different kitchens.
- **Himono** — dried fish.

Fish paste (*nerimono*), kombu, and seaweeds such as nori, wakame and mozuku are on the list below but not written up as separate pages yet. What the sources say about them is summarised further down.

## Why it is the way it is

The farm ministry's explanations come back to the same few reasons.

**Fish spoil, so they are salted and dried where they land.** The ministry says fish hold a lot of water, and their own enzymes and bacteria make them go off quickly; drying reduces the water and slows both. Salting salmon is the first processing step after the catch, so it is done at the fishing grounds — which is why, it says, Hokkaido produces so much of it.

**The method follows the fish.** Large salmon are usually rubbed directly with salt, which pulls out more water but can salt unevenly and let the fat oxidise. Roe and lightly salted products are usually soaked in brine, which salts evenly and oxidises less.

**Some fish are dangerous, and the rule is about the person, not the recipe.** The health ministry says fugu toxin is concentrated in the guts, especially the liver and ovaries, and is not destroyed by salting, soaking in water or heat. So the safeguard is that only a handler approved by the prefecture may prepare fugu for the public, and certain parts may not be served at all.

**Some fish travel well alive.** Kyoto is a hamo city because, the ministry says, the fish is hardy enough to be carried alive from the Seto Inland Sea and the Genkai Sea.

**Seaweed is light when dry.** The ministry says kombu, nori, wakame, tengusa, hijiki and funori spread across Japan in the Edo period, helped by how light dried seaweed is.

## The ones without a page yet

**Fish paste.** The farm ministry defines *nerimono* as fish ground with salt and set by heat — grilled, boiled, steamed or fried. It says the oldest surviving record of kamaboko is a menu for a celebration in 1115, and that early kamaboko was shaped like a grilled chikuwa. *Chikuwa* takes its name from the look of paste smeared on a bamboo skewer and grilled, said to resemble a cattail head. The ministry lists regional kinds: *sasa-kamaboko* in Miyagi, *hanpen* in Tokyo, steamed board kamaboko in Odawara, *noyaki* chikuwa in Shimane, *jakoten* in Ehime, *tsukeage* in Kagoshima, *chikiagi* in Okinawa, and *tōfu-chikuwa*, fish mixed with tofu, in Tottori. The main raw material today is Alaska pollock. The industry association's own history pages would not open for me, so I give only the ministry's dates.

**Kombu.** The Japan Kombu Association, an industry body, says over 95% of kombu harvested in Japan comes from Hokkaido, the rest from the Sanriku coast of Aomori, Iwate and Miyagi. Its history page says kombu was shipped in the Edo period by *kitamaebune* along a western route through Shimonoseki and the Seto Inland Sea to Osaka — the "kombu road". It describes Osaka simmering kombu in soy, Okinawa stir-frying and stewing it with pork and vegetables, and the Kanto region, which the route reached late, as an area that eats comparatively little. How to make kombu stock is on the dashi overview.

**Nori, wakame and mozuku.** The nori growers' association, an industry foundation, says nori farming on Japan's coasts runs each year from 1 September to 30 April on sea leased through the local fishing cooperative, and that new nori from Tohoku and Tokyo Bay starts to appear in early November. In the Ariake Sea, it says, a tidal range of about 6 m exposes the nori to sunlight at low tide, which is said to increase its amino acids. The farm ministry says wakame is in season in March and April and most of it is blanched, salted and shipped year-round. Okinawa's fisheries research centre said in 2007 that over 95% of Japan's mozuku came from Okinawa; I could not confirm a more recent figure.

## Where to eat it

The sources describe where these foods belong rather than which shops to visit.

- **Salmon dishes** such as *harako-meshi* are home and regional cooking in Miyagi; the Iwate dish *hizu-namasu* is sold at fishmongers and supermarkets in the salmon season and before New Year.
- **Fugu** gathers at Shimonoseki, which has a wholesale market specialising in it.
- **Anago** is a standard topping at Edomae sushi shops; *anago-meshi* is a Miyajima specialty.
- **Hamo** takes so much work, the farm ministry says, that it is mostly eaten at *ryōtei*, *kappō* and restaurants rather than at home.

## The same name, a different place

For most of these foods, I could not find an official source comparing them where they are produced with the same food in Tokyo, so this page does not say one is better. What the sources do show is that the place of production and the place of eating are often different:

- **Salted salmon** is processed where it is landed, and Hokkaido produces a lot of it.
- **Kyoto's hamo** is caught elsewhere and brought in alive; Kyoto is the city that does the bone-cutting, not the fishing ground.
- **Kombu** is the one case where the sources describe the gap directly: grown almost entirely in Hokkaido, eaten most distinctively in Osaka and Okinawa, eaten comparatively little in Kanto.
- **Fugu** is prepared by licensed handlers everywhere, but the name and content of the licence differ by prefecture.

## What people get wrong

- **"Cooking fugu makes it safe."** The health ministry says heat does not destroy the toxin. The safeguard is the licensed handler and the banned parts.
- **"Ikura and sujiko are different fish."** They are the same salmon roe, in the membrane or loosened from it.
- **"Kyoto’s hamo is a Kyoto fish."** Most of it is caught in the Seto Inland Sea and Genkai Sea.
- **"Anago is just a cheaper unagi."** The farm ministry describes it as a different fish, lower in fat, and not yet farmed. For unagi, start from the unagi overview.
