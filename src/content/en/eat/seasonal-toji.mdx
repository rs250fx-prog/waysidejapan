*Tōji*, the winter solstice, falls around 22 December, and the food that goes with it is the plainest on Japan’s calendar: pumpkin. Sometimes it is simmered on its own, sometimes with azuki beans in a dish called *itoko-ni*. The farm ministry does not give one reason for the pumpkin. Its regional offices list several side by side, from a play on sounds to the simple fact that a summer vegetable lasts into winter. A bath with yuzu citrus belongs to the same day.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the [overview of food for annual events](/en/eat/seasonal/).

## Where it came from

The ministry’s Kyushu regional office describes the solstice as the day of the year with the shortest daylight. It says that on this day one’s luck was thought to fall and the body to weaken, and that customs arose, it is said, to draw in good fortune and to pray to stay free of illness.

The same material gives the day another name, *ichiyō raifuku*, and says it was also thought of as the day bad luck ends and the turn towards good fortune begins. In other words, the solstice is a low point and a turning point at once, and the food is explained both ways: as a guard for the low point and as a welcome for the turn.

The solstice is not one of the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*, which run from 7 January to 9 September. It is a point in the sun’s year rather than a numbered day of the month, which is why the date is given as "around" 22 December.

## What makes it solstice food

### Pumpkin, for three reasons

The ministry’s Hokuriku and Kyushu offices, and its *washoku* culture material, give the reasons together rather than choosing one:

- **The sound "n".** Another name for pumpkin is *nankin*. Eating foods with the sound "n" in their names was said to bring *un*, luck. The posters give other examples in the same breath: daikon radish, *ninjin* (carrot) and *renkon* (lotus root).
- **It keeps.** Pumpkin is harvested in summer and stores well. The Hokuriku poster and the Kyushu material say it contains beta-carotene and vitamin C, and so suited a winter when vegetables were scarce.
- **A long life.** The Hokuriku poster adds that because it keeps, it was also said to carry a wish for a long life.

The first reason is wordplay, the second is about the larder, and the third grows out of the second. None of the sources ranks them, and this page does not either.

### Itoko-ni, pumpkin with azuki

The Kyushu office describes *itoko-ni*, pumpkin simmered with azuki beans. It says the red colour of azuki was thought to drive away evil and misfortune, so the pairing is explained as pumpkin to raise one’s luck and azuki to clear away bad luck. Red as a colour that wards things off turns up across Japan’s food calendar, and the [overview](/en/eat/seasonal/) gathers the other examples.

The material used here does not explain the name *itoko-ni* itself, and this page does not offer one.

## The sayings

The Hokuriku poster records three sayings about pumpkin, each of which says something different.

- **"Eat pumpkin at the solstice and you will not catch a cold."** The poster gives it together with the yuzu bath. It is recorded as a saying, and this page repeats it only as that.
- **"Don’t let the solstice pumpkin see the new year."** The poster explains that after the solstice stored pumpkins begin to spoil, so the saying tells you to finish them before the year is out. It is the practical side of the "it keeps" reason: it keeps until about now, and not much longer.
- **"Shibai, konnyaku, imo, nankin."** Theatre, konnyaku, potatoes and pumpkin. The poster says this was a list, made in the Edo period, of things women were said to like, strung together because it sounds good. It is not a solstice rule, but it shows pumpkin as an everyday pleasure as well as a ritual food.

## The yuzu bath

The ministry’s posters and culture material mention a bath with yuzu citrus floating in it, *yuzu-yu*, on the solstice. They do not explain where the custom came from or what it was thought to do, beyond pairing it with the cold saying above. This page leaves it there.

## How to eat it

The sources describe what is eaten and why, not a set way of eating it. They give no rule for the hour, the number of pieces or how the pumpkin is cut, and this page does not add one. The Hokuriku saying about finishing the pumpkin before the new year is the nearest thing to an instruction.

## Where the material comes from

The detailed explanations here come from two of the ministry’s regional offices, Hokuriku and Kyushu, and from its national *washoku* culture material. They describe the custom in general terms. They do not compare how the solstice is kept from one region to another, so this page does not describe regional versions.

## What people get wrong

- **"There is one reason for the pumpkin."** The ministry gives at least three, side by side: the "n" sound, the fact that it keeps, and a wish for a long life.
- **"The solstice is a day of bad luck."** The Kyushu material describes it as both: a day when luck was thought to fall, and, as *ichiyō raifuku*, the day the turn towards good fortune begins.
- **"Pumpkin at the solstice is about the pumpkin alone."** The posters name daikon, carrot and lotus root as "n" foods too, and *itoko-ni* adds azuki beans.
- **"The solstice is one of the five seasonal festivals."** It is not. Those fall on fixed dates from 7 January to 9 September.
