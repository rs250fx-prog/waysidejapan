Japan’s New Year food comes in a stacked box, and almost everything in it has a meaning attached. The farm ministry lists them one by one: beans for health or hard work, roe for many children, datemaki for never lacking clothes. On 7 January comes something else, a rice porridge with seven spring herbs. This page sets out what the official sources say about both, and where they disagree.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the overview of food for annual events.

## Where it came from

**Osechi.** The ministry says *osechi* originally meant the food offered at all five of the seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*, and that only later did the word come to mean the most important of them, the New Year food. It says the basic form was settled in the late Edo period.

**Seven-herb porridge.** The 7th of January is *jinjitsu*, the first of the five festivals. The ministry’s *washoku* culture material says an old Chinese almanac, the *Jingchu Suishi Ji*, records it as a day for divining about people, on which a hot broth of seven greens was eaten to stay free of illness. In Japan that observance merged with the custom of gathering young greens at New Year, and *nanakusa-gayu* was the result.

The same material says that in the Heian period the dish was not part of official court ritual and was a broth rather than a porridge. Between the Muromachi and Edo periods the custom of putting seven young greens into rice porridge took shape, and the day joined the five festivals.

## What makes it New Year food

### The three celebration dishes

The ministry names three *iwai-zakana*, celebration side dishes, as the core:

- **Kuromame**, black beans
- **Tazukuri**. The ministry says they were once used to fertilise rice fields, so they stand for a good harvest.
- **Kazunoko**, herring roe. One fish holds a great many eggs, so it stands for many descendants.

The black beans have two readings, on two ministry pages. The osechi page says they are a wish to *mamemameshiku* work, to work diligently. The ministry’s children’s page says they are a wish for the whole family to stay healthy, *mame*. The emphasis differs, and this site gives both.

### The rest of the box

The ministry’s osechi page gives a meaning for each of these. They are explanations, in the page’s words, not rules about what must be in the box.

- **Datemaki**: it looks like the pattern of a kimono, so you will not go short of clothes.
- **Kuri-kinton**: its golden colour stands for money.
- **Renkon**, lotus root: you can see through the holes, so the year ahead is clear.
- **Satoimo**, taro: one plant bears many small tubers, so many descendants.
- **Ebi**, prawns: a long life, until your back is bent.
- **Kobu-maki**, rolled kelp: a play on *yorokobu*, to be glad.
- **Buri**, yellowtail: a fish that changes its name as it grows, so it stands for getting on in life.
- **Nishime**, simmered vegetables: ingredients whose names contain the sound "n" are linked to *un*, luck.

### The box itself

The ministry’s culture material says the stacked box, the *jūbako*, is used mainly for New Year osechi. I could not find an official explanation of what the stacking means, and I have not given one.

### The seven herbs

The ministry’s culture material lists them as *seri*, *nazuna*, *gogyō*, *hakobera*, *hotokenoza*, *suzuna* and *suzushiro*. It says eating wild plants at the new year was thought to pass on their strong life force, and so a long life.

It records a song sung while chopping the herbs, which begins with the birds of China not yet having crossed to Japan. The material explains it as a prayer to drive off birds, which harm crops.

It also gives a more practical reading: that 7 January is the day the New Year observances that began on the 1st come to an end, and a sensible day to rest a stomach tired of mochi. The ministry gives this as one more explanation, alongside the others.

### After the porridge

The same material mentions the mirror mochi, *kagami-mochi*, eaten on 11 January as "tooth-hardening" mochi. Teeth are linked to age, so it is a wish for a long life. Mochi and zōni, the New Year soup with mochi, are covered in the overview of rice and what goes with it.

## How to eat it

The sources used here describe meanings, not a sequence of eating, and I have not invented one. The ministry’s page on the seven-herb porridge itself could not be opened when this page was researched, so recipes and serving customs are not covered.

## Not all of Japan does this the same way

I could not find an official source in this research comparing osechi or the seven-herb porridge between regions. The regional differences that are documented for New Year concern zōni, which the rice-and-sides overview covers.

## New Year away from home

Nothing in the sources compares how this food is eaten in one region and in Tokyo or elsewhere, so this section is empty for now.

## What people get wrong

- **"Osechi always meant New Year food."** The ministry says the word first covered the food for all five seasonal festivals.
- **"Every dish has one meaning."** The black beans have two on the ministry’s own pages.
- **"The stacked box has a fixed meaning."** I could not find any meaning for the stacking in an official source.
- **"Seven-herb porridge is ancient Japanese court food."** The ministry says it was a broth, not a porridge, in the Heian period, and that the porridge form took shape between the Muromachi and Edo periods.
