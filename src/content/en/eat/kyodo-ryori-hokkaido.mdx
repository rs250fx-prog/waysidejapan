Hokkaido's local dishes are mostly salmon, herring and the food of people who came to farm. The farm ministry's database lists 27 Hokkaido dishes, among them Ishikari-nabe, *sanpei-jiru*, salmon *chanchan-yaki*, *ruibe*, *butadon*, *zangi*, *izushi*, Matsumae-zuke, ramen and jingisukan. This page covers where the best-known came from, notes where the sources tell it differently, and links to the pages that already cover lamb, ramen and pork.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

**Ishikari-nabe.** A miso hotpot of salmon, chunks and trimmings, with vegetables in kelp stock and a pinch of *sanshō* pepper to finish; some homes add salmon roe or butter. The farm ministry calls it a fishermen's dish from Ishikari, at the mouth of the Ishikari river, where salmon fishing thrived: fishermen celebrating a big catch are said to have put salmon into a pot of miso soup. In the 1945–54 period (the Shōwa 20s) it was served to visitors who came to watch the salmon seine-netting, and that is said to be how it became known across Japan. The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page adds that it was once called *dainabe*, a dish eaten between jobs, that cabbage and some of the other vegetables in it spread only after the Meiji era and so reflect the settling of Hokkaido, and that the name Ishikari-nabe was given after the war, for the visitors. The two accounts do not conflict. In 2007 Ishikari city launched an "Ishikari-nabe revival project" and made 15 September Ishikari-nabe Day, for the salmon season and a play on the numbers.

**Sanpei-jiru.** Salted salmon or herring simmered with carrot, daikon and other vegetables, seasoned only by the salt in the fish. The ministry says it has been eaten for more than 200 years and appears in *Tōyūki*, a late-Edo travel record. It sets it apart from Ishikari-nabe, which is miso-based and uses fresh salmon. Central and eastern Hokkaido use salted salmon, the north salted cod as well, and the Hiyama district has both a salt *shio-sanpei* and a *miso-sanpei*. The name has two theories:

- **A fisherman's soup**: a lord of the Matsumae domain, out hunting, liked the soup at the house of a fisherman called Saitō Sanpei.
- **A dish**: it was served in Arita-ware bowls called *sanpei-zara*.

This page gives both.

**Salmon chanchan-yaki.** Salmon and vegetables steam-grilled in butter and seasoned with miso, sugar, mirin and sake, from the Ishikari district. For the name the ministry gives several theories: it is quick to make, father (*o-tō-chan*) makes it, or it is the sound of spatula on hotplate. In Ishikari it is said to have begun in the early Shōwa era with fishermen grilling their catch on board, on hotplates cut from oil drums. The ministry's page says the Ainu people caught salmon as a precious food source long ago, and that Hokkaido's salmon catch is the largest in Japan. For the fish itself, see [salmon](/en/eat/seafood-salmon/).

**Ruibe.** Salmon or trout frozen and sliced thin without thawing, along the coast at Ishikari, Hakodate and Kushiro. The ministry says it "is said to be" of Ainu origin: fish was buried in snow to freeze and keep, and cut while still frozen. The name is said to come from the Ainu word *ruipe*, from "melt" and "food". The ministry adds that freezing kills parasites, so it also served as protection against them. With modern freezers it is eaten all year.

**Butadon.** Grilled pork on rice under a sweet soy sauce, handed down in the Tokachi district, with Obihiro said to be its birthplace. The farm ministry says pig farming began in Tokachi around the end of the Meiji era, and that in the early Shōwa era a diner in Obihiro put charcoal-grilled pork under a sauce like the one for grilled eel, because eel was dear and hard to get. The 100-year food page tells it in more detail: the settler group Banseisha brought four pigs to Tokachi in the Meiji era and began raising them under the motto "cows for milk, horses for power, pigs for food", and a young cook around the 1930s, aiming for a taste ordinary people could afford in war and recession, took a hint from the eel bowl and made a soy-based sauce. It cites a free magazine from the Tokachi Mainichi newspaper. These are the same story at different levels of detail. 10 February is Butadon Day. More in [Japanese pork](/en/eat/meat-pork/).

**Zangi.** What the rest of Japan calls *karaage*, fried chicken, Hokkaido calls zangi, and it is marinated in a strong soy-based seasoning. The ministry says it is said to have begun around 1955 at a chicken restaurant in Kushiro that cut whole birds into pieces and fried them. For the name it gives one theory, that "n" was added to the Chinese *zhaji*, fried chicken, for luck (*un*). It turns up at home, at celebrations, at sports days and on outings, in lunch boxes and in *izakaya*. For karaage elsewhere, see [karaage](/en/eat/chicken-karaage/).

**Izushi.** Fish and vegetables fermented with rice *kōji*, a *nare-zushi*. The ministry says it is said to have begun in fishermen's homes around the first snow; it was pickled in late autumn and eaten around New Year. Because it takes so much work, the ministry says, more people now buy it at the supermarket and almost no one makes it at home, though groups have formed around Hokkaido to keep it going.

**Matsumae-zuke.** Dried squid and kelp cut into strips and steeped in soy sauce, a keeping food from Matsumae in the south, said to come from the Matsumae domain. From the late Edo period into Meiji it used herring roe; as herring fishing declined, squid and kelp took over. Kits for making it at home are on sale.

**Jingisukan and ramen.** Both are on the list, and both have their own pages. The ministry says mutton began to be eaten in the Taishō era, when the First World War made imported wool hard to get, that jingisukan's origin has several theories, and that it was chosen in 2007, with Ishikari-nabe and chanchan-yaki, as one of the "100 local dishes of farming, mountain and fishing villages"; see [jingisukan](/en/eat/meat-jingisukan/). The ministry's ramen entry is "ramen" for all of Hokkaido, not "Sapporo ramen". It mentions a theory that the "Nanking soba" of a Western-style restaurant in Hakodate in 1884 (Meiji 17) was Japan's first ramen, and says no records survive to settle it. It names Sapporo miso, Hakodate salt and Asahikawa soy, Asahikawa's layer of aromatic oil that keeps the soup hot, and Kushiro's thin wavy noodles, said to be thin so they cook fast. See [Sapporo ramen](/en/eat/ramen-sapporo/).

## Hokkaido's lists

**The farm ministry.** The database has, as I counted them, 27 Hokkaido dishes. I counted both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages, and the counts matched. They include butadon, chanchan-yaki, *imo-mochi*, herring pickles, Ishikari-nabe, *ika-meshi* (rice-stuffed squid), ramen, *sekihan*, Bibai *tori-meshi*, Bibai and Muroran yakitori, whale soup, ruibe, herring soba, zangi, skate simmered in soy, salmon roe in soy, *beko-mochi*, izushi, Matsumae-zuke, *teppō-jiru*, sanpei-jiru and jingisukan.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them, number 15 for Hokkaido: two modern and thirteen future, and none in the traditional category. They are Sapporo soup curry (fiscal 2025, special award), Muroran yakitori (2025), *age-imo*, *gatatan*, *supakatsu* and Tomakomai *hokki* curry (all 2024), Ishikari-nabe (2023, special award), Kushiro soba (2023, modern), *hirugai* curry and butadon (2023), and Otaru *ankake* yakisoba, jingisukan (modern), *shinko-yaki*, Tomakomai curry ramen and Nayoro simmered jingisukan (all 2021). Ishikari-nabe, butadon, jingisukan and Muroran yakitori are on both lists. The two lists are chosen separately.

**What is missing.** Soup curry is not in the farm ministry's list. Neither is the name "Sapporo ramen"; the entry is simply ramen.

## What shaped it

**The land.** The ministry's Hokkaido story says the island is about 22 per cent of Japan's area, cool and dry with long snow cover, the northern limit of the temperate climate and the southern limit of the subarctic.

**The Ainu and the settlers.** Once called Ezo, it was home to the Ainu. From the Kamakura period people from Honshu came and trade began; by the mid-16th century southern Hokkaido was Japanese land, and in the Edo period the Matsumae domain was set there. The ministry says food culture passed on by the Ainu and by migrants from Honshu has had a large influence on Hokkaido's local dishes today. The Meiji government drove settlement hard, gathered migrants from all over Japan, brought in Western farming methods, and Hokkaido grew into the country's largest food-producing region. Among individual dishes, the ministry's mentions of the Ainu are on the ruibe and salmon pages. I did not find the *tondenhei*, the farmer-soldiers, in the ministry's Hokkaido pages, and I did not read the Hokkaido government's own material on Ainu food.

**By district.** The story picks out *sekihan* made with sweetened beans in central Hokkaido, which it credits to the founder of a Sapporo cooking school; dried herring from the north, which shaped local dishes in Fukui, Kyoto and Ishikawa; Matsumae and the Kitamae trading ships in the south; and Tokachi's butadon in the east.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Ishikari-nabe**: several shops in the Ishikari district are known for it.
- **Butadon**: widely served at restaurants in Tokachi.
- **Zangi**: a regular in izakaya, and sold to take away by specialist shops.
- **Ruibe**: restaurants, souvenir shops and the airport.
- **Izushi**: mostly bought at the supermarket rather than made at home.
- **Matsumae-zuke**: makers in Matsumae, department stores and supermarkets.

**Prices.** I found no official dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** Ishikari-nabe is a winter staple, and it and chanchan-yaki follow the salmon. Sanpei-jiru is an everyday winter dish at home. Izushi is pickled in late autumn and eaten around New Year. Jingisukan goes outdoors, to cherry-blossom parties in spring and barbecues in summer, and 29 April is Jingisukan Day.

**The occasion.** Zangi runs from home dinners to celebrations. Sanpei-jiru is a home side dish, not restaurant food.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Tokachigawa Onsen and butadon.** The Tokachigawa Onsen tourism association's food page invites visitors to try both butadon and ramen at the spa, and recommends "Obihiro's speciality, butadon", listing it under a sushi restaurant too. The farm ministry places butadon in Tokachi with Obihiro as its birthplace. So you can eat a Tokachi dish at a Tokachi onsen; I do not call it the onsen's own dish. See [Hokkaido's onsen](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/).

**No link found.** I found no dish named both by the ministry and by Noboribetsu, Jōzankei, Tōya or Akan. See [Noboribetsu](/en/soak/noboribetsu/).

- **Hakodate**: about 1 hour 20 minutes from Haneda by air; about 4 hours 30 minutes from Tokyo station by *Hayabusa* to Shin-Hakodate-Hokuto and the Hakodate Liner; about 3 hours 30 minutes from Sapporo by limited express; about 40 minutes by air from New Chitose. ## Hokkaido food away from Hokkaido

- **The Tokyo shop.** Hokkaido Dosanko Plaza, set up by the prefecture to help Hokkaido firms with marketing. The Yūrakuchō shop, on the ground floor of the Tokyo Kōtsū Kaikan at 2-10-1 Yūrakuchō, Chiyoda-ku, opened on 20 July 1999, carries about 1,200 items and is run by a consortium of Sapporo Marui Mitsukoshi and Hokkaido Hyakka. There are others in Ikebukuro, Saitama-Shintoshin, Kichijōji, Haneda airport (Terminal 1, second floor), Machida and Ebina. It sells fish products, farm and dairy produce, drink and crafts, with new products every three months and weekly tastings. The prefecture's page gives no hours or prices.
- **Easy to carry home.** Izushi from the supermarket, Matsumae-zuke kits, jingisukan meat and sauce for home, butadon sauce.
- **Season.** At home, Ishikari-nabe and chanchan-yaki follow the salmon; a shop sells all year.
- **No taste comparison.** I found no official one, and make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Soup curry is a listed Hokkaido dish."** Soup curry is not in the farm ministry's list; it is a 100-year food.
- **"Ishikari-nabe and sanpei-jiru are the same."** Ishikari-nabe is miso and fresh salmon; sanpei-jiru is salted fish and its own salt.
- **"Ishikari-nabe is an old name."** The 100-year food page says it was named after the war, for visitors.
- **"Zangi is a different dish from karaage."** It is Hokkaido's name for fried chicken, marinated strongly.
- **"Hokkaido ramen is one style."** The ministry names miso, salt and soy by city.
