Oysters and snow crab both come wrapped in rules. Whether an oyster may be eaten raw is decided by the water it was harvested from. Which snow crab may call itself Echizen-gani is decided by law and checked with a tag. This page covers where the two came from, what the official sources say about safety, seasons and names, and what the tags do and do not tell you. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Where they came from

**Hiroshima.** The farm ministry says people ate wild oysters here from the Jōmon and Yayoi periods, judging by shell mounds, and that farming is thought to have begun in the 1500s–1600s. Hiroshima city puts farming in Hiroshima Bay at more than 450 years, from the Tenbun era at the end of the Muromachi period.

**Miyagi.** The prefecture says farming is said to have begun in the 1600s, when Uchiumi Shōzaemon found oysters attached on Nonoshima in Matsushima Bay and scattered young ones to grow. Teachers invited from Hiroshima in the late 1800s did not make their methods stick. In 1924 a hanging method for collecting seed on oyster shells was devised, and with it Miyagi began exporting seed oysters around the world. Raft farming followed in 1930, longlines around 1952.

**Fukui’s female crab.** The farm ministry says *seiko-gani* is named for "carrying young on its back". It was once not shipped but eaten locally, and the roots of the dish *seige* are also said to lie with the *kitamaebune* ships that sailed the Sea of Japan from the mid-Edo period into the Meiji 30s (1897–1906).

## What makes oysters different

**Sorted by water, not freshness.** Hiroshima prefecture says raw-eating oysters are those harvested in designated waters, or in conditionally designated waters after artificial purification. Oysters from other waters can only be shipped for cooking. Freshness, it says, is the same for both.

**The standards.** A 2018 farm ministry briefing summarises the health ministry’s rules under the Food Sanitation Act, in place since 1967: no more than 50,000 viable bacteria per gram, an *E. coli* most probable number of 230 or less per 100 g, and a *Vibrio parahaemolyticus* most probable number of 100 or less per gram for shucked oysters. The oysters must come from water with a coliform most probable number of 70 or less per 100 ml, or be purified in equivalent seawater or 3% artificial seawater. Hiroshima and Okayama say they are kept at 10°C or below (−15°C or below if frozen). Norovirus is not among the items on that slide; I did not see the full text of the standard.

**The label.** Hiroshima says the harvest area must be shown on raw-eating oysters, and divides Hiroshima Bay into north, west, central and south, alongside Kure Bay, Hiro Bay, Mitsu Bay and the east of the prefecture. An older Okayama page (2010) adds that cooking oysters must say they need cooking.

**Norovirus.** The health ministry says bivalves take in viruses with plankton and concentrate them, and poisoning happens when they are eaten raw or undercooked. Its cooking guide is 85–90°C at the centre for 90 seconds or more, as in the Codex standard. Cases occur all year but rise from around November and peak in December and January. Sendai city asks people never to eat cooking oysters raw.

**What producers do.** Miyagi says raw-eating oysters in the shell are purified for 22 hours or more before shipping, and producers test for norovirus and shellfish toxins themselves and publish the results on JF Miyagi’s site. In Hiroshima Bay, producers test for norovirus during the raw-eating season, and positive lots are shipped for cooking.

## What makes snow crab different

**One crab, many names.** Tottori prefecture says the male snow crab is *matsuba-gani* in San’in and *Echizen-gani* from Fukui, and Kyoto’s *Taiza-gani* is the same species. A just-moulted male is *waka-matsuba-gani*, or *mizu-gani*. The female is *oya-gani* in San’in, *kōbako-gani* or *seiko-gani* elsewhere. The eggs she carries outside are *sotoko*, those inside the shell *uchiko*, the farm ministry says.

**Seasons set for the stock.** Fukui’s seasons are males 6 November to 20 March, females 6 November to 31 December, mizu-gani 19 February to 20 March. The farm ministry says the female season is short for resource protection. Tottori gives the best time instead: matsuba-gani November to March, oya-gani November to December, waka-matsuba-gani February.

**Tags.** Tottori tags matsuba-gani of 11 cm shell width or more landed by its offshore trawlers to certify Tottori origin; its landing ports are Sakai, Tottori and Ajiro. Fukui puts a yellow tag stamped with the landing port on snow crab landed in the prefecture, carrying the GI mark since 27 September 2018.

**The GI.** Echizen-gani was registered as a geographical indication on 27 September 2018, number 69, for Fukui prefecture. The farm ministry’s page says it is kept cool from catch to landing, and that Fukui has presented it to the imperial household for more than 90 years.

**The top grades.** Tottori’s *Itsukiboshi* requires a shell width of 13.5 cm or more, 1.2 kg or more, all legs present, bright colour and a full body of meat. Fukui’s *Echizen-gani Kiwami*, since 2015, requires 1.3 kg or more, a 14.5 cm shell and a 3 cm claw, and adds a Kiwami tag.

## Seasons and numbers

The farm ministry separates two meanings of "season" for Hiroshima oysters: they are landed from October to May, and from December to February they store the most glycogen and are said to taste best.

By the fisheries agency’s 2023 figures, Hiroshima produced 60% of Japan’s farmed oysters by volume, Miyagi 14%, Okayama 8%, Hyogo 6%. By value Hyogo, at 11%, comes second, ahead of Miyagi. Production peaked at 270,000 t in 1988 and is now around 150,000–170,000 t. Hiroshima prefecture says residents ate 13.7 oysters each in 2018, against a national 5.4.

## Oyster huts and oyster boats

A 2011 Hiroshima prefecture release announced *kaki-goya*, oyster huts, opening across the prefecture, on dates from 29 October to 9 December that year. It did not say whether they served raw-eating or cooking oysters, and I could not confirm which huts operate now.

*Kakifune Kanawa*, an oyster boat moored off Ōtemachi in Naka ward, is run by Kanawa, a company founded in 1962 to spread Hiroshima oysters across Japan, which serves oysters from its own rafts. The story that oyster boats began in the Edo period appeared only in search summaries, so it is not given here.

## How to eat it

- **Read the bag.** Sendai city tells buyers to check for the raw-eating label. If it says cooking, cook it through.
- **Seige.** In the Kōno district of Minami-Echizen, boiled seiko-gani is simmered with grated daikon in an earthenware pot, then miso and rock laver go in. The farm ministry says each household’s recipe differs.

## Not all of Japan’s oysters are Hiroshima’s

Hiroshima farms on rafts with hanging lines. Miyagi changes method by water: simple hanging in shallow Matsushima Bay and Mangokuura, longlines off Ishinomaki Bay and the Oshika Peninsula, rafts in the north. Its oysters grow for one to two years, and a high share are eaten raw.

## Oysters and crab away from the coast

I could not find an official source comparing taste or freshness at the producing area with Tokyo, so this page does not say one is better. What the sources show is a system for checking origin:

- **Crab tags** show the landing port (Fukui) or Tottori origin. No public source links a tag to taste.
- **The name is enforced.** On 24 September 2026 the farm ministry issued an order under the Geographical Indications Act to the town of Echizen in Fukui. From at least September 2020 to April 2026, the town had listed on a hometown-tax donation site, as Echizen-gani, products made mainly from Fukui mizu-gani that may not use that name. Labels such as "Echizen-produced snow crab" were treated as violations too.
- **Oyster labels** carry the harvest area and the raw or cooking class wherever they are sold, and the purification and testing happen before shipping. Whether shell oysters at coastal huts are raw-eating or cooking oysters, I could not confirm.

## What people get wrong

- **"Cooking oysters are the less fresh ones."** The split is by harvest water.
- **"Matsuba-gani and Echizen-gani are different crabs."** Both are male snow crab, named by where they are landed.
- **"Any Fukui snow crab is Echizen-gani."** The 2026 order shows that Fukui mizu-gani, for one, may not use the name.
- **"Raw-eating means virus-free."** The standard items on the ministry briefing are bacterial counts. Norovirus testing is done by producers themselves.
