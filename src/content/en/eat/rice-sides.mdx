faq:
  - q: Why do Japanese meals come with pickles?
    a: The farm ministry describes the basic Japanese meal, ichijū sansai, as rice with a soup, one main dish and two side dishes, combined with pickles. The pickles are part of the pattern, not a garnish added by the restaurant.
  - q: Is nattō eaten everywhere in Japan in the same way?
    a: No. Mito in Ibaraki is known for small-bean nattō and straw-wrapped nattō, Yamagata mashes nattō into a winter soup, and Kyoto’s tera-nattō is a salted, dried kind made quite differently. I could not find an official survey of how eating habits differ east and west, so this page does not give one.
  - q: Why are New Year rice cakes square in Tokyo and round in Kyoto?
    a: The farm ministry puts the rough dividing line near Sekigahara in Gifu, with square mochi usual to the east and round to the west, and several exceptions on both sides. It gives one account of the square shape, that cutting a flat sheet of mochi into pieces began in the Edo period and spread from Edo because the pieces were easy to carry. A Japanese meal is built around a bowl of rice, and much of what sits next to it exists because of the rice: a few slices of pickle, a spoon of tsukudani simmered in soy, a pack of nattō, a soup. Some of it goes into the rice instead, as in rice cooked with chestnuts or sea bream. And some of it is rice in another form, pounded into mochi or steamed with beans for a celebration.

None of these is a single dish. Each is a family of local foods, and the same name can mean a different food in a different prefecture. This page is the map. The lineage pages go into one family at a time.

## What it is

Five groups, all of them everyday or seasonal foods rather than restaurant specialities:

- **Rice cooked with things in it** (*takikomi gohan*, and *kamameshi*, "pot rice"). Rice cooked together with vegetables, fish or chestnuts.
- **Mochi and sekihan.** Glutinous rice pounded into cakes, the New Year soup *zōni* they go into, and *sekihan*, glutinous rice coloured with beans for celebrations.
- **Tofu and nattō.** Two soybean foods made in completely different ways.
- **Pickles, tsukudani and furikake.** The small, strongly seasoned things eaten a little at a time with rice.
- **Soups.** Local soups such as pork miso soup and *kenchin-jiru*. Miso soup in general is covered in the nabe section, and stock in the dashi section, so this page only lists the soups.

Onigiri are covered with convenience stores, and station boxed lunches, including the famous kamameshi in a clay pot, with food shopping.

## Why it is the way it is

**The meal has a shape.** The farm ministry, in a footnote to its food-education white paper, describes *ichijū sansai*, the basic Japanese meal, as rice as the staple, a soup such as miso soup or clear soup, and three dishes (one main, two side), combined with pickles. That pattern is why pickles and soup belong on the tray. The ministry’s page on washoku’s UNESCO listing goes no further than saying that a meal built on this pattern is said to be well balanced; I could not find an official description of what each part is "for".

**Much of it is preservation.** The ministry’s guide to traditional foods says pickles came out of fermentation as a way to keep food, and are closely tied to climate. In cold regions they developed as a storage technique; in warm regions they lean more towards a simple side dish to help eat the staple. Tsukudani, small fish and seaweed simmered down in soy, is described by the ministry’s Tokyo entry as keeping longer because of the strong seasoning, and as a supporting player that tightens up a meal rather than a main dish.

**Rice was precious, so things were added to it.** Nara prefecture’s entry in the ministry’s local-food database says that in the Nara period millet was mixed into rice to bulk it out, and that cooking rice with various ingredients to save rice is said to be the start of cooked-in rice. That is one account, from one prefecture’s page.

**Rice is also the celebration.** Mochi and sekihan are made from glutinous rice and belong to festivals, New Year and family events. The ministry lists many forms of mochi across Japan and calls them food for occasions, closely tied to daily life.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **The same name, different foods.** Ehime’s *tai-meshi* is two dishes: in Tōyo (around Imabari) a whole sea bream is cooked with the rice, and in Nanyo (Uwajima, Seiyo) raw slices are soaked in a sauce and poured, sauce and all, over hot rice.
- **The shape of a rice cake is regional.** Square to the east and round to the west, divided roughly near Sekigahara, with exceptions on both sides.
- **Nattō is several foods.** The sticky, stringy kind, the small-bean kind Mito is known for, and Kyoto’s salted, dried *tera-nattō* are made differently.

## Where to eat it

Places the farm ministry ties to particular foods:

- **Kyoto** — pickles: *senmaizuke*, *suguki*, *shibazuke*.
- **Mito, Ibaraki** — small-bean and straw-wrapped nattō.
- **Ehime** — *tai-meshi*, which the ministry says is also sold as station boxed lunches and ready-mix packs.
- **Akita** — smoked *iburi-gakko* pickles, and a sweetened *sekihan*.
- **Lake Shinji, Shimane** — clam soup.

## The same name, a different place

I could not find an official source that compares any of these foods at home with the same food sold in Tokyo or elsewhere. What the ministry does show is variation inside Japan under one name:

- *Zōni* in Tokyo is a clear soup with grilled square mochi; in Kyoto it is white miso with round mochi.
- *Sekihan* in Akita has sugar added; in Hokkaido it is made with sweet *amanattō* beans and coloured with food colouring.
- *Kenchin-jiru* in Kanagawa and in Iwate use different ingredients.
- Clam soup at Lake Shinji uses *yamato-shijimi*; at Lake Biwa it is made with *seta-shijimi*, a clam found only in the lake, whose catch the ministry says has fallen sharply.
- Tsukudani in Tokyo is centred on Tsukudajima; the ministry also lists smelt tsukudani from Kasumigaura and Hyōgo’s *ikanago no kugini* as separate kinds.

These are differences between places, not between an original and a copy.

## What people get wrong

- **"Pickles are a garnish."** In the ministry’s description of the basic meal, pickles are one of the parts.
- **"Nattō is one thing."** It is several foods made by different methods.
- **"Mochi is always square (or round)."** It depends where you are, and some prefectures use both.
- **"Kamameshi was invented by the railway."** I could not find a source I could use for the origin of kamameshi as a dish. What Oginoya, the best-known kamameshi maker, says is that it began selling at Yokokawa station in 1885, and that its first boxed lunch was salt onigiri with *takuan* pickle.
- **"Mochi is harmless."** The Consumer Affairs Agency issues a New Year warning about choking on mochi; see the mochi page.
