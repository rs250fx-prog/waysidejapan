Tea kaiseki is the meal served at a tea gathering before the tea itself: one tray, rice and soup first, and matcha at the end. Banquet kaiseki, which is what most restaurants now mean by the word, is a meal for drinking sake, with rice near the end. The farm ministry describes the banquet as developed on the base of tea food, so this page covers both: the tea meal as the sources document it, and how the banquet differs.

For kaiseki in general, start from the kaiseki overview.

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's booklet *Washoku no Ryūgi* traces tea kaiseki to Sen no Rikyū, who about 400 years ago completed *wabi-cha* and, the booklet says, cut away the excess in food as well. The fashion of the time was the lavish honzen ryōri, with a second and third tray carried in; Rikyū kept to one. The booklet says he built tea kaiseki by taking in the shōjin ryōri of Zen temples. The Omotesenke tea school's page on tea food does not mention Rikyū, so the link to him rests on the farm ministry's booklet alone. The booklet's history chapter places the completion of tea kaiseki in the Momoyama period.

**The word.** The farm ministry's magazine says *kaiseki* comes from Zen monks in training who put a warmed stone inside the front of their robes to bear their hunger, and that the meal began as a host's way of offering a little food and sake to guests from afar, just enough to warm the stomach, while the water for tea came to the boil. The Omotesenke school calls the warmed stone a tradition, and says the word means simple food. I could not confirm any other account of the word in an official source.

## What makes it tea kaiseki

**Chaji and chakai.** The farm ministry's magazine distinguishes two kinds of gathering:

- A **chaji** is a small gathering to which a host invites close guests. It is a full course including kaiseki, and usually takes about four hours.
- A **chakai** serves only thick or thin tea. The food is a simplified kaiseki in a box, called *tenshin*.

The Omotesenke school describes the chaji as a way of receiving guests by combining tea and kaiseki to suit the season and the time of day, and calls kaiseki the most formal food of tea.

**The flow of a chaji**, per the farm ministry's magazine: hot water in the waiting room, entering the tea room, the host laying the charcoal; while the water heats, kaiseki of one soup and three dishes is served with sake; then the main sweet, and thick and thin tea.

## The order, course by course

The farm ministry's booklet, partly in the words of a cook it interviewed:

1. **The tray.** On a lacquered tray (*oshiki*), the rice bowl on the left, the soup bowl on the right, and the *mukōzuke* at the back. A mouthful of freshly cooked rice; the soup is miso soup, in a small amount. The name *mukōzuke* comes from being placed "beyond" the rice and soup. It was often *namasu* in the past; now it is more often kelp-cured fish or sashimi dressed with seasoned vinegar.
2. **Sake.** As the rice and soup are finished, a sake kettle and cups arrive. Then the guest takes up the mukōzuke.
3. **Nimono-wan**, the simmered dish in a lidded bowl — one of the three dishes, and the centrepiece of kaiseki. A second round of sake.
4. **Yakimono**, the grilled dish. From here the guests pass the dish and serve themselves.
5. **Azukebachi**, a bowl of dishes to go with rice — simmered vegetables, fried food, vinegared food. From here the food is for drinking.
6. **Hassun**, on a cedar tray. Two kinds in principle, such as something from the mountains and something from the sea, or animal and vegetable. The host offers the last sake.
7. **Yutō and kō-no-mono**, a jug of hot water and pickles. The guests clean their dishes and drop their chopsticks onto the tray to show they have finished.

The Omotesenke school gives the base as soup, mukōzuke, nimono and yakimono, with *shiizakana*, a clear soup and hassun added; these three are there to offer sake and keep the conversation going. It calls the nimono the highlight of the meal, a clear-broth preparation, and says the host brings rice, soup and mukōzuke on the tray. It ends, too, with hot water and pickles.

**Why guests serve themselves.** The farm ministry's booklet explains passing dishes round as a wish to spare the host trouble, and bringing each dish freshly made as part of the spirit of tea.

## Banquet kaiseki: how it differs

The sources describe the difference in more than one way:

- **Purpose and rice.** The chef in the farm ministry's 2023 magazine: tea kaiseki began as a light meal before tea, rice first; the banquet is for enjoying sake, rice after. He says cooks are trying to cross the line between the two.
- **Start and finish.** The farm ministry's booklet: tea kaiseki begins with a tray of rice and soup and ends with matcha; the banquet serves rice near the end.
- **Order.** The same booklet says that in the banquet the hassun became showier and the order of dishes can change with the cook's ideas.
- **Lineage.** One chapter of the booklet says the banquet developed on the base of tea food; another says the restaurant banquet grew in the Edo period from warrior honzen ryōri. This site does not choose.

I could not find an official source for the fixed sequence of banquet courses often printed in guides, so this page does not give one.

## The shops, and what they charge

Only one official site I checked states that its prices include tax, and it is an eel restaurant, not a specialist kaiseki house. Atsuta Hōraiken in Nagoya: all prices include tax, with a 10% service charge added. At the main restaurant, eel and seasonal kaiseki courses from ¥12,100 at lunch and ¥16,500 in the evening. Booking by three days before; it may not be possible in busy periods. Checked 2026-10-11. The structure to watch is a service charge on top of the course price.

## How to order it

- **Courses.** JNTO says kaiseki menus are usually set in advance: you choose the number of courses and leave the rest to the chef. It marks some restaurants as reservation recommended, others as required.
- **At a kappō counter.** The farm ministry's booklet says the original kappō lets you order one dish at a time and talk with the cook about how to prepare the fish of the day; some kappō also have courses.
- **Dislikes and allergies.** GO TOKYO, writing about omakase sushi, advises mentioning them early; that is sushi, but the idea of leaving it to the chef is the same.

## Not all kaiseki is this

- **Season and time.** The Omotesenke school says a summer morning chaji is made one soup and two dishes, avoiding raw fish.
- **Meat and fish.** Kyoto's official tourism site says kaiseki was originally vegetarian (shōjin) and today may include meat and seafood.
- **JNTO** gives grilled fish, soup and rice as the basic elements, but not the only ones.

## Kaiseki away from Kyoto

Kyoto prefecture registered Kyō-ryōri as an intangible cultural property in 2022, carried by the master, the cooks, and the proprietress and servers together; it also says ryōtei have fallen by about 90% in 30 years, without showing data. JNTO lists Tokyo kaiseki restaurants alongside Kyoto ones. I found no official comparison of kaiseki in Kyoto and elsewhere, and make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kaiseki is a long banquet."** Tea kaiseki is the light meal before tea; the banquet is a separate form.
- **"Rice comes at the end."** In tea kaiseki it comes first.
- **"The courses always come in the same order."** The farm ministry says banquet cooks change it.
- **"The warmed-stone story is settled history."** The Omotesenke school calls it a tradition.
