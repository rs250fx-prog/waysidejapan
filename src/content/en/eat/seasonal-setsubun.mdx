Setsubun, in early February, has three foods, and each comes with more than one explanation. Roasted soybeans are thrown to drive out evil and then eaten, one for each year of your age. Sardines are grilled in parts of the country. And a sushi roll, *ehōmaki*, is eaten whole, facing the year’s lucky direction. The roll is the newest of the three, and the farm ministry is candid that nobody knows where it began.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the overview of food for annual events.

## Where it came from

**The day.** *Setsubun* means "dividing the seasons". The ministry explains that there is one before the start of each season, but because the old calendar treated the start of spring as the start of the year, the word now usually means the one in spring.

**The beans.** The ministry says bean-throwing is a custom for warding off misfortune and evil and wishing for good health, that it is said to have begun in the Muromachi period, and that it is said to come from a pun: *mame*, bean, as *ma-me*, "the demon’s eye", which the beans destroy. Its *aff* magazine adds that beans were believed to have spiritual power, and that they carry a wish to work diligently, *mamemameshiku*. The Hokkaido office of the ministry says the way the beans roasted was once read to foretell a good or poor harvest, and that roasted beans were kept on the household altar all year to ward off disaster.

**The roll.** The ministry gives several origins and does not settle on one:

- Its page on Osaka’s local dishes says the origin "is not certain" and names two leading theories: that it came out of the amusements of the pleasure quarters, or that merchant houses in Senba, Osaka, began it in the late Edo period to pray for good business.
- Its Setsubun page gives a theory that Osaka merchants began it between the late Edo and early Meiji periods, and another that it drew new attention after a 1977 campaign by an Osaka nori wholesalers’ association. It describes the custom as the New Year visit in the lucky direction, *ehō-mairi*, changed into a Setsubun habit.

This site does not choose between them.

## What makes it Setsubun

**Beans.** The ministry’s pages say to eat as many beans as your age, or your age plus one, and that this is said to bring a long life or keep colds away.

**Sardines.** The ministry’s *aff* magazine describes sardines at Setsubun as mainly a western Japan custom and gives two explanations, without choosing:

- the smell and smoke of grilling sardines drive evil away;
- the sardine, a weak fish, was called by words meaning "weak" and "lowly", and eating it at Setsubun removes the body’s yin energy.

In some areas, the magazine and the ministry’s culture material say, a sardine head is stuck on a holly branch and hung at the entrance.

**The roll.** The custom as the ministry describes it: face the year’s lucky direction, think of your wish, and eat the whole roll in one go, in silence, so that good luck does not escape, or so that a tie is not cut. The filling is said to be seven ingredients after the Seven Lucky Gods, but the ministry says there is no rule. It also says it is fine to eat it in whatever way is easy, and that making it a size you can finish helps prevent food waste.

## How it spread

According to the ministry’s Osaka page, ehōmaki was not widely eaten until after the war. From around the 1970s the nori trade and others promoted it to sell more, and in the 1990s convenience stores and supermarkets began to sell it, which spread it across the country. The ministry’s culture material says only that "in some regions" ehōmaki is eaten.

## How to eat it

The ministry’s own advice is the plainest: eat it in a way that is easy for you. The face-the-direction, eat-it-whole, say-nothing version is the custom as described, not a requirement the ministry sets.

## Not all of Setsubun is this

- **Peanuts instead of soybeans.** The Hokkaido office says peanuts are used in some cases in Hokkaido.
- **Konnyaku and oden in Sorachi.** The same office says that in Sorachi, Hokkaido, people eat simmered konnyaku or oden at Setsubun, calling it *karada no suna-oroshi*, clearing the sand from the body. It says the custom began in Shikoku and spread among settlers.
- **Sardines mainly in the west**, as above.

## Setsubun away from Osaka

The ehōmaki story is the closest thing here to a dish leaving home: a roll told as an Osaka custom, which the ministry says went national through convenience stores and supermarkets. Beyond that, I could not find an official source comparing how Setsubun food is eaten in Osaka and elsewhere. A national nori association’s material on ehōmaki could not be checked for this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ehōmaki is an ancient tradition."** The ministry says its origin is uncertain and that it spread nationwide from the 1990s.
- **"There is one origin story."** The ministry gives at least three, from the pleasure quarters, from Senba merchants, and a 1977 nori campaign that brought it back to attention.
- **"You must eat it in silence."** That is the custom as described. The ministry says eating it in an easy way is fine.
- **"The sardine has one meaning."** The ministry’s magazine gives two and does not say which is the origin.
