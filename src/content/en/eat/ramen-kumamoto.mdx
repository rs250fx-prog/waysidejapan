If Hakata is the famous child of Kyushu tonkotsu and Kurume the parent, Kumamoto is the cousin who went his own way: the same white pork-bone base, but with chicken in the pot, the garlic cooked rather than raw, and a spoon of black garlic oil floating on top that makes the bowl smell of something roasted. It is the one Kyushu ramen you can recognise from across the room.

## Where it came from

The Ramen Museum's account begins in 1952, when a man named Shikasho opened a ramen shop in Tamana, a town north of Kumamoto city, which it calls the pioneer of Kumamoto ramen. The Japan Tourism Agency's own explanatory text for the style describes it as a derivative of Kurume ramen — the white-soup technique carried south from the Chikugo river — and the museum's Kumamoto page is the one that records both Kurume's Sankyū and Hakata's Akanoren as having begun white soup around 1947–48, without deciding between them.

## What makes it Kumamoto ramen

**The soup.** Pork bones with chicken carcasses added — the museum says many Kumamoto shops do this, and the tourism agency's text describes the chicken stock as blended into the pork. It is rounder and less aggressively porky than Kurume's.

**The garlic.** Hakata serves garlic raw, crushed at the counter. Kumamoto cooks it: fried garlic chips on the bowl and *mā-yu*, black garlic oil, spooned over the soup. The museum's summary is that Kumamoto flavours the bowl "with garlic in processed forms".

**The noodle.** Thicker and firmer than Hakata's, with what the museum calls a solid bite.

**On top.** Pork, wood-ear mushroom, often a soft egg, and the garlic chips.

## Why Kumamoto

The sources are thin on the why. What is documented is the route — Kurume to Tamana to Kumamoto city — and the regional preference, in a prefecture with its own strong garlic and pork farming, for the roasted rather than the raw. The museum's phrase for the whole Kyushu family is that Sankyū's taste spread "through about a hundred apprentices"; Kumamoto is where one branch of that spread settled and changed.

## How to eat it

Stir the black oil in before the first mouthful or leave it on top for the aroma; either is normal. There is no hardness question and no refill. The garlic chips soften in the soup within minutes, so eat from the start.

## Kumamoto ramen away from Kumamoto

Kumamoto's style travelled early and far — Ajisen alone has shops across Asia — and what changes abroad is usually the *mā-yu*, which is either reduced or dropped for customers who find it bitter. In Tokyo, bowls sold as "Kumamoto ramen" without the black oil and the chips are Kyushu tonkotsu with a Kumamoto label. The oil is the test.

## Getting there

Kumamoto is on the Kyushu Shinkansen from Hakata; Tamana is on the Kagoshima main line to the north.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kumamoto ramen is Hakata ramen with garlic."** It is Kurume's descendant, with chicken in the stock, cooked garlic, and a thicker noodle.
- **"The black oil is a sauce."** It is garlic oil, and it is bitter on purpose.
