Yonezawa is a castle town in a snowy basin in southern Yamagata, and its beef is the northern member of the trio that Japanese people call the "three great wagyu" — a phrase the agriculture ministry's own documents record people using and decline to define. Its origin story is the most specific of any brand: one Englishman, one cow, and a train to Yokohama.

## Where it came from

The ministry's Geographical Indication file tells it. In 1871 the Kōjōkan, the former domain school in Yonezawa, hired an English teacher named Charles Henry Dallas; when he left in 1875 he took a cow from Iide, in the basin, back to the foreign settlement at Yokohama, where its beef was admired. That is the event the brand dates itself from. The region's cattle were, like Tajima's and Ōmi's, work animals before they were food, and the basin's farmers turned to fattening as Tokyo's appetite grew. Yonezawa beef became a registered GI in March 2017.

## What makes it Yonezawa beef

**The animal.** Japanese Black heifers that have never calved.

**The place.** Raised in the Okitama district — Yonezawa, Nan'yō and Nagai cities and the towns of Takahata, Kawanishi, Iide, Shirataka and Oguni — the basin of the upper Mogami river, snowbound from December to March.

**The age and grade.** Slaughtered at 33 months or older — later than most brands — and graded 3 or above. The GI file records that Yonezawa heifers fetched about 20% more than the Tokyo market average for A4–A5 wagyu heifers in 2013–15.

## Why Okitama

Cold, time and the slow season. A long winter means a long feeding period indoors, and the brand's minimum age reflects a local habit of taking cattle slowly to weight. The basin had the castle town's market and, from the Meiji period, Tokyo's; it is the north's answer to Kobe and Matsusaka, built on patience rather than bloodline.

The Yonezawa beef promotion council's site could not be reached for this edition, and the city's beef houses do not publish prices in a form this site could verify, so no local prices are given. The GI file's own benchmark — about 20% above the Tokyo wagyu-heifer average — is the honest guide. Yamagata city, forty minutes north, is the city that spends most in Japan on ramen eaten out; Yonezawa itself is a sukiyaki and steak town.

## How to eat it

Sukiyaki is the local form, and the Yonezawa houses serve it in the Kanto manner with a broth; steak and shabu-shabu follow. In winter, which is most of the year, the hotpot is the point.

## Yonezawa beef away from Yamagata

It travels to Tokyo and Sendai department stores as gift beef and to a handful of restaurants; the brand has no registered-shop system with a statue at the door, so the certificate and the ID number are the check. Of the three great names it is the one visitors are least likely to meet by accident and most likely to find honestly labelled when they do.

## Getting there

Yonezawa is on the Yamagata Shinkansen, about two hours from Tokyo; the beef houses are around the station and the castle park. Iide, where Dallas's cow came from, is a village at the foot of the mountains to the west.

## What people get wrong

- **"Three great wagyu is official."** The ministry records the phrase and defines nothing.
- **"Yonezawa is A5."** The floor is grade 3; the brand is about place, sex and age.
- **"A Japanese discovered it."** By the brand's own account, an English teacher did, with one cow.
