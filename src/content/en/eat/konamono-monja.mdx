Monjayaki is the one konamono you eat straight off the hot plate. The batter is loose, it is poured into a ring of chopped cabbage on an oiled iron plate, and you eat it a little at a time with a small spatula, pressing it onto the hot metal first. It belongs to Tokyo, and to one street in particular: Nishinaka-dori in Tsukishima, which the farm ministry and Tokyo’s tourism office both call the monja street.

For the batter family as a whole, start from the konamono overview.

## Where it came from

The sources agree on the name and disagree on the date.

**The farm ministry.** In its children’s guide to local dishes, the farm ministry lists monjayaki as a Tokyo dish, born in the *shitamachi* (the old downtown) of Tsukishima in the poor years after the war. It says that just after the war, when children could not get paper or brushes, they were taught letters by writing them on an iron plate with flour dissolved in water and a spatula. That was called *moji-yaki*, “letter-grilling”, and the name is said to have changed into *monjayaki*.

**The shop owners’ union.** The Tsukishima Monja Promotion Association, the union of the street’s shops, says monjayaki developed in Tokyo’s shitamachi from the middle of the Meiji era, and also traces it to moji-yaki.

So the ministry places it after 1945 and the union places it in the late nineteenth century. Both are official in their own way, they do not say the same thing, and this site does not choose. Tokyo’s tourism site, GO TOKYO, calls it Tsukishima’s “soul food”, born there; the union’s wording is that it developed across the shitamachi, which is not quite the same claim.

There are older stories — that moji-yaki appears in Edo-period prints, or in a Meiji-era book of trades — but I could only find them in search summaries, not in the sources themselves, so they are not used here. I could not reach an official page for the monja society that is sometimes cited either.

Osaka’s tourism bureau names monjayaki in one of its theories about okonomiyaki: that Tokyo’s monjayaki became the stall food *dondon-yaki*, which became the sauce-flavoured *issen yōshoku* in the Kansai. The bureau gives it as one theory among several.

## How Tsukishima became the monja street

The union’s own history is the clearest account of how one neighbourhood ended up with so many shops.

- **Shōwa 30s (1955–64):** four monja shops.
- **From around the start of Heisei (1989):** the number rose quickly.
- **April 1997:** 28 shops within a 500-metre radius of the shopping street. That month the 28 owners met and formed the Tsukishima Monja Promotion Association as a voluntary group.
- **December 2000:** the Ōedo subway line opened, and the union says individual shops’ sales rose by about 20%.
- **September 2002:** with 47 members, the association became a cooperative union.

The union sells meal vouchers that work in member shops, and sells *omiyage monja* — monjayaki to take home and cook. Tsukishima itself, GO TOKYO notes, is reclaimed land, built as an artificial island in 1892.

On the union’s own site, one shop introduction describes Yoshimiya as founded in 1954 and “the original Tsukishima monja”. That is how the shop is introduced, not a finding of the union, and I have not treated it as one.

## What makes it monjayaki

The union publishes a step-by-step method. In outline:

1. **The batter.** Flour, sauce and dashi mixed into a thin batter.
2. **The fillings.** Cabbage, shredded dried squid, tempura bits (*agedama*), sakura shrimp and so on. The union says cutting the cabbage into coarse strips is a feature of Tsukishima monja.
3. **Fry and chop.** Oil the plate, put the fillings on — plenty of them — and fry them while chopping finely with the spatulas.
4. **The ring.** When the cabbage has softened, push it into a doughnut-shaped ring, the *dote* (“embankment”), and pour the batter into the middle.
5. **Mix and spread.** The batter thickens quickly. Cut it into the ring of fillings with the spatula, mix, and spread the whole thing over the plate.
6. **Finish.** When bubbles form, scatter green laver (*aonori*).
7. **Eat.** Press a little onto the plate with the small spatula so it scorches, and eat it from the spatula.

The farm ministry lists fillings such as cabbage, pork, squid, *mentaiko* (spiced cod roe) and *mochi*, and flavours such as sauce, cheese and curry.

**Why the ring?** The union’s page gives the steps and not the reason. You will see explanations elsewhere; I could not find one in an official source, so I am not offering one.

## How to eat it

**At the table.** GO TOKYO describes trying your hand at monjayaki on the iron plate built into the table. That is the basic form: you cook it yourself.

**If you are not sure you can.** The union addresses first-timers directly: staff will cook it quickly for you, so there is nothing to worry about, and it is fine if you cannot do it yourself. That is the union speaking for its members; I could not confirm that every shop works this way, so ask.

**With the small spatula.** Both the farm ministry and the union describe eating it by pressing it, a little at a time, onto the plate with a small spatula. That is how people eat it.

**Try the okonomiyaki too.** GO TOKYO notes that many monja shops also serve okonomiyaki and suggests comparing the two.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Not all of Tsukishima is the union

The count of shops depends on the source:

- The farm ministry: about 80 shops on Nishinaka-dori.
- GO TOKYO: more than 80 monja specialists.
- The union: 54 member shops (34 member companies) as of June 2019.
- The union’s online member list: 48, when I checked on 11 October 2026.

My reading is that the larger numbers count the street and the smaller ones count union members. None of the sources says so in those words; it is my interpretation of their figures. Either way, the method above is the union’s, and a shop that is not a member may do things differently.

## Monjayaki away from Tsukishima

What the sources let me say about Tsukishima specifically: the shops are concentrated on one street; the union describes coarse-cut cabbage as the Tsukishima style; the union publishes the ring method and tells beginners that staff will cook for them; and the union sells monjayaki to take home.

What I could not find is any official comparison with monjayaki elsewhere — at chains, in other parts of Tokyo or outside Tokyo — in taste, method, menu or price. I have one Tsukishima price list and no tax-included official prices from anywhere else, so I cannot compare costs. I am not going to say the Tsukishima version is better.

## What people get wrong

- **“Monjayaki is runny okonomiyaki.”** It is a different method: the fillings are fried first, made into a ring, and the batter is poured into the middle. Official sources I found do not compare the two batters directly.
- **“Its origin is settled.”** The farm ministry says postwar; the union says mid-Meiji. Both trace the name to moji-yaki.
- **“You have to cook it yourself.”** You usually do, but the union says staff will cook it for first-timers.
- **“Tsukishima has exactly 80 shops.”** About 80, more than 80, 54 or 48, depending on who counts and what.
- **“The ring has a known purpose.”** The union gives the step, not the reason.
