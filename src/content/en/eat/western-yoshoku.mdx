*Yōshoku* (洋食) is Western cooking reshaped by Japanese cooks: omurice, croquettes, beef stew, the hamburg steak, the pork cutlet. The sources I could reach start it in different decades, and two restaurants, one in Ginza and one in Osaka, are each named as the origin of omurice. This page sets out what each source says and leaves the disagreements standing.

For Western foods made Japanese in general, start from the Western foods overview. The pork cutlet and rice curry, two of the three dishes Kikkoman’s journal calls the "big three" of yōshoku along with the croquette, have their own pages; this one does not repeat them.

## Where it came from

There is no single starting point. I found four, and they do not agree.

- **The end of the meat ban.** Kikkoman’s exhibition on yōshoku says that in 1872 (Meiji 5) the lifting of the ban on eating meat was reported, Western restaurants opened in the treaty ports, and around 1897 (Meiji 30) Japanese-Western dishes appeared. From those came the cutlet, curry, croquettes, chicken rice, omurice and more. The exhibition calls yōshoku a uniquely Japanese cuisine but gives no strict definition.
- **One dish at a time.** Kikkoman’s research journal says Western cooking was too different in taste, ingredients and method to be taken in whole, so dishes were taken one by one into a diet built around rice, each turned a little Japanese. Early Meiji had its odd experiments — potatoes pickled in rice-bran paste, miso soup with milk, sashimi with mayonnaise — and out of that trial and error came rice curry, the pork cutlet, croquettes, omurice and hayashi rice.
- **The navy.** The Agency for Cultural Affairs, in its "100-year food" listing for navy food culture, says the navy took up Western-style meals to deal with poor nutrition, and that this is said to be the beginning of Japanese yōshoku. A navy cookbook of 1908 (Meiji 41), it says, carried more than 100 Western dishes and sweets.
- **The shops themselves.** Ginza’s official English site introduces Rengatei, founded in 1895, as the first restaurant in Japan to be called a *yōshoku* restaurant. Hokkyokusei in Osaka calls itself a pioneer of yōshoku restaurants.

These begin at different moments, and this site does not say which is right. I could not find an official definition of yōshoku.

**Rengatei, Ginza (1895).** Ginza’s official site credits Rengatei with devising the pork cutlet, fried dishes, omurice and hayashi rice. The Chūō ward tourism association’s "first stories" map dates the pork cutlet to 1899 and says the founder, Kida Motojirō, devised a method using vegetable oil to suit Japanese tastes. It also lists fried prawns (*ebi furai*) and omurice as born there, with no year. The cutlet is covered on the tonkatsu page.

**Shiseido Parlour, Ginza.** The parlour’s official timeline begins with a pharmacy, opened in Ginza in 1872 by Fukuhara Arinobu, which it calls Japan’s first private Western-style pharmacy. In 1902 a soda fountain inside it began selling soda water and ice cream. In July 1928 the shop reopened as the Shiseido Ice Cream Parlour and began serving full Western cooking.

## What makes it yōshoku

The clearest descriptions come from the restaurants, so read them as their own accounts.

**A croquette with no potato.** Shiseido Parlour says its meat croquette was devised in 1931 by its third head chef, Takaishi (高石鍈之助), and that the idea is said to have come from a foie gras croquette he met at an imperial banquet. It is bound with béchamel sauce and uses no potato. The croquette is shaped like a small bale, but the parlour still calls it by the French-style name *kuroketto*.

**Omurice built on chicken rice.** The parlour’s omurice is chicken rice made to a recipe it says has not changed since opening, with an omelette on top and tomato sauce. It started as an off-menu dish made at customers’ request and was later added to the main menu. The parlour is explaining its own dish, not claiming the invention.

**Beef stew in a covered pot.** The parlour’s beef stew uses wagyu *sanmai-niku* (三枚肉) steeped overnight in red wine, with demi-glace and tomato sauces, and is brought to the table in a lidded enamel pot so it stays hot. The page does not say who devised it or when.

**The hamburg steak.** Kikkoman’s journal records a "mutton hamburg steak" on the menu of a luncheon hosted by Yamagata Aritomo in 1885 (Meiji 18), as a dish of full Western cooking. The farm ministry’s JAS standard for chilled hamburg steak describes minced meat mixed with finely chopped onion or other vegetables, a binder and seasonings, shaped into an oval or similar form, and fried, grilled or steamed; the binders it lists include breadcrumbs, flour, starch, egg and milk powder. That is a standard for packaged products, not for homes or restaurants. When it became a dish eaten with rice, I could not confirm.

## Two shops, one omurice

Two accounts stand:

- **Rengatei.** Ginza’s official site lists omurice among the dishes Rengatei devised. The Chūō tourism association also lists it as born at Rengatei. Neither gives a year or a story.
- **Hokkyokusei.** Hokkyokusei’s official history says the founder, Kitahashi Shigeo (北橋茂男), opened in 1922 (Taishō 11) in front of the Shiomibashi stop on Naniwa-suji. It was first called *Panya no Shokudō* (パンヤの食堂), in gratitude to Maruki, a restaurant-and-bakery where he had been head cook, whose bread he sold at his door. In a time when the public bath cost 10 sen, he sold yōshoku at a flat 10 sen and did well. The company calls itself the original omurice shop. Osaka’s tourism bureau and JR West’s campaign site introduce it as an original omurice specialist of more than 100 years, without a year or a basis.

The Hokkyokusei pages I read give no account of how omurice was first made. Other stories circulate, but I could not confirm them from a shop or public source. Both accounts above come from the shops or the sites that introduce them, and this site does not say which came first.

## The shops, and what they charge

Prices checked on 2026-10-11 from the official page of Shiseido Parlour’s Ginza main restaurant. The page says prices include tax and that a 10% service charge is added.

- **Meat croquette** — ¥3,100
- **Wagyu beef stew** — ¥4,500
- **Ginza Modern Lunch** (course, weekdays until 4 pm) — ¥5,500
- **Chef’s recommended course** — ¥8,500 or ¥12,000

I could not get official prices for Rengatei (its own site would not connect, and Ginza’s official page lists none) or for Hokkyokusei.

## How to read the menu

At the Ginza main restaurant, add 10% to the listed price, and choose between courses and single dishes. Shiseido Parlour uses both *seiyō ryōri* (Western cooking) and *yōshoku* for its food — its timeline calls the 1928 menu full Western cooking, and its 1998 retort-pouch range a "yōshoku series" — so the two words overlap even on one restaurant’s pages.

## Not all yōshoku is this

- **Navy yōshoku.** The Agency for Cultural Affairs recognised "navy food culture — navy curry, beef stew and nikujaga" as a 100-year food (in the 2021 round, under Hiroshima prefecture, applied for by the council of former naval port cities). It says a navy-style beef stew, based on the navy’s yōshoku, is carried on as a local dish in Yokosuka, Kure, Sasebo and Maizuru.
- **The pork cutlet and curry.** Covered on the tonkatsu and rice curry pages.
- **Napolitan.** The coffee-house Napolitan is on the page about coffee houses and shaved ice, and the Yokohama hotel’s version on the Napolitan page.

## The first shop and the flagship

Hokkyokusei shows that the place a dish began and the shop that carries its name today are not always the same. On the company’s own history, it began in 1922 in front of the Shiomibashi stop. Today’s Shinsaibashi main shop opened as an omurice specialist on 26 October 1989, in a building converted from the company’s earlier blowfish restaurant, Kitahashi; the building is in the sukiya style and was built in 1950 (Shōwa 25). The company says its Kita-Horie café restaurant, opened in 1984 and now the Horie shop, is older as a "Hokkyokusei" than the main shop.

I found no official or public source comparing the dishes at the shops where yōshoku dishes began with those at other restaurants or chains, so this page does not compare them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yōshoku has an official definition."** I could not find one. Kikkoman, the Agency for Cultural Affairs and the shops each describe it from a different starting point.
- **"Omurice was definitely invented at X."** Two shops are named as the origin. This site does not choose.
- **"A croquette is a potato croquette."** Shiseido Parlour’s meat croquette uses béchamel and no potato.
- **"The JAS standard defines the hamburg steak."** It defines a packaged chilled product.
