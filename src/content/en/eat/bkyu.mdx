*B-kyū gurume*, “B-grade gourmet”, is the phrase Japan uses for the cheap, local, everyday dish a town is known for. It sounds like a category with rules. It is not. The best-known event built around these dishes, the B-1 Grand Prix, says the word has no clear definition, avoids it itself, and says its contest is not about food at all. This page is the map: what the term means and does not mean, how the B-1 Grand Prix works, which towns have won, and the fried food — korokke above all — that sits alongside these dishes. The lineage pages go deep on one dish at a time.

## What it is

**A phrase without a definition.** The B-1 Grand Prix organisers write that there is no clear definition of B-grade gourmet food, but that the basic image is “cheap and tasty food”. Within *gotōchi gurume* — local food seen as food culture — they say the cheap, everyday dishes loved by local people were named *B-kyū gotōchi gurume*, B-grade local gourmet. They say the B stands for “brand”, and that the word is thought to come from “B movie”.

**A phrase the organisers now avoid.** On the same page they say the B-1 Grand Prix is not an event that sells B-grade gourmet food, that seeing it as deciding Japan’s best B-grade food is a misunderstanding, and that they currently decline all requests to cooperate with projects billed as “B-grade gourmet” or “B-grade local gourmet”. I use the phrase on this page because it is the phrase you will meet, not because the organisers endorse it.

**No official definition.** I looked for a definition of *gotōchi gurume*, local food, from a ministry, the tourism agency or a local government, and found none. A 2012 farm ministry feature on local flavours of eastern Japan uses the phrase once without defining it, and does not mention B-grade food or the B-1 Grand Prix. The kyōdo-ryōri overview, on Japan’s official local dishes, reaches the same place from the other side.

## Why it is the way it is: the B-1 Grand Prix

The contest explains most of why certain town dishes became famous, so it is worth knowing how it runs. All of this is from the organisers’ own site.

**It began over yakitori.** In autumn 2004, members of the Hachinohe Senbei-jiru Research Institute, a group promoting Hachinohe’s cracker soup, discussed the idea of a national event at a yakitori shop in Hachinohe. Around 2005 the activity was still little known, and gathering ten groups from around the country was hard work.

**The first one was small.** It was held in Hachinohe, Aomori, in February 2006. Ten town-revival groups from Hokkaido to Kyushu took part, and 17,000 people came over two days.

**Then it grew.** The fifth contest, in Atsugi, drew 435,000 visitors. The eighth, in Toyokawa in 2013, had 64 groups and 581,000 visitors. The record on the organisers’ list is the seventh, in Kitakyushu in 2012, with 610,000. The host town changes each time.

**Who runs it.** The organisers’ history page says it is run by the Ai-B League, a liaison council of town-revival groups using local food, formed as a result of the B-1 Grand Prix; the league had 76 member groups as of December 2013. Another of the site’s pages names no organiser except for the “Ai B League Association” in its footer. The organisers call it one of the largest town-revival events in Japan.

**What it is for.** In the organisers’ words, it is not a gourmet event for selling food. It is a once-a-year joint promotion by town-revival groups, meant to get people to visit their towns and raise each area’s brand.

**Who can enter.** Entrants serve a dish loved in their area, but restaurants and restaurant trade associations cannot enter as such. The entrants are the town groups.

**How the vote works.** Visitors vote with chopsticks: each person gets one pair, which counts as two votes. Rankings are announced down to tenth place. The awards go to the town-revival group, not to the dish. The organisers say most voters choose on taste, but 20 to 30% vote on something else — to support a group’s effort, for a performance while they queued, or for good service. How gold, silver and bronze are decided in detail, and how many servings are counted, was not on the pages I read.

## What it costs

I have no prices for this page. The B-1 Grand Prix is not a food-selling event by its own account. For korokke I could not find a tax-included price from an official or local-government source. Nagasaki city’s guide to Toruko rice gives shop prices without saying whether tax is included, so they are not used. The one tax-included price in this group of pages is for monjayaki, on its own page.

A few things the table cannot show:

**Hachinohe senbei-jiru.** The farm ministry traces it to *Nanbu senbei*, a cracker of flour, salt and water baked round in an iron mould, from the old Nanbu domain across south-eastern Aomori and northern Iwate. In an area where cold summers made rice hard to grow, the crackers were a valuable keeping food. There are several theories of origin; one is that it began before the war with crackers simmered in a soup of the bones of *ugui* (dace) caught in the river. During the war iron pans were given up for the war effort and crackers stopped being made; after the war, makers developed a chewy soup cracker that does not fall apart. It is a soy-flavoured chicken-stock hotpot with vegetables and *ito-konnyaku*, with crackers broken in, and there are salt-flavoured versions with cod or grilled mackerel, as a port town would have, and a horse-meat version. About 200 restaurants in Hachinohe serve it. The ministry’s children’s page says it has been eaten at home for about 200 years, while its main entry says the name *senbei-jiru* took hold only in the Heisei era, when Hachinohe tourism groups promoted it to visitors. The dish and the name have different ages.

**Kofu torimotsu-ni.** Yamanashi’s official tourism site says it was born around 1950 at Okuto, a soba shop founded in 1913, and spread around Kofu. That is the shop’s account; I found no independent record. The site says it won gold at “a festival of local food”, without naming it; the B-1 list shows a Kofu torimotsu group winning gold at the fifth contest, in Atsugi in 2010. Its ingredients and seasoning were not on the page I read.

**Nagasaki Toruko rice.** Nagasaki city’s food site calls it a dish that represents Nagasaki: pilaf, a cutlet and spaghetti Napolitan on one plate, sometimes called a grown-up’s children’s lunch. The base is pilaf (or dry curry), a cutlet and Napolitan; shops vary it with korokke, carbonara and more. The city’s pages say one restaurant, Bistro Bordeaux, devised a “Turkish-style rice” that became the original, and elsewhere that it is “said to be one of the restaurants where Toruko rice began”. Why it is called *Toruko*, Turkey, was not on the pages I opened.

**Korokke and town fried food** has its own page: the 1895 magazine recipe, the butchers’ potato korokke, the 1918 song, Edo’s stall tempura, and Mishima’s town-defined korokke.

**Already elsewhere on this site.** Fukui’s sauce katsudon is in the donburi overview, Osaka’s kushikatsu in the tonkatsu overview, the Sasebo burger in the Western-food pages, and Tokyo’s monjayaki in the konamono overview.

## Where to eat it

- **Hachinohe, Aomori** — senbei-jiru, at about 200 restaurants by the farm ministry’s count.
- **Kofu, Yamanashi** — torimotsu-ni, at the Okuto shop where Yamanashi’s tourism site places its origin.
- **Nagasaki** — Toruko rice, at the restaurants on the city’s own food site.
- **Mishima, Shizuoka** — Mishima korokke, at certified shops.
- **Tsukishima, Tokyo** — monjayaki, on the street the farm ministry and Tokyo’s tourism office call the monja street.

Fares and travel times are not on these pages yet.

## The same name, a different place

For B-grade local food, what changes away from home is mostly the story rather than the plate — as far as I could find.

- **Hachinohe.** The home-cooked keeping food and the tourist speciality overlap: the dish is old in homes, the name is from Heisei-era promotion. I could not find how senbei-jiru served outside Hachinohe differs.
- **Nagasaki.** Toruko rice already varies from shop to shop at home. I found no comparison with versions outside Nagasaki.
- **Mishima.** Only the potato is defined, so there is no single Mishima korokke to compare with korokke elsewhere.
- **Korokke in general.** I found no official source comparing butchers’ and restaurants’ korokke, or korokke where potatoes are grown and in the cities.

## What people get wrong

- **“B-grade means second-rate.”** The organisers say the B in their event stands for “brand”; the phrase is thought to come from “B movie”, and its image is cheap and tasty.
- **“The B-1 Grand Prix picks Japan’s best cheap food.”** The organisers call that a misunderstanding. The awards go to town-revival groups, voted for with chopsticks.
- **“Restaurants compete for the title.”** Restaurants and their trade associations cannot enter as such.
- **“Every vote is about taste.”** The organisers say 20 to 30% of voters choose for other reasons.
- **“B-grade gourmet has an official definition.”** I found none, from the organisers or the government.
- **“Senbei-jiru is a centuries-old name.”** The dish is old in homes; the name dates from Heisei.
