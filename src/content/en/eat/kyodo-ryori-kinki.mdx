faq:
  - q: Is Ise udon an old dish?
    a: The way of eating it is old, the name is not. The farm ministry says shops in the Edo period served soft, thick udon with tamari so that Ise pilgrims could eat at once, but the name Ise udon was settled only in 1972, when the Ise city noodle restaurants' association decided on it.
  - q: Did kakinoha-zushi begin in Nara or in Wakayama?
    a: Both prefectures list it, with different stories. Nara's page places it in Gojō and Yoshino as summer festival food. Wakayama's page makes it autumn festival food in Katsuragi and Kudoyama, and the ministry's Wakayama story says Hashimoto is held to be the birthplace. Neither says which came first, and this page does not choose.
  - q: Is Kobe beef on Hyogo's list?
    a: Yes, as Kobe beef steak. The farm ministry's page says there is no breed called Kobe beef. It is Tajima cattle born and raised in Hyogo, slaughtered in the prefecture and meeting strict standards. Tajima beef has no entry of its own.
  - q: Why is Ōmi-Hachiman's konnyaku red?
    a: It is coloured with iron oxide. Why it is red is another matter. The farm ministry mentions theories, among them that Oda Nobunaga had it dyed, but says no historical record shows the origin and the reason remains unknown.

This page groups seven prefectures as Kinki: Kyoto, Osaka, Hyogo, Nara, Wakayama, Shiga and Mie — an old capital, a merchant city, two prefectures with no sea, and coasts on both the Japan Sea and the Pacific. I found no public text that describes their food as one, so this page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Kyoto

**The three pickles.** The farm ministry gives each of Kyoto's three great pickles a page. See also [tsukemono](/en/eat/rice-sides-tsukemono/).

- **Senmaizuke**: thin slices of Shōgoin turnip pickled in vinegar with kelp and chilli, not lactic-fermented. Said to have been devised in the Edo period by a palace cook, Ōfuji Tōsaburō; made from around November.
- **Shibazuke**: aubergine, cucumber and myōga salted with red shiso, said to have been devised by a priest of Sanzen-in in Ōhara. The name comes from a tradition that it was presented to Kenreimon-in and named "purple-leaf pickle".
- **Suguki**: salt only, fermented in a room at about 40°C. The ministry gives two origins — shrine families of Kamigamo growing a plant found wild by the Kamo river, or seed given by the palace. This page gives both. It is also a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2024, traditional, special award).

**Grilled hamo.** Most hamo eaten in Kyoto, the ministry says, comes from the Inland Sea and the Genkai Sea; the fish is hardy enough to be carried a long way alive. The Gion festival is also called the "hamo festival". See [anago and hamo](/en/eat/seafood-anago-hamo/).

**White miso zōni.** The New Year soup: round rice cakes for harmony and long life, *kashira-imo* taro for descendants and getting on, and daikon. The ministry says white miso has existed since the Heian period, fermented only a week to ten days with little salt, and that Kyoto has no habit of eating it every day.

**Saba-zushi** came along the "mackerel road" from Wakasa; see [saba-zushi](/en/eat/sushi-saba/). **Kyō-ryōri** has no entry of its own; the ministry's Kyoto story explains it as five systems — court banquet, *shōjin*, *honzen*, *kaiseki* and *yūsoku*. See [kaiseki](/en/eat/kaiseki/).

### Osaka

[Box sushi](/en/eat/sushi-osaka-hako/), [takoyaki](/en/eat/konamono-takoyaki/), [okonomiyaki](/en/eat/konamono-okonomiyaki/) and [kushikatsu](/en/eat/tonkatsu-kushikatsu/) all have their own pages. The ministry adds that many Osaka homes own a takoyaki maker, and that kushikatsu's "no double-dipping" is because customers share the sauce.

**Senba-jiru.** A clear soup of salted-mackerel trimmings and daikon in kelp stock. Apprentices in Senba's merchant houses saw fish such as salted mackerel only twice a month, and the trimmings went into the stock — "*shimatsu* cooking", wasting nothing. It is still eaten at home and in set-meal restaurants.

**Harihari-nabe.** Whale meat and mizuna, said to have begun at Tokuya, a whale restaurant in Sennichimae founded in 1967; the name is said to be the sound of eating mizuna. The ministry says pork stood in after commercial whaling stopped in the 1980s, that whaling resumed in 2019, and that Osaka once handled much whale because Taiji in Wakayama was a leading whaling base. A 100-year food (fiscal 2024, future).

### Hyogo

**Ikanago no kugi-ni.** Simmered sand lance from Harima, Settsu and Awaji. Kobe is said to be its birthplace; women of the Akashi fishing cooperative spread it with home recipes and classes. Fishing for the young fish opens in late February or early March and lasts about a month, and the ministry says catches have fallen sharply.

**Akashi-yaki.** Dipped in dashi, unlike takoyaki. Long called *tamago-yaki* locally, it was named Akashi-yaki by a city official around 1988 (Shōwa 63). The ministry suggests, without asserting, that it began with yolks left over from making Akashi-dama, an artificial coral.

**Botan-nabe.** Wild boar in mixed white and red miso, from Tamba-Sasayama. Said to have begun around 1908 (Meiji 41), when the army's 70th Infantry Regiment was stationed there and boar caught on exercises went into miso soup, later made into a hotpot at an inn. The word "botan-nabe" appears only in the Shōwa era, from a 1931 call for lyrics to the *Sasayama Kouta*.

**Izushi sara-soba.** Soba on small plates, five to a serving. The ministry dates it to 1706, when the lord Sengoku Masaaki moved from Ueda and brought soba makers; the plates are said to come from late-Edo stalls.

**Kobe beef steak.** The ministry's page says there is no breed called Kobe beef: it is Tajima cattle born and raised in Hyogo and meeting strict standards. See [Kobe beef](/en/eat/wagyu-kobe/) and [Tajima beef](/en/eat/wagyu-tajima/).

**Boiled matsuba-gani.** Snow crab from Tajima. Two theories for the name — pine needles (*matsuba*) burned to boil it, or meat that spreads like pine needles in water. Catches peaked around 1965, then fell sharply.

### Nara

**Kakinoha-zushi.** Salted mackerel and rice in a persimmon leaf, from Gojō and Yoshino, for summer festivals. The ministry gives two origins — Kii fishermen carrying salted Kumano Sea mackerel over the passes to sell at summer festivals, or a change from preserved and soldiers' food. This page gives both. See [kakinoha-zushi](/en/eat/sushi-kakinoha/), and Wakayama below.

**Chagayu.** Cold rice simmered in roasted *hōjicha*. The ministry says Kūkai is said to have sown tea seed from Tang China at Butsuryū-ji in Uda in the early 9th century, and that the menu of Tōdai-ji's *Omizutori* includes gruels called *gecha* and *gobo*. "Yamato's morning begins with tea gruel," goes the saying.

**Nyūmen.** Miwa is held to be where sōmen began, over 1,200 years ago by legend; cold in summer, hot as nyūmen in winter. See [Miwa sōmen](/en/eat/somen-miwa/).

**Kashiwa no sukiyaki.** *Kashiwa* is the Kansai word for chicken; chicken sukiyaki is eaten at the autumn festivals of Tenmangū shrines.

### Wakayama

**Mehari-zushi.** A big rice ball wrapped in salted takana, from the south, a lunch for fishing and mountain work. Three theories for the name — a mouth opened so wide your eyes widen, food so good your eyes widen, or wrapping it as you seal a gap (*mebari*). This page does not choose.

**Umeboshi.** The ministry says Andō Naotsugu, chief retainer of the Tanabe domain, encouraged heavily taxed farmers to grow wild plums on poor, tax-exempt land. The Nankō variety was registered in 1965.

**Onomi sashimi.** Whale tail meat, the costliest cut, from Taiji. The ministry says organised whaling began in Kumano in 1606 under Wada Yorimoto and the old style ended with an 1878 disaster at sea; whaling continues in Taiji in other forms.

**Kakinoha-zushi again.** Wakayama's page puts it in Katsuragi and Kudoyama as a festive dish for autumn festivals, said to date from the Edo period. The ministry's Wakayama story says Hashimoto is held to be the birthplace, with mackerel carried up the Kinokawa by boat. The sources do not say whether Nara's or Wakayama's came first.

Also on the list: *utsubo*, moray eel, best November to March; *okaisan*, tea gruel; and *Kinzanji* miso from Yuasa, said to have been brought in the early 13th century by a priest of Kōkoku-ji in Yura.

### Shiga

**Funa-zushi.** Nigoro-buna carp salted in spring, packed in rice in summer, fermented until New Year; a shrine offering, and long eaten as a remedy for stomach trouble. See [funa-zushi](/en/eat/sushi-funa/).

**Aka-konnyaku.** Konnyaku dyed red with iron oxide, from Ōmi-Hachiman. Theories include Oda Nobunaga having it dyed, but the ministry says no record shows its origin and "the reason it is red remains unknown".

**Yaki-moroko no dorozu.** *Honmoroko*, found only around Lake Biwa, grilled under a "mud vinegar" of miso, sugar, mustard and vinegar. It is listed as critically endangered (IA), but restocking and farming keep it on the table.

**Ōmi beef in miso.** The ministry says the Hikone domain presented miso-cured beef to the shogun's house as a tonic in 1781; this site's [Ōmi beef](/en/eat/wagyu-omi/) page, following the producers' council and the GI file, gives 1771 for the first presentation. I give both.

Also: *ame-no-io gohan*, Biwa trout cooked whole with rice; *saba-sōmen* for the Nagahama Hikiyama festival; and *decchi-yōkan*, from Ōmi merchant families.

### Mie

**Tekone-zushi.** From Ise-Shima: fishermen cut up their catch on board and mixed it by hand (*te-kone*) — bonito or tuna marinated in sauce, on sushi rice. A 100-year food (fiscal 2025, traditional, special award).

**Ise udon.** Soft, thick udon in tamari. In the Edo period, shops kept noodles boiling so that Ise pilgrims could eat at once. The way of eating is old but the name is new: the ministry says it was settled only in 1972, by the Ise city noodle restaurants' association, and that small packs of noodles and sauce, developed in 1968, took it into homes.

**Kōnago no kugi-ni.** *Kōnago* is another name for ikanago, the same fish as Hyogo's. Mie's page says the dish began in Kobe in 1935, and that fishing had been banned for several years as of 2021.

Also: *aho-daki*, over-salted pickled daikon re-simmered, and the *naisho-mochi* of the "mochi road" along the pilgrim routes. Matsusaka beef is not in the farm ministry's list; see [Matsusaka beef](/en/eat/wagyu-matsusaka/).

## What makes it Kinki

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Kyoto 30, Osaka 30, Hyogo 30, Nara 30, Wakayama 27, Shiga 30, Mie 30 — 207 in all. I counted both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages, and the counts matched.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them: Kyoto 4, Osaka 7, Hyogo 8, Nara 4, Wakayama 3, Shiga 6, Mie 10 — 42 in all. Some are on both lists, among them Kyoto's suguki, Osaka's harihari-nabe, Hyogo's botan-nabe, Izushi sara-soba and Akashi-yaki, and Mie's tekone-zushi and Ise udon. Others are only 100-year foods, among them Kyoto's Saikyō white miso, Osaka's *awa-okoshi*, Nara's Yoshino *kuzu*, Wakayama's Yuasa soy sauce, Shiga's Ōtsu eel culture and Yokkaichi's *tonteki*. The two lists are chosen separately.

**What recurs.** Kakinoha-zushi is on the Nara and Wakayama lists; white miso zōni on Kyoto's and Osaka's. Tea gruel and *sanma-zushi* are on Nara's, Wakayama's and Mie's; *kokera-zushi* on Hyogo's, Wakayama's and Mie's; hamo on Kyoto's, Osaka's and Hyogo's.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: *yudōfu*, *obanzai*, Tajima beef, *sobameshi*, Nara-zuke, Wakayama ramen, *kue*, salad bread, Matsusaka beef, Ise-ebi and Akafuku. That is all this says.

## What the seven share

**A capital far from the sea.** The ministry's Kyoto story says fresh fish was hard to get inland, so what came from the Japan Sea and along the Wakasa road was mostly cured — dried cod, herring, salted mackerel. Cooking skill grew from making cured food taste good, and a *ryōtei* owner is quoted on *deaimon*, ingredients that suit each other, as a mark of Kyoto cooking.

**"The nation's kitchen."** The ministry's Osaka story says rice and goods from all over Japan gathered in Osaka's domain warehouses in the Edo period. Osaka city's market history says the central wholesale market opened on 11 November 1931. On stock, the ministry says Osaka prefers rich *ma-kombu* and Kyoto lighter *Rishiri* kelp. See [dashi east and west](/en/eat/dashi-east-west/).

**Mackerel, salt and fermentation.** Kyoto's mackerel road, Shiga's through Kutsuki, the Kumano Sea mackerel of kakinoha-zushi, nare-zushi in Wakayama and Mie's Higashi-Kishū, and Shiga's funa-zushi all keep fish with salt and time. No public source I read says they are connected, so I do not.

**Tea gruel, for different reasons.** Wakayama's okaisan stretched rice where farmland was scarce, and Higashi-Kishū ate tea gruel because rice was precious. Nara's story says the opposite of its own: not to save rice, but to eat quickly and get back to the fields.

I found no public source that calls Kinki or Kyoto food "light", so I do not.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the official pages describe them:

- **Senmaizuke and suguki**: pickle shops; suguki is a year-end gift.
- **Akashi-yaki**: about 70 shops in Akashi (2021).
- **Izushi sara-soba**: about 40 shops in Izushi (2021).
- **Tekone-zushi**: many restaurants in Ise-Shima.
- **Senba-jiru, Ise udon, aka-konnyaku**: homes, supermarkets and school lunches.

**Prices.** I found no official tax-inclusive price for any dish on these lists. The nearest is Ise-ebi, spiny lobster, which is not on them. Mie's tourism federation (article dated 21 August 2026, reported in November 2020 and partly revised, with a note that prices may change) lists an Ise-ebi course at Daiki in Ise from ¥14,300, tax included (checked 11 October 2026).

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** Suguki is harvested mid-November to January, and senmaizuke is made from around November. Hamo runs from early summer into autumn. Ikanago fishing lasts about a month from late February or early March. Matsuba-gani is November to March, botan-nabe a winter dish for New Year and guests, white miso zōni for the first three days of the year, and chagayu an everyday breakfast.

**Ise-ebi.** The prefecture says fishing is closed from May, the spawning season, to the end of September; a restaurant notice in the federation's article asks you to check ahead from mid-July to mid-August. The two differ, and I do not settle it.

## Not all of Kinki is this

**Two kakinoha-zushi.** Nara's for summer festivals in Gojō and Yoshino; Wakayama's for autumn festivals in Katsuragi and Kudoyama.

**One fish, two names.** Hyogo's ikanago is Mie's kōnago.

**Mehari-zushi across a border.** It is on Wakayama's list, not Nara's — Nara's *mana-mehari* is a different dish — yet Totsukawa in Nara presents it as local.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Yunoyama Onsen and sōhei-nabe.** Mie's tourism federation describes *sōhei-nabe*, a hotpot the warrior monks of Sangaku-ji at Yunoyama are said to have eaten for stamina — mountain food and meat such as boar in several misos — served at Yunoyama's inns, about 10 minutes by bus from Yunoyama-Onsen station on the Kintetsu line. Sōhei-nabe is not in the farm ministry's list.

**[Nanki-Katsuura](/en/soak/nanki-katsuura/) and fresh tuna.** Wakayama prefecture gives Katsuura port's fresh tuna landings in 2018 as 8,979 tonnes, first in Japan, and the Nachi-Katsuura tourism bureau lists tuna bowls near Kii-Katsuura station alongside the town's baths. There is no tuna item on Wakayama's farm ministry list. Bathing at Yurinoyama Onsen is ¥400 for adults, tax included (checked 11 October 2026).

**Yunomine and Kawayu.** The Kumano Hongū tourism association says you can boil eggs and vegetables in [Yunomine](/en/soak/yunomine/)'s *yuzutsu*, a spring at 90°C (6:00–21:00); the environment ministry's spa plan gives the Yuzutsu source at 84.3°C. Its food page lists a café at Kawayu serving home-made mehari-zushi.

**Totsukawa.** The village tourism association introduces mehari-zushi, once a lunch for mountain work and now for walking the old pilgrim road, and *Totsukawa yubeshi*, a hollowed yuzu filled with miso, walnut and sesame and dried in the cold wind; the same site lists Tōsenji and Totsukawa Onsen. There is no railway: about four hours by bus from Yamato-Yagi, the village says.

**Ogoto and funa-zushi.** The [Ogoto Onsen](/en/soak/ogoto/) tourism association's English page names Ōmi beef, Biwa trout, ayu and funa-zushi as food served there. Funa-zushi is on the farm ministry's Shiga list, which does not name Ogoto.

**No link found.** A June 2013 report on Mie's tourism site describes a Sakakibara Onsen inn's summer kaiseki with Ise-ebi and Matsusaka beef; it may not be current. For matsuba-gani and the spa town, see [Kinosaki](/en/soak/kinosaki/); for sanma-zushi on that coast, [Shirahama](/en/soak/shirahama/).

**Getting there.** Official figures from different bodies and dates; check before you travel.

- **Kansai airport**: about 47 minutes to Osaka by *Haruka* (JR West, March 2023 timetable); about 75 minutes to Kyoto (Kyoto city).
- **Nara**: about 34 minutes from Kyoto or Osaka-Namba by Kintetsu.
- **Ōtsu**: about 40 minutes from Osaka, about 9 from Kyoto.
- **Ise-Shima**: the Kintetsu *Shimakaze* leaves Osaka-Namba at 10:40 and reaches Toba at 12:31 (not on Tuesdays).
- **Shirahama**: about 2 hours 40 minutes from Shin-Osaka by *Kuroshio*; **Kii-Katsuura** about four hours (JR West timetable, 13 October 2026).

## Kinki food away from Kinki

- **Nara.** Nara Mahoroba-kan is now in Shimbashi (1-8-4 Shimbashi), with a shop, a café and a restaurant working with Nara producers.
- **Shiga.** Coco Shiga, 2-7-1 Nihonbashi, with an Ōmi beef restaurant upstairs.
- **Mie.** Mie Terrace, Nihonbashi Muromachi, whose restaurant serves "local dishes and earthenware-pot dishes".
- **Wakayama.** Wakayama Kishū-kan in Yūrakuchō, enlarged with an eat-in corner on 7 June 2024.
- **Kyoto, Osaka, Hyogo.** Kyoto-kan is now a website; I found no official Tokyo shop for Osaka or Hyogo.

**What changes.** At the source these dishes have seasons and settings — crab and sand lance with short seasons, zōni for three days, funa-zushi at festivals. The Tokyo shops open all year. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ise udon is an old name."** The way of eating it is old; the name dates from 1972.
- **"Kobe beef is a breed."** The ministry says it is Tajima cattle that meet a standard.
- **"Kakinoha-zushi is Nara's alone."** Wakayama lists it too, with a different story.
- **"Matsusaka beef is Mie's official local dish."** Matsusaka beef is not in the farm ministry's list.
- **"Nara's Tokyo shop is in Nihonbashi."** It is in Shimbashi now.
