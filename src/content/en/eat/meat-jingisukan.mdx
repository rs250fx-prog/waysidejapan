*Jingisukan* is lamb or mutton grilled on a domed pan, with vegetables round the rim. The name is the Japanese reading of Genghis Khan, and that is the source of a common mistake about it. The farm ministry says it outright: the dish is often thought to be Mongolian, and it was born in Hokkaido. This page covers its competing origin stories, the pan, the two Hokkaido styles, and the awkward fact behind the meat.

For Japan’s other meats in general, start from the meat overview.

## Where it came from

There are several accounts, and the official sources themselves say the origin is disputed. This site sets them side by side and does not choose.

- **Devised in Hokkaido, early Shōwa.** The farm ministry’s children’s page says the dish was thought up in Hokkaido at the start of the Shōwa era, so that people would eat more of the mutton then being produced in quantity.
- **Wool first, meat later.** The ministry’s local-food page says that in the Taishō era the First World War made imported wool hard to get, and the state encouraged sheep farming. Hokkaido took it up, and mutton began to be eaten around then. After the war, imported wool and synthetic fibre spread, and sheep farming shifted to meat. Jingisukan, the page says, is a dish that settled in as mutton eating grew.
- **Modelled on a Chinese dish.** The same page gives one origin theory: that in the early Shōwa era it was devised for Japanese people unused to mutton, with reference to a Chinese dish of grilled mutton (*kao yang rou*).
- **Seen in Manchuria.** The ministry’s traditional-food guide gives another: that Japanese who went to Manchuria in the Taishō era saw mutton being grilled there.
- **Sheep stations.** The Hokkaido Heritage site, run by a private body, says that in the Taishō era a plan to raise a million sheep, to supply wool for uniforms, put sheep stations in Takikawa and in Tsukisamu in Sapporo, where ways of eating the meat were also studied. Spread to the general public, it says, came after the war. Hokkaido’s Sorachi subprefectural office says sheep stations were opened in five places across Japan, including Takikawa and Sapporo, and that the first recipe is said to appear in a 1931 book, *Men’yō to sono kaikata* (Sheep and how to keep them).

These do not quite agree on dates: one source has the dish devised in the early Shōwa era, another a recipe in print in 1931, another spreading widely only after the war. The fair reading is to keep apart when it was devised and when ordinary people started eating it.

**The name.** The traditional-food guide says the name is said to come from Genghis Khan feeding his soldiers mutton and vegetables grilled together in wartime. The Hokkaido Heritage site mentions that there are other theories, including a link to the legend of Minamoto no Yoshitsune, without detail.

## What makes it jingisukan

**The pan.** A dedicated pan whose centre rises in a dome. The ministry’s local-food page explains the design: the centre is raised and the grilling surface has grooves, so juices from the meat run down the grooves to the vegetables round the edge and season them. The traditional-food guide adds the practical points: oil the whole pan so nothing sticks, grill the meat in the middle with the vegetables around it, do not overcook the meat or it turns hard, and at the end use the sauce pooled at the rim to cook udon or similar. The local-food page notes that Hokkaido households have a jingisukan pan.

**Lamb or mutton.** The ministry’s local-food page draws the line by age: lamb is under one year old and has little smell; mutton is two years or older, fattier, with a fuller flavour.

**Two styles.** The local-food page gives the reason there are two: mutton has a smell of its own, so it is either grilled raw and dipped in sauce, or marinated first in a soy-based sauce and then grilled.

- **Marinated (*ajitsuke*), the Takikawa style.** The meat is soaked in sauce before grilling. The traditional-food guide says this is the norm in inland central Hokkaido, in Takikawa. The Hokkaido Heritage site describes the Takikawa marinade as grated local onion and apple. The Sorachi office says Takikawa is known as the birthplace of marinated jingisukan, and lists six makers in the city.
- **Sauce afterwards, the Sapporo style.** The meat is grilled unseasoned and dipped in sauce from a separate dish. The guide says this is the norm in cities such as Sapporo and Tsukisamu and on the central Hokkaido coast.

**A home sauce.** The local-food page prints a home recipe credited to Nanbu Akiko’s *Hokkaidō no aji*: sake, soy, vinegar, salt and sugar, with grated garlic, ginger, apple and pear, white sesame and *shichimi* chilli.

**A day.** The farm ministry gives 29 April as Jingisukan Day, a pun on *yō-niku*, mutton. The Hokkaido Heritage site names the registering body only as the Kinenbi Kyōkai, an anniversary association.

## Where the lamb comes from

The farm ministry’s June 2024 report on sheep and goats gives the numbers.

- **Sheep.** In 2023 Japan had 24,631 sheep on 999 farms. Hokkaido had 13,659, or 58 percent. The next largest flocks were much smaller: Iwate 1,111, Tochigi 855, Nagano 802.
- **Meat.** In FY2022 domestic lamb and mutton production was 98 tonnes and imports 20,187 tonnes. Domestic meat was 0.5 percent of the total.
- **Imports.** In FY2022, 72 percent came from Australia and 25 percent from New Zealand.

So Hokkaido is where Japan’s sheep are, and most of the meat on a jingisukan pan, by those figures, is imported. The ministry’s local-food pages do not describe how the meat is distributed; they mention only that supermarkets sell lamb and sauce for home cooking.

## How to eat it

From the ministry’s guide: oil the pan, put the meat in the centre and the vegetables around it, take the meat off before it toughens, and let the vegetables cook in the juices. In the Sapporo style, dip each piece in the sauce; in the Takikawa style the seasoning is already on. At the end, the guide suggests cooking udon in the sauce left at the rim.

I could not confirm what "*nama* lamb" on a menu means from an official source, so I do not define it. When the Hokkaido Heritage site says the Sapporo style grills "raw meat", it means meat that has not been marinated.

## Not all jingisukan is this

The differences the sources confirm are inside Hokkaido: marinated in Takikawa and inland, grilled then dipped in Sapporo and on the coast. Sheep are also kept in Iwate, Tochigi and Nagano, but I have no source on how jingisukan is eaten there.

## Jingisukan away from Hokkaido

I could not find an official source comparing jingisukan in Hokkaido with jingisukan in Tokyo or elsewhere, so I make no comparison. What the figures say applies everywhere: almost all of Japan’s lamb and mutton is imported, whether you eat it in Sapporo or anywhere else.

## What people get wrong

- **"It is Mongolian barbecue."** The farm ministry says it was born in Hokkaido.
- **"The name proves the origin."** The Genghis Khan story is given only as something that is said, and other theories exist.
- **"It is Hokkaido lamb."** Hokkaido has most of the sheep, but in FY2022 domestic meat was 0.5 percent of supply.
- **"There is one jingisukan."** Takikawa marinates; Sapporo dips.
- **"Lamb and mutton are the same."** The ministry separates them by age: under a year, and two years or more.
