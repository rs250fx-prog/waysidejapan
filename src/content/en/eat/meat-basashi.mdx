*Basashi* is raw horse, sliced thin and eaten with soy sauce and sharp condiments. The farm ministry’s local-food database has three separate entries for it — Kumamoto, Yamanashi and Aizu in Fukushima — and each region tells a different story about why it eats horse at all. This page covers all three, then turns to wild deer and boar, because the rules for them are the opposite: horse is served raw under controls, and wild meat is never to be eaten raw.

For Japan’s other meats in general, start from the meat overview.

## Where it came from

**Kumamoto.** The farm ministry gives the leading theory, while noting there are others: Katō Kiyomasa, first lord of the Kumamoto domain, ran out of food during the invasion of Korea, ate his army’s horses, found them good, and kept eating horse after he came home. In the Edo era only some farmers and others ate it. In the Meiji era it spread to Kumamoto and the Aso area; after the war, in the years of food shortage, it began to be eaten in Aso; and in the Shōwa 30s (1955–64) restaurants began to serve it.

**Yamanashi.** The ministry’s Yamanashi page starts with geography and religion. The old province of Kai was known from the time of the *ritsuryō* state as a province that supplied horses, and many were kept to carry loads for pilgrims climbing Mount Fuji. Because horses were close at hand, the page says, horse-meat dishes developed using meat that was easy to get.

**Aizu.** The Fukushima page says it is said to have started around the Boshin War, when horse meat was fed to wounded soldiers brought into the region. Aizu-Wakamatsu is inland, fresh raw fish was hard to get, and horse sashimi took the place of fish sashimi — to the point that locally "sashimi" is said to mean horse. At first horse meat was usually eaten cooked. The page says it is reported that raw basashi spread after a popular professional wrestler visited and ate it raw.

## What makes it basashi

**Kumamoto: sweet soy, cut to order.** Thin slices of raw horse dipped in sweet soy sauce. The meat is called *sakura-niku*, "cherry-blossom meat", because the cut face is pink. It oxidises easily and darkens with time, so shops slice it after you order. The fat melts at a low temperature, so the ministry advises eating it promptly. The condiments are sliced onion, grated ginger, grated garlic and small green onion. A popular variant is *sakura-nattō*: basashi chopped fine and mixed with nattō, a quail egg and green onion.

**Yamanashi: your choice of condiment.** Raw horse with grated ginger, garlic, leek, *shichimi* chilli, wasabi or other condiments as you like, and soy sauce. It is eaten all year round. The Yamanashi page gives four explanations for the name *sakura-niku* and does not choose between them:

- the meat is the colour of cherry blossom;
- horse that has fattened over winter is best in spring;
- in the Edo era, game meat was called "sakura" as a euphemism;
- a song attributed to Sakamoto Ryōma.

**Aizu: karashi-miso.** Soy sauce with *karashi-miso*, a mustard-miso, is the usual way. The Fukushima page says this too is held to have taken root because the same wrestler brought it. It adds that Aizu horse meat is said to have little marbling in the red meat, and that it is basically eaten at home, with few chances to have it eating out. A local group, the Aizu Brand Horse Meat Sakura Association, promotes 29 August as Horse Meat Day.

## How it is controlled

The Kumamoto page is specific. Kumamoto is described as Japan’s largest producer of horse meat. There are two labels:

- **"Kumamoto-san" horse meat** — horses born and raised in Kumamoto;
- **"Kumamoto" horse meat** — foals imported from Canada and elsewhere and raised in Kumamoto for about one to one and a half years.

Today horses are butchered in hygiene-controlled meat plants, and the meat is always frozen before it is distributed, as a measure against parasites and similar risks.

## How to eat it

- **In Kumamoto**, eat the slices soon after they come, because the fat melts and the meat darkens. Use the onion, ginger and garlic with the sweet soy.
- **In Yamanashi**, pick from the condiments and add soy.
- **In Aizu**, use soy with karashi-miso — though the ministry notes that most basashi in Aizu is eaten at home, not in restaurants.

## Deer and wild boar are not this

The farm ministry defines *gibier* (jibie) as the meat of wild birds and animals used for food. It says crop damage by deer and boar has become a problem in many areas, and as more are caught, using the meat as food has spread nationwide. The official writing on it is almost entirely about safety.

**Never raw.** The farm ministry warns that eating raw or undercooked wild deer or boar carries a risk of food poisoning from hepatitis E virus, enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli* and parasites. Its advice is to cook the meat right through and to disinfect anything that touched it raw.

**The number.** The health ministry’s web magazine gives the figure: heat to 75°C at the centre for one minute or more. Its reasons:

- wild animals, unlike livestock, have no managed feed or health, so nobody knows what pathogens they carry;
- eating them raw or underdone is "very dangerous";
- freezing is not enough — the meat must still be cooked through, because the trichinella worm is resistant to freezing;
- the main pathogens are hepatitis E virus (incubation 15 to 50 days), enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli*, and trichinella, which in Japan has been reported from bear meat.

The health ministry adds that hepatitis E in pregnant women tends to become severe, with a fatality rate that can reach 20 percent.

**Where the meat comes from.** Processing wild animals for food as a business falls under the Food Sanitation Act. Restaurants and shops must buy meat butchered at a facility licensed for meat processing. The health ministry has hygiene guidelines for businesses, last revised on 26 June 2023, with a colour atlas revised on 19 March 2025.

**A certification scheme.** The farm ministry runs a national certification for game-processing facilities that follow hygiene standards, distribute to a cutting chart, and label for traceability. Its list ran from No. 001 to No. 043 when checked, the latest certified on 4 March 2026, with some numbers missing. Which animals and what standards it covers in detail, I could not confirm.

I could not find an official source on regional deer or boar dishes, so none are described here.

## Basashi away from home

I could not find an official source comparing basashi in its home regions with basashi in Tokyo or elsewhere. The differences the sources do show are between the regions: sweet soy in Kumamoto, karashi-miso in Aizu, free choice of condiments in Yamanashi; restaurants in Kumamoto since 1955–64, mostly home eating in Aizu. One rule travels with the meat: Kumamoto horse is always frozen before distribution. For wild game, the rule is national — shops anywhere must buy from licensed facilities.

I could not confirm a claim that Fukushima is the second-largest producer after Kumamoto, or an official source on horse meat in Nagano, so neither is stated.

## What people get wrong

- **"Raw meat is raw meat."** Horse for basashi in Kumamoto is frozen before distribution. Wild deer and boar should be cooked to 75°C at the centre for a minute or more.
- **"Freezing makes game safe."** The health ministry says trichinella survives freezing.
- **"Basashi is one dish."** Kumamoto, Yamanashi and Aizu each have their own history and dip.
- **"Kumamoto horse means a Kumamoto horse."** "Kumamoto" horse meat can be from foals imported from Canada and elsewhere and raised there; "Kumamoto-san" means born and raised there.
- **"Sakura-niku is named for its colour."** That is one of four explanations the Yamanashi page gives.
