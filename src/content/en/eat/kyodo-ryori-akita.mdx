Akita's best-known dishes are hotpots: rice and chicken in one, a fish that arrives with the winter thunder in the other. The farm ministry's database lists 29 Akita dishes, among them kiritanpo hotpot, shottsuru hotpot, *damako* hotpot, smoked pickled radish (*iburi-gakko*), hatahata sushi, nattō soup and taro soup. This page covers the two best-known, and the argument between two towns over one of them.

For how Japan records local dishes, start from the local-dishes overview.

## Kiritanpo hotpot

**Where it came from.** The farm ministry says it began in the Ōdate and Kazuno area, where people who went up into the mountains to burn charcoal or fell trees pounded their leftover rice, pressed it onto sticks, toasted it, and added it to a bird stew. The school nutritionists' association tells a version with *matagi* hunters, who wrapped rice round cedar skewers, toasted it over charcoal and simmered it with pheasant or mountain birds.

**The name.** Two explanations:

- **The farm ministry**: the toasted rice looked like the head of a bulrush, so it was called *tanpo*, "short head", and *kiri-tanpo* is tanpo cut to fit the pot.
- **The nutritionists' association**: *tanpo* was the cover over a spear blade, which the rice on its stick resembled.

This page gives both.

**Hinai chicken.** The ministry says Hinai fowl, once essential to the dish, was designated a national natural monument and for a time could not be eaten; at the call of the mayor of Hinai town, *Hinai-jidori* was bred, and the dish returned to home tables.

**When it is eaten.** Mostly in the new-rice season. In northern Akita families gather round the pot when the harvest is in. In Ōdate it is home cooking and also indispensable for guests, weddings and funerals. Nowadays "kiritanpo" generally means the hotpot.

**How it is made.** Slightly firm rice for the tanpo; stock from chicken bones; chicken, burdock, shirataki, maitake, seasoned with soy, sake and salt; cut tanpo, leek and *seri* added for a final simmer. Recipes differ by area and household.

**Recognition.** One of the 100 local dishes chosen in 2007, Akita prefecture says; a traditional 100-year food (2021) with a special award, described as a hotpot with Hinai chicken stock from Ōdate.

## The birthplace argument

Ōdate holds the *Honba Ōdate Kiritanpo Matsuri* ("home of kiritanpo") and Kazuno the *Kiritanpo Hasshō Matsuri* ("birthplace of kiritanpo") every year, and the farm ministry says the two cities compete. Kazuno's roadside station states flatly that kiritanpo was born in Kazuno; its Kiritanpo-kan serves the hotpot in limited numbers, miso-grilled tanpo, and lets you shape and toast your own (its prices do not state tax, so none are given). This page does not choose a winner.

## Shottsuru hotpot

**The sauce.** *Shottsuru* is Akita's fish sauce: fish, mainly hatahata, salted and left a year or more until it dissolves, then strained. The farm ministry says it was devised as a substitute when soy sauce was a luxury.

**The fish.** Hatahata normally live about 500 m down, and appear near the coast in huge shoals for a short spawning season, when thunder rolls over rough seas — hence the names *hatahata-gami*, "thunder god", and *kaminari-uo*, "thunder fish". The waters off Akita are among Japan's main grounds, and it is the classic winter hotpot.

**The dish.** Its birthplace is said to be Oga. It is eaten mainly in November and December, with tofu, leek and Chinese cabbage. In Oga people sometimes eat a soup of hatahata alone. Homes eat it, and restaurants always serve it in winter.

## Akita's 100-year foods

The Cultural Agency lists ten for Akita. Traditional: kiritanpo (2021, special award), white rice cake (2021), iburi-gakko (2023, special award), Nishimonai soba (2023). Modern: jumbo rabbit dishes (2021), Akita *tsukudani* (2024). Future: Akita *kayaki* (2021), Yokote yakisoba (2022), Kosaka-machi katsu ramen (2023), Kazuno offal (2024).

## Akita food away from Akita

- **The Tokyo shop.** Akita Bisaikan in Takanawa, set up by the prefecture and run by a contractor, serves Inaniwa udon, kiritanpo, hatahata and Hinai chicken in a 74-seat restaurant, 11:00–23:00, and sells pickles, shottsuru and sake; it ships nationwide online.
- **Season.** At home, kiritanpo follows the new rice and shottsuru follows the hatahata in November and December. A Tokyo restaurant serves them all year.
- **Occasion.** In Ōdate, kiritanpo is a home, guest and ceremonial dish.
- **No taste comparison.** I found no official one, and make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kiritanpo is a skewer snack."** "Kiritanpo" now usually means the hotpot.
- **"It has one birthplace."** Ōdate and Kazuno both claim it.
- **"Shottsuru is just soy sauce."** It is fish sauce from fermented hatahata.
- **"Hatahata are always available."** They come inshore only for a short spawning season.
