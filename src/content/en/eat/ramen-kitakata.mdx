Kitakata is a small town in the Aizu basin of Fukushima, inland and snowy, known for its *kura* — the thick-walled storehouses that line its streets, more than 4,000 of them by the prefecture's count. It has about 40,000 people and around 90 shops serving ramen. Some of them open at seven in the morning, and the people eating there are not tourists.

That is the whole story of Kitakata ramen in one scene: a quiet style, in a quiet town, eaten at an hour when the rest of Japan is having toast or rice.

## Where it came from

The tourism bodies for the town and the region agree on the outline. In the late Taishō or early Shōwa years — the mid-1920s — a young man from China sold *shina soba* from a cart in the town. Local eateries learned the method from him, and the town's noodle makers refined it into what is now called Kitakata ramen. The shop regarded as the origin was Genraiken, a Chinese restaurant founded by that man, Pan Qinxing (潘欽星), and it carried the word "original" (元祖) over its door until it closed in September 2025, after roughly a hundred years.

The style stayed local for half a century. What changed was the storehouses. From the 1970s Kitakata became known as a "town of kura", and in 1983 it tied up with a travel-guide publisher. The ramen rode along: by the agriculture ministry's account, 1984 is when Kitakata became the first town in Japan to attempt to revive itself through ramen. At the peak in the 1980s the town had a population in the 30,000s and some 120 ramen shops. In 1987, 40 shops and five noodle makers formed an association, the Rōmenkai; in 1988 a Tokyo company began a franchise chain under the Bannai name, which is how most of Japan outside Fukushima first ate Kitakata ramen.

## What makes it Kitakata ramen

**The noodle is the point.** It is flat, wavy, thick, and made with a lot of water — the term used locally is *jukusei takasui men*, "aged high-water noodle". The tourism federation describes it as containing noticeably more water than an ordinary noodle; the effect is a slippery, springy chew that no other regional style quite has. Compare it with the dry, hair-thin Hakata noodle and you are looking at the two ends of the Japanese spectrum.

**The soup is light.** Soy sauce is the base, though the shops vary widely, and salt and back-fat versions have become popular. Pork bone is the usual stock, with dried sardine at some shops. Bannai Shokudō, probably the best-known shop today, serves a clear salt-based bowl on a pork stock; Makoto Shokudō, until it closed in 2023, was the reference for the soy version.

**The pork.** Slices of slow-cooked pork belly. At Bannai Shokudō the signature bowl is the *niku soba*, in which the pork covers the surface so completely that you cannot see the noodles.

## Why Kitakata

Two things, both to do with the storehouses. The first is water: the town sits on soft water from the Iide mountains (the tourism association cites a hardness of 13, among the softest in Japan), which is why it had brewers of sake, soy sauce and miso in the first place — and good soy sauce and good water are most of a Kitakata soup. The second is the morning. The town has long had the habit of eating ramen early, and the tourism association describes *asa-rā* as a local breakfast first and a tourist attraction second; JR East's campaigns made it famous, but did not invent it.

The density is real but the superlative is not sourced. "Most ramen shops per head in Japan" is a claim you will see everywhere; I could not find a public body that states it, so this page says only what the region's tourism office says: about 90 shops for a town of roughly 40,000–50,000 people.

- **Bannai Shokudō** (細田 7230). Open 07:00 to 18:00 (last orders 17:50), closed Thursdays and some Wednesdays. Its prices are not published online, so none are given here.
- **Genraiken** closed in September 2025. **Makoto Shokudō** closed in September 2023. Both were still trading when most English-language guides were written; check before you plan a day around a named shop.
- **Kitakata Ramen Bannai** (the chain). Operated by Menshoku Co. of Tokyo, which traces its recipe to Bannai Shokudō; the first franchise shop opened in 1988. The chain's menu lists the basic Kitakata ramen at ¥890, negi ramen ¥1,020, chāshū ramen ¥1,360, wonton ramen ¥1,130, rice ¥200, five gyōza ¥390, a large portion for ¥160 extra. The menu page does not state whether tax is included, and notes that some branches do not carry every item.

## How it is eaten

Early. Bannai Shokudō opens at seven, and until it closed Makoto Shokudō did too. The *niku soba* — the pork-covered bowl — is the thing to order at Bannai Shokudō; at the chain the same idea is sold as *yakibuta rāmen*. Beyond that there is no system to learn: no hardness scale, no refill, no order form. You sit, you order, you eat. If you want the town's version of the experience, go before nine.

## Nearby, and different

Fukushima has a second ramen town, and Yamagata a third, and they are often grouped with Kitakata as the "three great ramens of Japan" — a phrase whose origin I could not trace to any authority, so treat it as marketing.

- **Shirakawa**, in southern Fukushima, makes hand-cut wavy noodles using soba-making technique, in a clear soy soup on chicken and pork stock. Its tourism association dates the lineage to a shop opened in 1887 and a hand-pulled-noodle shop in 1921, and counts more than 100 shops around the city.
- **Yonezawa**, across the mountains in Yamagata, uses a thin wavy noodle, high in water and hand-kneaded after resting, in a light soy soup on chicken and dried-sardine stock; guidebooks count more than 100 shops there too.

The three share the light soy soup and the wavy noodle. What separates Kitakata is the sheer width of the noodle, the salt-and-pork alternative, and the morning.

## Getting there

Kitakata is on JR's Ban'etsu West line, which runs from Kōriyama on the Tōhoku Shinkansen through Aizu-Wakamatsu to Kitakata. In the October 2026 timetable the morning trains out of Kōriyama mostly terminate at Aizu-Wakamatsu, where you change; the direct services to Kitakata (including the Akabeko limited express and the Aizu Satono rapid) leave Kōriyama around 10:00. If the point is morning ramen, that means either staying the night before in Kitakata or Aizu-Wakamatsu, or accepting a late-morning bowl — the shops are still open. I have not been able to confirm the fare and journey time from an official page, so none are given here.

## What people get wrong

- **"It is a tourist dish."** The morning habit belongs to the town. Tourism came after the storehouses, in the 1980s.
- **"The famous shops are all still there."** Two of the three names in every guide have closed since 2023.
- **"Kitakata ramen is the Bannai chain."** The chain is a Tokyo franchise from 1988; the shop it is named after is in Kitakata and opens at seven.
- **"It is always soy sauce."** The best-known shop today serves salt.
- **"It has the most ramen shops per capita in Japan."** Often said, never sourced. About 90 shops for 40,000–50,000 people is remarkable enough without the superlative.
