Most visitors meet karaage as fried chicken. Yet by the definition of the one organisation that has written one down, not really a chicken dish at all but a way of frying. Where it began has several answers, and none of them is settled. Where it lives is clearer: two towns in Oita prefecture, Usa and Nakatsu, built a whole category of specialist shop around it.

For chicken in Japan generally — what jidori means and how the parts are named — start from the chicken overview.

## Where it came from

The Japan Karaage Association, founded in 2008 and incorporated in 2010, runs tests and a qualification for karaage specialists (it calls them *karaagenists*) and sets out the history on its site. Its account, and the accounts of Oita prefecture and Nakatsu city, give several origins at different scales. This site lists them and does not choose.

- **The word.** In *fucha* cooking, which came to Japan in the early Edo period, 唐揚げ was read *karaage* or *tōage*. The association quotes the *Fucha Ryōri Shō* of 1772: tofu cut small, fried in oil, then simmered in soy sauce and sake. That is not today’s karaage.
- **The method.** A cookbook of 1803, *Shirōto Bōchō*, describes something closer to the modern dish under the names *iridashi* and *koromo-kake*.
- **The restaurant dish.** Around 1932, the chef of a Ginza chicken branch of Shokudō Mikasa, the forerunner of Mikasa Kaikan, is said to have created "young chicken karaage" for the menu.
- **Oita.** The association links Oita’s karaage to postwar national poultry policy, meant to guard against food shortages. Oita prefecture’s magazine gives three explanations side by side: there were many poultry farms around Nakatsu, Buzen and Usa, so chicken was cheap; without refrigerators, people kept meat in salt or soy; and people returning from former Manchuria brought the frying method with them. Nakatsu city gives its own list — plentiful chicken, repatriates recreating what they ate in China, and a deli whose freshly fried karaage became popular and was copied — and says each is only what "is said".
- **Imabari’s *senzanki*.** The association counts this dish as a kind of karaage, and gives two stories: pheasants from Mt Chikami fried in oil, or a man called "Sen-san" who invented it. It adds that no written record has been found.

## What makes it karaage

**A method, not a meat.** The association’s definition: karaage (also written から揚げ or 空揚げ) is a way of cooking in frying oil, or the dish made that way, in which the ingredient is dusted lightly with wheat flour or katakuri-ko starch and fried. It is not limited to chicken; fish, vegetables and other meats count. The association treats から揚げ as the same thing and writes 唐揚げ itself.

**No line against tatsuta-age.** The association’s definition, history page and FAQ say nothing about *tatsuta-age*, and I could not find an official source that separates the two. So this page does not.

**The Nakatsu way.** Nakatsu city describes what its specialist shops do. The chicken is marinated in a sauce based on soy or salt, with garlic, ginger and about ten seasonings in all. The sauce, the marinating time and the hidden ingredients differ from shop to shop, so each tastes different. It is fried after you order. And some shops work at making it good to eat cold.

**Parts, not just pieces.** Two Nakatsu shops featured in Oita prefecture’s magazine list thigh (*momo*), breast (*mune*) and a mix as the standards, alongside neck (*seseri*), gizzard (*zuri*), breastbone cartilage (*yagen nankotsu*), boneless thigh and breast, wing tips (*tebasaki*) and skin (*tori-kawa*). The names match the farm ministry’s trading standard for chicken parts.

## Usa and Nakatsu are not the same claim

It is easy to read that Nakatsu is the birthplace of karaage shops. The association’s history page says something else:

- **Usa is the birthplace of the specialist shop.** A shop called Shōsuke, which learned its method from Rairaiken in Usa, was the first in Japan to open as a karaage specialist, in the Shōwa 30s (1955–64). On that basis the association calls Usa the birthplace of karaage specialist shops.
- **Nakatsu is the "sacred ground".** The same page calls Nakatsu the holy place of karaage and says it has more than 60 specialist shops. The association does not run a certification for this; the word appears in its event descriptions, not as a title it awards.

**How many shops?** In Oita prefecture’s magazine, the local karaage group is said to have about 17 member shops, while a shop owner puts the number of specialist shops in the city at 60 or more — and says there were fewer than ten when he was at high school. Those are probably counting different things. Nakatsu city’s karaage map, dated October 2023, lists the shops but gives no total in its text. I could not find a current official count.

**The name is recent.** The magazine says "Nakatsu karaage" took hold as a name only during the boom in local and B-grade food. Before that, people talked about "the karaage shops of Nakatsu". One owner offers his own definition — domestic chicken, seasoned firmly with soy, garlic and ginger so it needs nothing added, and a head shop in Nakatsu — but that is one person’s summary, not an official one.

**The contest.** The association holds an annual *Karaage Grand Prix*. It announced the 17th results on 10 April 2026 and the 18th contest on 18 June 2026. Nakatsu city reported a record haul of awards for its shops at the 17th; its own list did not match the number it gave, so I have not repeated one.

## The shops, and what they charge

I could not find tax-included prices published by Nakatsu or Usa specialists or by restaurant chains. Two convenience-store examples, tax included, as posted on 2026-10-10:

- **Lawson, Karaage-kun regular** — ¥288 for five pieces.
- **FamilyMart, Famichiki** — ¥248, listed by FamilyMart as fried chicken rather than karaage, at the 8% reduced tax rate.

As of 2026-10-10, takeaway food is taxed at 8% and eating in at 10%. Neither page states a weight.

## How to order it

At a Nakatsu specialist, going by the city’s description and the shops in the prefecture’s magazine:

- **Choose the part.** Thigh, breast or a mix are the standards; many shops add neck, gizzard, cartilage, wings or skin.
- **Expect to wait.** It is fried after you order.
- **It keeps.** The city says some shops work at making it good to eat cold.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Not all of Japan’s fried chicken is this

- **Toriten (Beppu, Oita).** The farm ministry says Oita city ranked first for household chicken consumption in a 2019 government survey of prefectural capitals and designated cities, and that alongside karaage, Oita eats *toriten*: chicken fried in tempura batter. The ministry traces it to Tōyōken in Beppu in the early Shōwa era, where bone-in karaage was thought hard for women to eat, so boneless thigh was cut into bite-sized pieces and fried like tempura. It is eaten with vinegared soy and mustard, or ponzu or kabosu. The prefecture’s magazine also mentions the view that toriten began as a variation on karaage.
- **Senzanki (Imabari, Ehime).** Counted by the association as a kind of karaage, with no written record of how it began.
- **The convenience-store hot snack.** Lawson’s Karaage-kun and FamilyMart’s Famichiki sit on the counter; FamilyMart calls the second one fried chicken, not karaage.

## Karaage away from Oita

What I could confirm about Nakatsu is the system — fried to order, a marinade that differs by shop, a menu of parts — and that shop menus vary. What I could not find is an official comparison between karaage in Nakatsu and karaage from Nakatsu-style shops in Tokyo or elsewhere, in taste, quality or price. This site does not say the home town’s is better. If a shop elsewhere calls itself Nakatsu karaage, the one owner’s definition above (a head shop in Nakatsu) is a personal view, not a rule I can confirm.

## What people get wrong

- **"Karaage means chicken."** By the association’s definition, it is a frying method for any ingredient.
- **"Nakatsu invented the karaage shop."** The association places the first specialist shop in Usa; Nakatsu is where the shops multiplied.
- **"Karaage is an old dish."** The word is old and once meant fried, simmered tofu; the chicken dish on a restaurant menu is dated to around 1932.
- **"Karaage and tatsuta-age are defined differently."** Perhaps, but I could not find an official source that says how.
- **"Nakatsu karaage has an official recipe."** The city says each shop’s sauce and method differ.
