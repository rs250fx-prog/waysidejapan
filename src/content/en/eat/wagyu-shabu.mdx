Shabu-shabu is one of the plainest ways to eat wagyu: a slice of marbled beef so thin you can read through it, held in chopsticks and swished through a pot of simmering stock for three or four seconds, then dipped and eaten. The name is the sound of the swish. It is the dish the big all-you-can-eat chains have built on, because it needs no chef at the table and the beef cooks itself, and it is the cleanest way to taste what the marbling actually does.

## Where it came from

Honestly: this site could not confirm it from an official source. A founding shop and a date circulate in every guidebook, but the restaurant's own account and a public record were not available for this edition, and the rule here is not to repeat a date without one. What can be said is that the dish is a postwar restaurant form, that the name is onomatopoeic, and that the brand houses — Kisoji among them — have served it alongside sukiyaki for decades.

## What makes it shabu-shabu

**The meat.** Beef sliced thinner than for sukiyaki — thin enough to cook in seconds. Pork is the chains' everyday version and is the cheapest course on every menu.

**The pot.** Plain stock — kelp, often — kept at a simmer, sometimes split into two flavours in a divided pot at the chains. Vegetables, tofu and mushrooms go in and stay; the meat goes in and comes out.

**The sauces.** *Ponzu* (citrus and soy) and *goma-dare* (sesame), one of each per person, with grated daikon and spring onion to add. This is where the flavour is; the pot is just hot water with manners.

**The finish.** Noodles or rice in the broth, which by then tastes of everything that passed through it.

## Why the chains love it

Because it is self-service wagyu. Sukiyaki at a house is cooked for you; shabu-shabu at a chain is a pot, a timer and a tray of meat, and the customer does the rest. Shabuyo (Skylark) and Onyasai (Reins) run hundreds of shops on this model, and Teikoku Databank's analysis of the restaurant market credits the all-you-can-eat genres with most of the sector's recent growth. The law treats it as seriously as a steakhouse: shabu-shabu restaurants must display the ten-digit traceability number of their beef.

- **Shabuyo**: all-you-can-eat pork at weekday lunch (no time limit) and weekday dinner (100 minutes), with beef courses above; central shops charge a little more.
- **Onyasai**: all-you-can-eat beef courses, a lunch course, and a pork set that is not a buffet.
- **Kisoji**: a set of 90 g or 120 g of domestic sirloin; central Tokyo shops charge more.

Shabuyo's official menu gives prices before tax, and the other menus do not say whether tax is included, so prices are not repeated in these notes.

## How to eat it

1. Put the vegetables in first and let them cook; they stay.
2. Hold a slice of meat in your chopsticks, swish it in the stock until it just turns from red to pink — three or four seconds — and lift it out. Do not drop it in and hunt for it.
3. Dip in ponzu or sesame. Add daikon or onion to the dip, not to the pot.
4. Skim the foam that gathers; the chains give you a ladle for it.
5. Finish with the noodles in the broth. At a timed course, order the finish before last orders.

## Shabu-shabu away from home

The chains have made it national and uniform; what changes is the stock (kelp in Kansai, lighter still in the chains) and the dip, with sesame sauce more prominent in the east. Abroad, "shabu-shabu" is often a hotpot with a spiced broth that is doing the flavouring — a different idea, closer to Chinese hotpot. The Japanese version's point is that the broth does nothing and the beef does everything, which is why the quality of the slice is the whole dish.

## What people get wrong

- **"It is boiled beef."** It is swished for three seconds; boiled beef is what happens when you leave it in.
- **"The broth is the flavour."** The dip is. The broth is a cooking medium until the noodles go in.
- **"It is the cheap option."** At the chains, yes, and the cheapest course there is pork.
