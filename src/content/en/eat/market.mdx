A Japanese fish market is two things at once, and visitors usually only meet one of them. The first is a piece of public infrastructure where fish from all over the country is auctioned before dawn to licensed traders, and where you, as a member of the public, cannot buy anything. The second is the ring of restaurants, stalls and shops that grows up around it or in place of it, where the seafood bowl (*kaisendon*) lives. This page explains the first so that the second makes sense, and is honest about what it cannot tell you: what a bowl should cost.

## What a wholesale market is

The farm ministry describes a wholesale market as the hub where fruit and vegetables, fish, meat and flowers from all over Japan are gathered and sold on to retailers, restaurants and processors. It is established with the approval of the national or prefectural government; buyers and sellers must be qualified; trading follows set methods; and the day's results are published. The ministry lists four functions: gathering and dividing up the goods, setting prices, settling payment, and publishing information. The national law governing these markets was revised in June 2018.

**The chain.** Shippers send to a *wholesaler*, who sells to *intermediate wholesalers* (*nakaoroshi*) and other licensed buyers, who sell to shops, restaurants and processors, who sell to you. In Tokyo, both wholesalers and intermediate wholesalers need a licence from the governor. Tokyo's own description of the fish side: the wholesaler lays out the catch and runs the auction; buyers bid with their fingers; the highest bid wins; the intermediate wholesaler then sells from its own stall to fishmongers and sushi shops, who sell to consumers.

**Two ways to set a price.** In an *auction* (*seri*), one seller faces many buyers and the goods go to the highest bidder. The ministry says it is used for expensive goods and goods of uneven quality — tuna, melons, beef. In *negotiated trading* (*aitai*), one seller and one buyer agree a price between them.

**The hours.** The ministry's example: negotiated trading from about 2 a.m., auctions from about 6 a.m., goods out to customers by 7. Tokyo's page on fish puts the auction start at about 5 a.m. Markets differ.

The ministry's figure, from the end of fiscal 2017, was 64 central wholesale markets in 40 cities, 34 of them handling fish.

## Why visitors cannot just walk in and buy

Because the people inside are trading for businesses. The ministry says plainly that at most wholesale markets the general public cannot shop. Some markets hold open days, perhaps monthly, or an annual market festival, when the public can buy inside and try a mock auction.

Tokyo's Toyosu market is built for visitors in a particular way: a gallery route above the trading floors, and a restaurant and shopping area inside, with set hours, and closed on Sundays, holidays and market holidays. The wholesale halls themselves sell only to large buyers.

## Inside, outside, and town markets

Three different things are all called "market" in English.

- **Inside the wholesale market** (*jōnai*). The licensed market itself. At Toyosu, visitors reach the restaurants and shops along the gallery route, on market days only.
- **Outside it** (*jōgai*). The commercial area next to a wholesale market. At Toyosu that is Senkyaku Banrai, which Tokyo describes as built on the outer area next to the market, and which says it opens every day, including market holidays. At Tsukiji, the outer market is a set of shopping streets run by a local merchants' association; its own site says many shops close on Tokyo's market holidays.
- **Town markets.** Kanazawa's Ōmichō describes itself as the city's everyday kitchen for about 300 years, with about 170 shops. Hakodate's morning market began in 1945 with farmers selling vegetables by the station, and by 1947 was an open-air market where producers sold to consumers. These are where most visitors eat a seafood bowl outside Tokyo. I could not confirm whether either is a wholesale market in the legal sense, and I have not found an official definition of *jōgai* or "town market" either, so these descriptions come from the markets themselves.

## The seafood bowl

Rice, usually in a bowl, topped with raw seafood. I have not found an official definition, or an account of its origin, so this page describes it from the menus.

**Bowls are named by count and by choice.** Kanazawa's Kaisendon-ya Hirai offers about 20 bowls, including a mini bowl and bowls with tempura or eel, and lists local-sounding names such as the "Noto Kiwami" and "Kanazawa Zanmai". In Hakodate's Donburi Yokochō, shops name their bowls by how many toppings and who chooses: "three-colour, choose three toppings", "ten colours", "seven-colour morning market bowl", "chef's choice", "all salmon".

**"Local" and "seasonal" are the shop's claim.** Ōmichō's Kaisendon Ichiba describes a seasonal chef's-choice bowl and bowls with Hokuriku fish such as *nodoguro* (blackthroat seaperch) and white shrimp, bought daily at the market. Ikiikitei says its fish comes straight from Kanazawa port. These are the shops' own words.

**Some bowls have no price.** Hirai's menu lists a sea urchin and salmon roe bowl at *jika*, market price, alongside priced bowls; a Donburi Yokochō restaurant lists live squid sashimi the same way. My reading — not an official one — is that items whose wholesale cost moves with the day's auction are hard to print at a fixed price. Whatever the reason, the practical step is the same: ask before you order.

## What it costs

This page does not give a price for a seafood bowl, and that is deliberate. The market restaurants checked for it — in Ōmichō and Hakodate — post prices in yen but do not say whether tax is included, and Hakodate's federation, which states that its featured prices include tax, had its menu file offline. In Tokyo, neither the market nor the complex next door published a tax-included bowl price. This site only gives tax-included prices it can verify, so the honest answer for now is: the shops post their menus, and you can read the price at the door.

## Where to go

- **Toyosu, Tokyo** — the visitor route from 05:00 to 17:00 on market days; Senkyaku Banrai next door every day.
- **Tsukiji, Tokyo** — the outer market's shopping streets; check the market calendar, because many shops follow it.
- **Ōmichō, Kanazawa** — near Musashi-ga-tsuji in the city centre; hours are set by each shop.
- **Hakodate morning market** — by Hakodate station, open all year, from 05:00 (May–December) or 06:00 (January–April) until a little after 14:00.

Fares and travel times are not on these pages yet.

## The same name, a different market

What changes between a market in a fishing town and a market in Tokyo, on the facts available:

- **What the market is for.** Toyosu is where fish from all over Japan is priced by auction, and visitors are guests on a gallery. Hakodate's morning market began as farmers selling direct, and Ōmichō calls itself the city's kitchen. The same English word covers both.
- **When you can go.** Toyosu's inner market is closed on Sundays, holidays and market holidays; the complex next door is not. Hakodate's morning market trades all year from early morning. Ōmichō's bowl shops keep their own, mostly daytime, hours.
- **Who claims the fish is local.** Kanazawa shops advertise Hokuriku fish and fish from Kanazawa port. Tokyo says Senkyaku Banrai's restaurants cook with fish chosen by the market's intermediate wholesalers. Neither claim is a measure of quality, and no official source compares them.
- **The price comparison is missing.** I could not get tax-included prices in either place, so I cannot tell you whether a bowl in Hakodate is cheaper than one in Tokyo.

## What people get wrong

- **"You can buy fish at the fish market."** Not inside a wholesale market, as a rule. You can at Tsukiji's outer streets, in the complex next to Toyosu, and at town markets like Ōmichō and Hakodate.
- **"Tsukiji is still the fish market."** The wholesale market moved to Toyosu in October 2018. Tsukiji's outer market is a separate shopping area.
- **"The market is open every day."** Toyosu's inner market closes on Sundays, holidays and market holidays, and Tokyo tells visitors to check its market calendar.
- **"Market price means rip-off."** It means the price is not fixed on the menu. It is a reason to ask, not a reason to assume.
- **"A seafood bowl has a standard size."** Shops name and price bowls by how many toppings, who chooses them, and which fish — from a mini bowl to ten kinds.
