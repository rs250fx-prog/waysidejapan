The most quoted fact about Japanese stock is that the west uses kelp and the east uses bonito. As a tendency it holds: the National Diet Library puts it in those terms, and a bowl of udon in Osaka is pale where Tokyo's is dark. But the reasons usually given are less certain than they sound, and household spending today does not split neatly along the line. This page sets out the tendency, the two explanations and how far the evidence for each goes.

For dashi in general — umami, the ingredients, vegetarian questions — start from the dashi overview.

## Where it came from

The National Diet Library's essay on dashi says Kantō has tended to use mainly katsuobushi and Kansai mainly kombu, and that two reasons are mainly put forward.

**1. Trade.** Kombu came from Hokkaido by the sea route. In the late 17th century the Edo merchant Kawamura Zuiken established the western route from Sakata round Shimonoseki to Osaka and Edo, and Hokkaido kombu reached Kyoto and Osaka, and then Edo. The best kombu shipped to Osaka sold there first, the library says, and what was left went on to Edo, so kombu stock did not develop in Kantō. This "kombu road", built up from the mid-Edo period to the end of the shogunate, reached as far as Kyushu and the Ryūkyūs. The farm ministry gives the earlier stage: in the Muromachi period kombu went from Hokkaido through Tsuruga to Kyoto and Osaka, and from Edo times by *kitamae* ship round the west. Its page on Osaka's kitsune udon says that as the *kitamae* route developed, kombu, flour and salt gathered in Osaka.

**2. Water.** The library's second reason: Kansai's soft water draws out kombu stock easily, Kantō's harder water does not, so Kantō cooks chose bonito for a strong stock. The farm ministry's booklet says, of Japan as a whole, that soft water draws out the flavour of kombu and katsuobushi effectively.

The library concludes that each region's basic taste is thought to have been set in the Edo period, when bulk shipping began.

## How strong is the water explanation?

Weaker than it is usually told. A study presented to the Japan Society of Cookery Science in 2004 extracted Hidaka kombu in tap water and in very soft, soft and hard water, and found that tasters rated the umami differently and the glutamate in the stock varied with the water. But its abstract does not show that harder water gives *less* glutamate, and it does not claim to explain the east–west split. The fair statement is that water may affect how kombu's umami comes out; that it decided the regional difference is an explanation, not a finding.

## What makes each side's stock

**Kansai.** The farm ministry's recipe for Osaka's kitsune udon: ma-kombu with mackerel and other fish flakes, or dried sardines; soak the kombu 40 minutes, bring to the boil, add the flakes for two or three minutes, season with light soy. The dish itself is said to have begun in 1893 at Matsubaya in Senba, when a customer put the side of fried tofu on his plain udon. Osaka's tourism bureau names Hokkaido kombu and Tosa katsuobushi as the base of Osaka stock, and adds that cheap dried anchovies from Osaka Bay and the Inland Sea supported the city's dashi culture. The Umami Information Center says ma-kombu is used mainly in Kansai around Osaka, and that stock from *arabushi* (bonito before mould-curing) is also liked in Kansai.

**Kyoto.** A Kyoto restaurateur interviewed by the city's tourism site in 2021 explains it as a chain: Kyoto's water is soft, so kombu is the lead and bonito is only for aroma, seasoned with salt or light soy — so the broth stays pale. Kombu, he says, is best drawn at about 60°C for an hour, and varieties differ.

**Kantō.** On the same chain: in hard water kombu gives less, so more bonito is used; more bonito leaves a fishy note, so a fragrant soy is used — and the broth darkens. That is a restaurateur's explanation. I could not find an official Tokyo recipe for soba or udon broth, so I give no proportions. The two main katsuobushi producers are Kagoshima and Shizuoka, not Tokyo.

**The soy.** The farm ministry says dark *koikuchi* soy is about 85% of production and light *usukuchi* about 11%; light soy is used mainly in Kansai and keeps the colour of ingredients, while dark soy has a stronger aroma said to cover fishiness. Kikkoman's account adds that Kantō became the great dark-soy region from the mid-Edo period (Noda, Chōshi), and that soba, tempura, eel and nigiri sushi are said to have grown up with it. I found no official source saying dark soy was chosen to make up for weak stock, so I do not say so.

## What household spending shows

The government household survey (2023–25 average) records what households spend each year on kombu and on katsuobushi. The file gives no unit beyond "amount"; the figures are presumably yen per household.

- **Kombu**, national 727: Toyama 1,587, Fukui 1,285, Aomori 1,174, Yamagata 1,112, Sakai 1,044, Kyoto 1,034. Central Tokyo 765, Osaka 761.
- **Katsuobushi and flakes**, national 859: Naha 2,033, Kōchi 1,346, Hamamatsu 1,120, Gifu 1,107, Shizuoka 1,061. Central Tokyo 913, Osaka 848.

So the top kombu buyers are on the old *kitamae* coast and in Tōhoku, with Kyoto and Sakai high too; the top bonito buyers include producing areas. Tokyo and Osaka spend almost the same on kombu, and Tokyo only a little more on bonito. These are home purchases, not restaurant broths, and the survey gives no reasons, so I draw no causes from them — only that the east–west line is not visible in what households in the two big cities buy.

## Kansai stock away from Kansai

- **The ingredients come from elsewhere on both sides.** Over 90% of kombu is from Hokkaido; most katsuobushi from Kagoshima and Shizuoka. Osaka's stock was built on shipped kombu, Tokyo's on shipped bonito.
- **The difference is a style, not freshness.** The library treats the split as regional taste fixed in the Edo period.
- **I could not compare broths.** No official source compares a Kansai-style udon broth in Tokyo with one in Osaka, so this page makes no claim either way.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kantō doesn't use kombu."** It is a tendency, and the classic first dashi uses both.
- **"Hard water proves it."** It is one of two explanations; the study cited here does not show the direction.
- **"Tokyo buys bonito, Osaka buys kombu."** In the household survey they spend almost the same on each.
- **"Dark soy is there to hide weak stock."** No official source says so.
