Sukiyaki is the dish that taught Japan to eat beef, and the one the country still reaches for on the last night of the year. Thin slices of marbled beef, cooked at the table in a shallow iron pan with sugar and soy sauce, lifted out and dipped in raw egg — it is sweet, rich and communal, and it has been argued about along the Tokyo–Osaka line for a hundred and fifty years.

## Where it came from

Beef hotpot — *gyūnabe* — arrived with the foreigners and the new government. By the account of the Tokyo house Imahan, a beef-hotpot shop opened in Tokyo in 1867, the year the government licensed a slaughterhouse; in Yokohama, Ōta Nawanoren dates its founding to 1868, when a street-food seller named Takahashi Otokichi, inspired by wild-boar hotpot, cut beef into chunks, stewed it in a shallow iron pan with a miso sauce and leeks against the smell, and sold it to the port. On 24 January 1872 the Meiji emperor's eating of beef was announced to the country — the "lifting of the ban" on meat, pushed, the historian Ishi Hiroyuki writes, by the need to serve beef at banquets with Western powers. Ten monks broke into the palace in protest. By 1877 Tokyo had more than 550 beef-hotpot shops. The GI file for Kobe beef notes the next step: the Taishō period, when *sukiyaki* reached the family table.

The word is older than the dish. Imahan gives three theories — a farmer's "plough-grill" (*suki-yaki*, cooking on a spade blade), "thin-sliced grill" (*sukimi*), and the aesthetic "*suki*" of tea-ceremony taste — and does not choose.

## What makes it sukiyaki

**The pan.** Shallow, cast iron, at the table.

**The beef.** Thin-sliced marbled wagyu; the brands made their names here, Matsusaka above all. Asakusa's Chinya, founded 1880, has gone the other way and set its own standard of "appropriately marbled" beef — modest marbling, grade 4 fat, 30-month heifers, a month's ageing — against the A5 arms race.

**The seasoning, and the argument.** Kanto makes *warishita*, a prepared broth of soy, sugar, mirin and stock, and simmers meat and vegetables in it; Imahan says it manages the sugar content and temperature of its broth to match the beef. Kansai sears the beef in the hot pan first with a little fat, scatters sugar and pours soy directly over it, eats the first slices, then adds vegetables and water. Kikkoman's 2024 household survey found broth-makers are about a third of homes everywhere except Kinki and Shikoku, and bottled sukiyaki sauce is commoner the further east you go. Osaka's Kitamura, founded 1881, is the tourism board's example of the broth-less Kansai method. But Kyoto's Mishimatei, founded 1873 by a couple who learned beef hotpot in Nagasaki, says plainly that it uses a *warishita* refined by its fifth generation — so "Kansai never uses broth" is a rule with its most famous exception in Kyoto.

**The egg.** A raw egg, beaten in a small bowl, as the dip. It is not optional at any house.

**The occasion.** Kikkoman's survey puts about 55% of household sukiyaki at New Year's Eve, the year end and the first days of January. It is the national celebration dinner, which is why the butchers' sukiyaki-cut gift boxes exist.

## Why Yokohama and Tokyo

The treaty port had the foreigners who wanted beef and the slaughterhouse to supply them; the capital had the government that needed its citizens to stop regarding beef as unclean. Hotpot was the form because it was already how Japan cooked boar and venison, and because miso and leeks hid a smell the country was not used to. The Kansai method, searing in sugar and soy, is the merchant cities' refinement once the smell no longer needed hiding.

- **Ōta Nawanoren**, Yokohama, since 1868: chunk-cut beef hotpot ¥8,200 (for two or more), sukiyaki ¥8,200, courses ¥14,300–17,600, plus 10% service.
- **Ningyōchō Imahan**, Tokyo, since 1895: sukiyaki alone ¥9,900 (superior) / ¥15,290 (supreme); set ¥13,530 / ¥18,920; courses ¥16,060 / ¥21,450; plus 12% service.
- **Mishimatei**, Kyoto, since 1873: lunch course ¥8,591, Tsuki course ¥17,545, Hana ¥24,200, Gokujō Hana ¥28,435; a sukiyaki set meal at its Takashimaya Kyoto branch is ¥3,300.
- **Chinya**, Asakusa, since 1880, reopened 2022 after a year's closure: restaurant prices not published; its sukiyaki gift cuts are ¥6,000–12,000 per 400 g.

The gap between ¥1,430 and ¥21,450 is the beef and the room. The method is the same.

## How to eat it

1. At a house, the staff cook the first round; watch which method they use and copy it for the second.
2. Beat the egg. Lift a slice straight from the pan into it, then eat; the egg stops the sugar and salt from being too much.
3. Vegetables — leek, tofu, shirataki, mushrooms, chrysanthemum leaves — go in after the first beef in Kansai, with it in Kanto.
4. Finish with udon or rice in the pan.
5. Service charges of 10–12% at the houses are stated, not tipped.

## Sukiyaki away from home

The east–west split is the thing that travels badly. A Tokyo house serving Kansai style, or an Osaka house serving broth, exists but is not the default, and a visitor who has eaten sukiyaki in one city and orders it in the other should expect a different procedure and a different sweetness. The bottled sauce — Kikkoman's survey shows it dominating eastern homes — is a Kanto *warishita* in a bottle, so the home version of the dish is drifting east nationally even as the Kansai houses keep searing. Abroad, "sukiyaki" on a menu is almost always the simmered Kanto form, often with the egg left out; the egg is the part foreign kitchens drop first and the part a Japanese table would miss most.

## Getting there

Ōta Nawanoren is in Yokohama's Isezakichō, a short walk from Kannai station; Imahan in Ningyōchō (Hibiya and Asakusa lines); Chinya by Kaminarimon in Asakusa; Mishimatei at Teramachi-Sanjō in central Kyoto.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sukiyaki is ancient."** 1867–68, with the slaughterhouse licence and the emperor's dinner.
- **"Kansai never uses broth."** Kyoto's oldest house does.
- **"The egg is a garnish."** It is the sauce.
- **"It is a special-occasion dish only."** At home, yes — half of it is eaten at New Year. At the chains it is a ¥1,430 lunch.
