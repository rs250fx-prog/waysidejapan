Kyoto is a capital without a coast, and its sushi is a solution to that. Saba-zushi — a whole cured mackerel fillet laid over a long bar of vinegared rice, pressed, wrapped in translucent kelp and sliced thick — is the sushi of the city's three great festivals, and it exists because of a road. Mackerel landed at Obama on the Sea of Japan, salted on the quay and carried over the mountains, reached the capital two or three days later with the salt, the ministry says, "at just the right strength". Kyoto did not invent a way to eat fresh fish. It made a dish out of the delay.

## Where it came from

The agriculture ministry's entry dates *saba-zushi* to the Edo period and places it in the festival calendar: the Aoi festival in May, Gion in July, Jidai in October, and the autumn festivals of the villages, when households made or bought it for guests. The mackerel came along the Saba Kaidō, the "mackerel road" from Wakasa, a day's walk north; by the time a salted fish arrived it had cured itself. The city's great house of the dish is Izuu, founded in the first year of Tenmei (1781) by a man named Izumiya Uhei, who — by the shop's own history — took a commoners' food and refined it with a cook's technique into *saba-sugata-zushi*, the whole-fish form, sold to the teahouses of Gion. It added seats for eating in only around 1970; for two centuries it was a shop you carried from.

The form belongs to the Kansai family. The National Diet Library's history records that when Edo went over to hand-pressed nigiri around the 1820s, Kyoto and Osaka kept pressed sushi; Osaka's *battera*, mackerel pressed in a box, is this dish's city cousin.

## What makes it saba-zushi

**The fish.** A whole mackerel fillet, salted and then vinegared, laid skin-up along the rice so the slice shows a silver stripe.

**The rice.** Vinegared, pressed into a bar under the fish, often with a little ginger inside.

**The kelp.** A sheet of white kelp (*shiraita kombu*) wrapped round the whole log, keeping it moist and adding a faint sweetness; it is peeled off or eaten as you like.

**The cut.** Thick slices, three or four to a serving. It is a substantial sushi, closer to a meal than a canapé.

## Why Kyoto

The mackerel road and the festival calendar. A capital of courtiers and temples needed celebration food that could be made ahead and carried to a viewing stand, and salted fish from the north coast arrived in exactly the condition to make it. The dish is the city's geography on a plate: sea fish, cured by the journey, finished by a court cook.

## The shops, and what they charge

Saba-zushi is sold by the log or the slice. Izuu, in Gion, does not publish prices in a form this site could verify; the department-store food halls at Kyoto station carry it from several makers at bento-and-up prices. Kyoto households rank 36th of 52 cities for noodles eaten out and above average for sushi, and saba-zushi is a large part of why: it is bought, not cooked.

## How to eat it

A thick slice, with the fingers or chopsticks, plain. A touch of soy is permitted by the ministry and frowned on by purists. It is best a few hours after it is made and good the next day; the kelp can be eaten or set aside.

## Saba-zushi away from Kyoto

The whole-fillet form rarely leaves the city except in a box from a department store; what travels is the family resemblance. Osaka's *battera* is the same cure in a rectangular mould, named after a Portuguese boat in 1891. Nara's *kakinoha-zushi* is a single slice of the same salted mackerel on a mouthful of rice in a persimmon leaf. Fukui, at the other end of the mackerel road, has its own pressed mackerel. Across Kansai the mackerel is the constant and the shape is local. In Tokyo, where "mackerel sushi" means a piece of *shime-saba* nigiri, the log is an exotic.

## Getting there

Izuu is in Gion, a few minutes' walk from Gion-Shijō station on the Keihan line; the Kyoto station food halls are the practical alternative. Obama, where the road began, is two hours north by bus or rail and has a museum of the mackerel road.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kyoto has no sushi culture."** It has a pressed-sushi culture older than nigiri, built for festivals.
- **"Mackerel sushi is a nigiri."** In Tokyo. In Kyoto it is a log of rice under a whole fish.
- **"It needs soy sauce."** The journey from Wakasa seasoned it.
