*Wagashi* — Japanese confectionery — runs from a bar of bean paste that keeps for a year to a fresh sweet shaped and named for a season and replaced after a short run. What holds them together is a short list of ingredients (rice, beans, sugar, starch) and two influences: tea, which gave them a reason to exist, and the seasons, which give them their shapes. This page is the map. The lineage pages cover *yōkan*, the bean-paste bar, and the griddle cake whose name changes as you cross Japan.

## What it is

**The origin of the word.** Toraya, the Kyoto-born confectioner, notes that *kashi* originally meant nuts and fruit — fruit is still called *mizugashi*, "water sweets" — and that rice cakes and dumplings were the first Japanese sweets.

**Three foreign waves.** Toraya names them: Chinese confections brought by envoys to Tang China (Asuka to Heian); *tenshin*, light dishes brought by Zen monks in the Kamakura and Muromachi periods, which were the forerunners of manjū and yōkan; and Portuguese sweets in the late Muromachi to early Edo, such as castella and konpeitō.

**Tea.** The Japan Wagashi Association says that after the monk Eisai brought tea in the early Kamakura period, tea drinking spread and the tea ceremony became fashionable. Tea gatherings in the Muromachi period included *tenshin*, among them a soup (*kan*); sweets for tea included chestnuts, rice crackers, chestnut-flour cakes and *funoyaki*, and this is said to have driven wagashi's development. Toraya says the *jōgashi* that spread around Kyoto in the Genroku era (1688–1704) are regarded as the origin of today's fine fresh sweets, *jōnamagashi*.

**How they are classed.** The association gives the usual division as fresh (*namagashi*), semi-dry (*hannamagashi*) and dry (*higashi*) — by water content. It then says this is scientifically right but does not match reality well: the same poured yōkan is semi-dry if worked firm and fresh if made soft, and *gyūhi* behaves the same way. The finer categories mix ingredients (rice-cake, bean-paste), methods (steamed, poured) and equipment (griddle, oven), which the association admits is confusing.

## How the seasons get in

The association describes two kinds of seasonal sweet:

1. **Made only in their season** — *hanabira-mochi* at New Year, *kusa-mochi*, *uguisu-mochi*, *sakura-mochi*, *kashiwa-mochi*, *mizu-yōkan*, chestnut sweets.
2. **Seasonal by shape, colour and name** — *kinton*, *nerikiri*, *konashi*. These are bean paste mixed with yam and the like; the taste stays the same all year, and the point is that the shape and colour tell you the season. They change every short while: around New Year a white and leaf-green "Mebuki" (budding); in November "Hatsushimo" (first frost), brown dusted with white sugar.

**Summer.** Kuzu sweets look translucent and cool, but kuzu hardens when chilled, and the association says it is best eaten at room temperature — coolness by appearance, not by the fridge.

**Wagashi Day, 16 June.** The association says that in 848 the emperor Ninmyō, following an oracle, offered 16 sweets and rice cakes to the gods on 16 June to pray against plague, and renamed the era Kashō. The custom continued through the Edo shogunate's *kashō itadaki* until the Meiji era; Wagashi Day revives it.

## Keeping them

- **Not the fridge.** The association says wagashi are mostly starch and do not like refrigeration.
- **The freezer, yes.** Mochi, daifuku, monaka, castella, dorayaki and fresh sweets freeze well for a while. Freeze in single portions soon after you get them; thaw at room temperature for two or three hours; eat promptly; do not refreeze.
- **Labels.** Osaka prefecture explains that foods that spoil quickly carry a "use by" date (*shōhi kigen*) and others a "best before" (*shōmi kigen*). Check which your box has.
- **Yōkan is the exception.** Toraya gives its yōkan a year from manufacture. See the yōkan page.

## How to eat them

The association's suggestions:

- **Daifuku by hand.** A sweets pick does not cut it well. A damp towel alongside is good hospitality.
- **With tea**, naturally — but sweets with red bean paste also suit coffee or black tea.
- **Yōkan with spirits**, cut into roughly 1 cm cubes.
- **Kuzu at room temperature.**

## Where to buy them

Toraya separates its shops: 10 company-run shops (Akasaka, Kyoto Ichijō, Ginza, Nihonbashi and others, plus Paris) that handle fresh sweets, and 71 counters in shopping centres and airports, eight of them at airports. It notes that products differ by shop. So the long-keeping sweets reach airports; the fresh ones are mainly at the company's own shops.

## The same sweet, a different place

- **What you can buy depends on where you are.** Toraya sells Kyoto-only yōkan (white miso, black soybean flour) and an airport-only one.
- **Long keeping made sweets travel.** Yōkan's shelf life is why Ogi, in Saga, could sell to the army and to distant cities.
- **The name changes.** The bean-filled griddle cake has a different name in Kantō, Chūgoku–Shikoku and Kyushu. See its page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Wagashi should be kept cold."** Most should not; freeze them instead.
- **"Seasonal sweets taste of the season."** The seasonal *nerikiri* taste the same all year; the shape and name change.
- **"Fresh and dry are clear categories."** The association itself says the classification does not match reality.
- **"Wagashi are purely Japanese."** Toraya traces three foreign waves.
