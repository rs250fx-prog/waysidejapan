Instant noodles are the Japanese meal most visitors eat without sitting down in a restaurant: a cup from a convenience store, some hot water, three minutes. They are also a Japanese invention, by the account of the company that made the first ones and of the industry's own association, and Japan still makes about 5.9 billion servings a year. This page is the map: what the word covers, how the hot water and the lid are meant to work, what a cup costs, and where the empty goes. The two lineage pages go deep on the [packet](/en/eat/instant-noodles-bag/) and the [cup](/en/eat/instant-noodles-cup/).

## What it is

Japan has a legal definition. The national JAS standard for instant noodles (the version I read is the 2016 revision; I have not checked later amendments) describes noodles made mainly from wheat or buckwheat flour, kneaded with salt or *kansui* (the alkaline water of ramen) or similar, with a seasoning packed in or already on the noodle, ready to eat after a simple preparation. Frozen and chilled noodles are excluded. The standard dates from 1965.

The industry's association in Japan (日本即席食品工業協会, known as JIFFA) sorts them several ways:

- **By package:** packet (*fukuro-men*) or cup (*kappu-men*).
- **By noodle:** Chinese-style, Japanese-style (udon, soba) and Western-style.
- **By how the noodle is dried:** fried, non-fried, or "fresh type".
- **By how it is eaten:** in soup, or as yakisoba and dipping noodles.

The drying is the heart of it. A **fried** noodle goes into oil at 140–160°C for one to two minutes, in a metal frame, and its water content drops from 30–40% to 3–6%. A **non-fried** noodle is dried with hot air at around 80°C for 30 minutes or more. JIFFA gives the fat as about 20 g per 100 g for fried noodles and 4–6 g for non-fried. The **fresh type** is steamed or boiled, treated in an acid solution and heat-sterilised; JIFFA says this was hard to do with alkaline ramen noodles until Nissin's Ra-Oh solved it in 1992.

Why do Japanese makers say "instant ramen" as well as "instant noodles"? JIFFA's answer: the first product was called Chicken Ramen, and Chinese-style noodles are by far the largest share.

## The numbers

JIFFA's figures for fiscal 2025:

- **Production in Japan:** 5,905.06 million servings — 3,943.86 million cups and 1,961.19 million packets. Cups are 66.8%.
- **Per person:** 47.9 servings a year, counting a population of 123.21 million.
- **Flour used:** about 300,000 tonnes.
- **Exports:** about 102.89 million servings (counted at 100 g each) to 66 countries and regions.
- **JAS-certified products** are 77.3% of the total; 1,476 JAS brands were announced, 1,233 cups and 253 packets.

The World Instant Noodles Association (WINA) puts world demand in 2025 at 124,209 million servings, up from 118,014 million in 2021. Japan is fifth, at 5,952 million, behind China and Hong Kong (43,268 million), Indonesia, India and Vietnam. Per head, WINA ranks Vietnam first (about 81 servings), then South Korea (about 79) and Thailand (about 60). The two Japanese figures are not the same thing: JIFFA counts production in a fiscal year, WINA counts demand. On my own arithmetic, not an official figure, Japan is about 4.8% of world demand.

The government household survey (households of two or more, 2023–25 average) adds a domestic angle. A household spends ¥5,833 a year on cup noodles and ¥2,412 on the survey's item "instant noodles" — probably packets, though I have not found the survey's definition. The cities that spend most on cups are Aomori (¥8,326), Fukushima, Niigata, Akita and Sendai; Kyoto (¥4,484) is near the bottom.

## How it works

**The water must boil.** Nissin's FAQ says "hot water" on its packages means water that has boiled; water kept warm in a thermos pot (set to 95°C or below) does not rehydrate the noodles properly, and water straight off the boil is best.

**The lid is part of the method.** JIFFA's example of cooking instructions on a cup: peel the lid back halfway, pour boiling water to the line inside the cup, close the lid and wait three minutes, then peel the lid off and stir well.

**The amount and the time change by product.** From the makers' own pages: Cup Noodle 280 ml, Donbei udon 350 ml, Chicken Ramen 400 ml, a small "eat from a bowl" packet 160 ml. Chicken Ramen takes three minutes with poured water or one minute simmered. Nissin also sells a Cup Noodle made with 330 ml of *cold* water, ready in five minutes. The package is the instruction, which is how JIFFA's labelling rules are designed.

**No microwave, no extra oil.** JIFFA recommends three warning marks on packages: "beware of burns", "do not microwave", and "beware of absorbed smells". It says a cup heated in a microwave can deform or spark; Nissin adds that its cup lids use aluminium. Both warn against adding your own oil — perilla, *egoma* or coconut oil, for example — to a foamed polystyrene cup, which can deform and leak hot water from the bottom. The store microwave rules are on the [konbini page](/en/eat/conbini/).

**Keeping it.** JIFFA gives a rough shelf life of about six months for cups and eight for packets, and says the date on the pack is the result of storage tests multiplied by a safety factor of 0.8 or more but less than 1. Store it out of sunlight, at room temperature, somewhere dry, and away from strong smells.

**Allergens.** Wheat is the main ingredient, and many products add egg, milk, shrimp or pork in the soup or toppings. Since 1 April 2026, when cashew nuts were added, the Consumer Affairs Agency requires nine allergens to be labelled and recommends twenty more. Nissin's Cup Noodle page, when I checked, still described "8 required and 20 recommended, 28 in total" while its allergen table already listed cashew nuts; I have not confirmed how the transition is being handled. The package is the authority, and Nissin itself says its website may differ from it. The wider picture is in [food allergies in Japan](/en/column/eating-in-japan-allergies/).

## What it costs

Makers publish a suggested retail price before tax — Nissin's is ¥248 for Cup Noodle — and each shop sets its own selling price, so I do not use makers' prices. Two chains publish tax-included prices (checked 13 October 2026, at the 8% takeaway rate):

- **Seven-Eleven:** Cup Noodle ¥267.84 (¥248 before tax), Cup Noodle Big ¥321.84, Mini Cup Noodle ¥168.48, all sold nationwide. The site shows decimals; I have not confirmed how the till rounds them.
- **Lawson:** its own-brand cup noodles run from ¥168 to ¥348 including tax, in standard and premium lines, some made with well-known ramen shops. It notes that Natural Lawson stores do not carry some of them.

Eating in changes the tax rate; the rule is explained on the [konbini page](/en/eat/conbini/). I have not found the tax agency's ruling on the specific case of buying a cup and using the store's hot water.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **The packet came first.** Chicken Ramen, August 1958, ¥35 when a ball of udon cost ¥6. The [ramen page](/en/eat/ramen/) tells how it put the word *rāmen* into general use.
- **The cup took over.** In 1989 cups overtook packets in production — 2,405 million to 2,225 million, by JIFFA's figures — and are now two thirds of the total.

## Where to find it, and the hot water

Supermarkets and convenience stores; Toyo Suisan describes its own sales route as "mass retailers, general retailers and others". I have not found an official source on drugstores.

The hot water is the question visitors ask. An article published on JNTO's site, written by a contributor, says convenience stores have a cup-noodle section and a hot-water dispenser where you can fill a cup on the spot, sometimes with a sign saying "please use freely", and that larger stores have tables and chairs. It does not say every store has one, and I could not open the chains' own pages on whether the water is free, how hot it is, or whether you must use the eat-in space.

The Cup Noodles Museums in Ikeda and Yokohama let you make your own; details are on the lineage pages.

## After you eat

Japanese bins are sorted by material, and a cup is several materials. Two cities' official guidance:

- **Osaka:** the outer film, the soup and topping sachets and the container go in plastic recycling; the paper lid goes in ordinary rubbish. Rinse with leftover washing-up water; anything that will not come clean goes in ordinary rubbish.
- **Yokohama:** rinse lightly or wipe; a paper cup-noodle container is burnable rubbish, not paper recycling.

JIFFA says cups are made either of foamed polystyrene or of paper coated with resin, and the recycling mark on the cup tells you which (PS for polystyrene, PP for polypropylene, or paper). Other cities may divide things differently; I have not checked Tokyo or Kyoto. I found no official advice on what to do with leftover soup.

## The same name, a different cup

Nissin says it does not export the Japanese taste as it is: Cup Noodle is made abroad to suit local preferences, with bases in Brazil, Singapore, Hong Kong, India, the Netherlands, Germany and Thailand. JNTO's profile counts more than 100 countries and over 150 flavours.

WINA's country notes describe tastes rather than products. Japan has pork-bone, chicken and fish stocks with soy, udon, soba and yakisoba, collaborations with famous shops, and low-calorie lines. In the United States chicken is the most common flavour, noodles are cut short to be eaten with spoon and fork because Americans do not slurp, and microwaveable products are popular. Indonesia favours fried *mi goreng*, mostly halal; India, masala and vegetarian lines. I found no official comparison of the same brand's ingredients or instructions in Japan and abroad, so do not assume a Cup Noodle at home is the one on a Tokyo shelf. On slurping itself, see [slurping noodles](/en/column/slurping-noodles/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Any hot water will do."** Nissin says boiling; thermos water below 95°C does not rehydrate properly.
- **"Microwave it if the water is not hot enough."** Not in the cup, unless the package says so.
- **"Instant noodles means ramen."** Kitsune udon, tempura soba and sauce yakisoba all come in cups.
- **"Japan eats the most."** It is fifth in WINA's ranking; per head, Vietnam and South Korea lead.
- **"A paper cup goes in paper recycling."** Not in Yokohama, which says burnable rubbish.
