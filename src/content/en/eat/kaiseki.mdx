"Kaiseki" is the word visitors meet on the most expensive menus in Japan, and it names two different meals. One is the meal served at a tea gathering. The other is a banquet built for drinking sake, which is what most restaurants mean today. Both sit in a family of set meal forms that came out of different settings: ritual, the tea room, the Zen temple, the drinking party, and food carried out of the house. This page is the map. The lineage pages cover tea kaiseki with the banquet, and shōjin ryōri, the temple food.

## What it is

Two words are read *kaiseki*:

- **懐石, the tea meal.** The meal of a *chaji*, a tea gathering. Rice and soup come first, and it ends with matcha. The farm ministry says that because people now often call any Japanese course meal "kaiseki", the tea version has come to be called *cha-kaiseki*, tea kaiseki, to tell it apart.
- **会席, the banquet.** The farm ministry's booklet *Washoku no Ryūgi* says that what is called Kaiseki in Japan today is a banquet meal eaten with sake, developed on the base of tea food but not the same as what is served at a tea gathering. Rice comes near the end.

A chef interviewed in the farm ministry's magazine in 2023 put the difference this way: both are food for hospitality, but tea kaiseki began as a light meal to fill the stomach before tea, with rice before the dishes, while the banquet is for enjoying sake, with rice after. He added that cooks are now trying to cross the line between the two.

## Why there are several forms

The farm ministry's booklet tells the history of Japanese food as a chain of settings:

- Home cooking, and in the Heian period the banquets of the court nobility.
- In the medieval period, **honzen ryōri**, warrior hospitality that grew by adding more trays, and **shōjin ryōri**, made in Zen monasteries, centred on vegetables and without animal protein.
- In the Momoyama period, the **kaiseki** of the tea ceremony was completed.
- In the Edo period, the restaurant **banquet kaiseki** — a meal for drinking, with many dishes laid out — developed from the warriors' honzen ryōri.

Kyoto city's education board (October 2019) says honzen ryōri was ceremonial food formed in the Muromachi period among warrior households, said to be the base of formal Japanese cooking; it ran from a first tray, the *honzen*, to a fifth.

The same farm ministry booklet gives two accounts of where the banquet came from: in one chapter it developed on the base of tea food, in another from warrior honzen ryōri. This site does not choose. Either way, these forms are not ranks of one meal. They came from different occasions, and the official sources describe them as related lines, not as better and worse.

## One soup and three dishes

The farm ministry's booklet says that until the early 20th century people ate from their own small tray, and *ichijū sansai* — one soup, three dishes — is what fitted on one. For guests, two or more trays were brought, and with two trays the meal became two soups and five dishes. So one soup and three dishes meant an everyday meal with one tray. The everyday side of that count is covered in the teishoku overview.

The same booklet also says tea kaiseki, which kept a single tray when two or three were the fashion, is one soup and three dishes, and the Omotesenke tea school gives the same base. It is the same count in two settings — the household tray and the tea-room tray — and I give both rather than choose.

## How the meal runs

The order of the tea meal is documented by the farm ministry and the Omotesenke school, and is set out on the tea kaiseki page.

For the banquet, I could not find an official source for the fixed sequence often printed in guides, with named courses from starter to fruit. The farm ministry's booklet says only that in a banquet the *hassun* became showier, the order of dishes can change with the cook's ideas, dishes arrive freshly made one after another, and rice comes near the end. Japan's national tourism organization (JNTO) says the basic elements are grilled fish, soup and rice, but are not limited to those; menus are usually set in advance, and diners choose the number of courses and leave the rest to the chef.

## Ryōtei, kappō and leaving it to the chef

- **Kappō.** The farm ministry's booklet explains the word: *katsu* is to cut, *pō* to apply heat, so it covers both raw and cooked preparation. The original *itamae kappō* is a counter restaurant where you order one dish at a time; if good sea bream has come in, you discuss with the cook whether to have it raw, simmered or grilled. Some kappō also have courses.
- **Ryōtei.** I found no official definition. Kyoto prefecture registered *Kyō-ryōri* (Kyoto cooking) as an intangible cultural property on 17 November 2022, and describes it as carried by three: the master, who oversees the menu, the room and the service; the cooks; and the proprietress and servers, who convey the meaning of the meal through their service.
- **Koryōriya**, the small neighbourhood restaurant, I could not find defined in any official source.
- **Omakase.** GO TOKYO, Tokyo's tourism site, writing about sushi, says *omakase* means leaving the choice to the chef; tell the chef about dislikes and allergies early. I could not find an official distinction between omakase and a set course.

## What it costs

I found tax-included prices on only one official site, and it is an eel restaurant with kaiseki courses, not a specialist kaiseki house. Atsuta Hōraiken in Nagoya states that all its prices include tax and that a 10% service charge is added. At its main restaurant, eel and seasonal kaiseki courses start at ¥12,100 at lunch and ¥16,500 in the evening; booking is required by three days before, and may not be possible in busy periods (checked 2026-10-11).

The mechanism to watch: some restaurants add a service charge to the tax-included price. Tenryūji's shōjin restaurant in Kyoto says it takes no service charge, but a garden entry fee is needed on top of the meal. I could not confirm tax-included prices for any Kyoto restaurant, so I give none.

## Booking

JNTO marks some of the kaiseki restaurants it lists as reservation recommended and some as required, and says to check each restaurant's official site for the latest. Kyoto's official tourism site recommends booking a shōjin restaurant ahead, and says places that cater for dietary restrictions may need to prepare. I could not find an official source on introduction-only restaurants, or on common rules for booking channels and cancellation; those vary by restaurant.

## Bento, briefly

The farm ministry's booklet traces the bento to portable food of Song-dynasty China, reaching Kyoto at the end of the 1300s. In the late 1500s came wooden boxes and *sagejū*, picnic sets with sake vessels and plates; at the end of the 1600s, boxes for watching kabuki; and in the mid-1800s, in Edo, the *makunouchi* bento sold for theatre-goers. Railway-station bento followed in the Meiji period. In 1937 a ryōtei created the four-compartment *Shōkadō* bento, after a story that the early Edo painter Shōkadō Shōjō used a farmer's seed box as a paint box.

The farm ministry's magazine says the makunouchi was made for ordinary audiences at the all-day Edo theatre, who ate in the intervals; the Edo-period *Morisada Mankō* lists rice balls, konnyaku, grilled tofu, simmered vegetables, kamaboko and rolled omelette — food that kept without refrigeration. The sources date it differently: "mid-1800s" in the booklet, "the Edo period" in the magazine. A Kyoto city handbook adds that Kyoto households ordered catered food (*shidashi*) for guests because home cooking was thought rude — the city's account of Kyoto, not of Japan. Station bento are covered in the food-shopping overview.

## Where to eat it

Kyoto has the most official material: the registered Kyō-ryōri tradition, restaurants inside temple grounds, and shōjin specialists; in February and March 2024 the Agency for Cultural Affairs held Kyoto cooking events on 11 days at 11 long-established ryōtei and other restaurants. Shōjin is also served at temple lodgings on Kōya (see the shōjin page), and JNTO's guide lists three Tokyo kaiseki restaurants alongside Kyoto ones.

## The same name, a different place

I found no official comparison of kaiseki or shōjin between Kyoto and other cities — of price, quantity, ingredients or quality — so this page makes none. What the sources do describe:

- **Kyoto's own worries.** A Kyoto prefecture request to the national government says ryōtei, where the skills of Japanese cooking are passed on, have fallen by about 90% in 30 years, and that finding proprietresses and servers is a problem. That is the prefecture's claim; the document does not show the data.
- **Temple food has a different structure at the temple.** On Kōya, the temple that serves you is also where you sleep and where you are invited to the morning service. A city restaurant does not have that setting. That is a difference in structure, not a judgment of the food.
- **Stock is a question for each restaurant, not each city.** Kyoto's tourism site warns that some shōjin restaurants use fish stock.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kaiseki is one thing."** It is two meals with one reading, and most restaurant kaiseki is the banquet.
- **"There is an official order of banquet courses."** I could not find one; the farm ministry says the cook may change the order.
- **"Honzen, kaiseki and shōjin are grades of the same meal."** The sources describe them as coming from different occasions.
- **"The price on the menu is the bill."** Some restaurants add a service charge; temple restaurants may need an entry fee.
