In the farm ministry’s description of the basic Japanese meal, pickles sit beside the rice as one of its parts. Tsukudani, small fish and seaweed simmered down in soy, is described by the same ministry as a supporting player that tightens up a meal, and a standard with rice in tea or inside onigiri. Furikake, which the ministry lists only as a seasoned dried product, is the third family here.

This page covers three families of them — pickles (*tsukemono*), tsukudani and furikake — with the most space for the one the sources say most about, Kyoto’s pickles. For rice and the things around it in general, start from the rice and sides overview.

## Where it came from

**Pickles.** The farm ministry’s guide to traditional foods says pickles were born from fermentation as a preservation technique, and that they are closely tied to climate. In cold regions they developed as a way to store food; in warm regions they are more a simple side dish for eating the staple. The ministry’s food-education white paper describes the basic meal, *ichijū sansai*, as rice, a soup and three dishes, combined with pickles. An account that the rice–soup–side–pickle pattern took shape at the end of the Heian period is something I could only find in search summaries, so it is not given here.

**Tsukudani.** Three official pages tell the story in three ways, and this site does not pick one:

- **The farm ministry’s Tokyo entry** says Tokugawa Ieyasu moved fishermen from Tsukuda village in Settsu to Tsukudajima at the mouth of the Sumida river, and that tsukudani is named after the island. Simmering strongly in *tamari* soy brought from Kishū made it keep longer.
- **The ministry’s guide to traditional foods** starts in Osaka: tsukudani began in Tsukuda village as small fish boiled with salt, a preserved food of the fishermen. Tsukudani-making in Edo took off after the village’s fishermen moved there.
- **The Fisheries Agency’s blog**, with material from the national association of prepared-food makers, says the fishermen of Tsukuda are said to have helped Ieyasu at the time of the Honnōji incident of 1582 and given him their simmered small fish. Later called to Edo, they named their small island Tsukudajima after home, and selling the surplus preserved food is said to be the start of tsukudani. The page carries the note that there are several views. It also gives 29 June as "tsukudani day", from the founding of the Sumiyoshi shrine on Tsukudajima on 29 June 1646 and the number 29.

The Ieyasu story is tradition. The Osaka start and the Tsukudajima start are both told, and both are given here. A further account naming a particular maker in 1858 appeared only in search summaries, so it is not.

**Furikake.** The ministry’s guide lists furikake only as a "seasoned dried product", alongside *mirin-boshi*, with no method or history. I opened the page for Mishima Foods’ furikake museum, and it did not describe the origin. I could not open an industry association’s or other company’s account. The origin of furikake is not given on this site.

## What makes it tsukemono

**Classified by what it is pickled in.** The ministry’s guide sorts pickles this way:

- **Salt** — *nozawana-zuke*, *takana-zuke*, *umeboshi*.
- **Soy sauce** — *fukujin-zuke*.
- **Miso** — miso pickles.
- **Sake lees** — *narazuke*.
- **Rice malt** (*kōji*) — *bettara-zuke*.
- **Vinegar** — *rakkyō-zuke*, *senmaizuke*.
- **Rice bran** (*nuka*) — *takuan*, *iburi-gakko*.

**Kyoto.** The ministry’s page on Kyoto pickles (*kyō-tsukemono*) gives three reasons the city developed them: there is no sea around Kyoto, so preserving techniques grew; the basin climate and good groundwater suited vegetable growing; and the many temples helped pickle culture develop. It names *senmaizuke*, *suguki-zuke*, *shibazuke*, rape-blossom pickles and *kyō-mibuna* pickles, eaten with rice, in *ochazuke* (rice with tea poured over) and with drinks.

- **Suguki.** Made from *suguki*, a kind of Kyoto heirloom turnip with its own sourness, pickled with salt alone and lactic-fermented. It is said to have begun in the early Edo period, made at first only by shrine families and presented to the imperial court as a luxury. It is harvested and pickled from mid-November to January. The Kyoto pickles page adds that it was long produced only in Kamigamo.
- **Senmaizuke.** Thin slices of *shōgoin* turnip, salted; households make it from around November. The ministry’s two pages spell its inventor differently — "Ōfuji Tōzaburō" in one, "Daikokuya Tōzaburō", a cook of the imperial palace, in the other — but both say a palace cook devised it in the Edo period. Eaten as it is, or, as the ministry suggests, with *karasumi* or smoked salmon between slices.
- **Shibazuke.** A speciality of Ōhara, made with red shiso, and said to have been named by Kenreimon-in.

**Tsukudani at the table.** The Tokyo entry lists whitebait, nori, small shrimp and shellfish as the main ingredients. It says tsukudani is seldom the main dish; its strong seasoning makes it a supporting player that tightens up a meal. It is a standard with *ochazuke* and as an onigiri filling.

## Not all of Japan’s pickles are this

The ministry’s guide gives regional examples shaped by climate:

- **Akita, *iburi-gakko*.** In winter it is hard to dry daikon outdoors, so it is smoked over the open hearth to dry it.
- **Kiso, Nagano, *sunki*.** Where salt was precious, it is fermented with lactic bacteria and no salt.
- **Nara, *narazuke*.** Said to go back to the time of the Heijō capital.
- **Kagoshima, *yamagawa-zuke*.**

Tsukudani also has regional kinds beyond Tokyo: the ministry lists smelt (*wakasagi*) tsukudani from Kasumigaura in Ibaraki and Hyōgo’s *ikanago no kugini*.

## Tsukemono away from home

I could not find a source comparing these foods where they come from with what is sold elsewhere. What the sources show is that Kyoto’s pickles are tied to Kyoto’s climate, water and temple culture, and that tsukudani is several local foods — Tsukudajima’s, Kasumigaura’s smelt, Hyōgo’s *kugini* — rather than one.

## What people get wrong

- **"Pickles are a garnish."** In the ministry’s description of the basic meal, they are one of its parts.
- **"All Japanese pickles are salted."** The ministry’s list includes soy, miso, sake lees, malt, vinegar and rice bran. *Sunki* uses no salt at all.
- **"Tsukudani has one origin story."** Three official pages tell it three ways, and one of them notes that there are several views.
- **"Furikake was invented by …"** Any name you read is one this site could not confirm.
