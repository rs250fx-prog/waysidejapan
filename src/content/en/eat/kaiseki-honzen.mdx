Honzen ryōri is the ceremonial meal of Japan's warrior households, served on a row of individual trays — from a first tray, the *honzen*, up to a fifth. Few visitors will sit down to the full form, but it stands behind much of what they do eat: the official sources trace the restaurant banquet, the guests' meal at Zen temples and, by contrast, tea kaiseki back to it. This page sets out what the farm ministry and Kyoto city say about it, and where its shape still shows.

For the formal meal in general, start from the [kaiseki overview](/en/eat/kaiseki/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's booklet *Washoku no Ryūgi* tells the history of Japanese food as a sequence. First home cooking; then the hospitality food and grand banquets of the Heian court nobility; then, in the medieval period, honzen ryōri — the warriors' hospitality food, which grew by adding to the number of trays — alongside the shōjin ryōri of Zen monasteries. Tea kaiseki was completed in the Momoyama period, and in the Edo period the restaurant banquet grew out of the warriors' honzen ryōri.

Kyoto city's education board, in material issued in October 2019, is more specific. Honzen ryōri, it says, was food for ceremonies, formed in the Muromachi period mainly among warrior households, and it is said to have become the base of formal Japanese cooking.

A Kyoto city handbook on the city's food culture lists honzen ryōri among the traditions that Kyoto cuisine inherits, alongside court cuisine (*yūsoku ryōri*), shōjin ryōri, kaiseki and river-fish cooking.

The ministry places honzen in the medieval period; Kyoto's board names the Muromachi period. Neither gives a year, and this page gives none.

## What makes it honzen

- **Trays, counted.** Kyoto's board says the meal ran from a first tray to a fifth. The main first tray was called the *honzen*, and that gave the meal its name.
- **More trays, more dishes.** The farm ministry explains the arithmetic through *ichijū sansai*, one soup and three dishes. Until the early 20th century, it says, people in Japan each had a small tray of their own, and one soup and three dishes is what fitted on it. For guests the custom was to bring out two trays or more; with two, the soups and side dishes increase to two soups and five dishes (*nijū gosai*). One soup and three dishes, in the ministry's words, means an everyday meal of a single tray.
- **An occasion, not a daily meal.** Kyoto's board calls honzen food for ceremonies; the ministry calls it warrior hospitality. Both tie it to an occasion.

The sources for this page do not list the dishes that went on each tray, so it describes no typical menu.

## Honzen and tea kaiseki

Tea kaiseki is described against honzen. The farm ministry's booklet says that in Sen no Rikyū's time, about 400 years ago, the lavish honzen ryōri, with a second and third tray carried in, was the norm. Rikyū's tea meal kept to one tray: rice and soup, then *mukōzuke*, a simmered dish and a grilled dish — one soup and three dishes. The order of that meal is on the [tea kaiseki page](/en/eat/kaiseki-tea/).

## Honzen and the banquet

The same booklet gives two accounts of where the restaurant banquet came from:

- One chapter says that in the Edo period it developed from warrior honzen ryōri, as a meal for drinking with many dishes laid out.
- Another says that what is called Kaiseki in Japan today is a banquet for drinking that developed on the base of tea food.

This site gives both and does not choose.

## Honzen at the temple

The tray-by-tray form also reached the hospitality of Zen temples. The farm ministry's booklet says that from the late Muromachi into the Edo period, lords, tea masters and wealthy merchants who came to practise Zen at Daitokuji in Kyoto were served a hospitality shōjin in the honzen style. It was enriched with tofu, yuba, fresh fu and natto, and was quite different from the monks' own meal of one soup and one dish. This honzen shōjin is said, the booklet adds, to have influenced the tea kaiseki that tea masters developed later, and it can still be eaten at restaurants serving Daitokuji. Temple food is covered on the [shōjin ryōri page](/en/eat/kaiseki-shojin/).

On Kōya, the temple lodging association names its lunch sets by their trays: a basic and a standard type with the *honzen* and a second tray (*ni-no-zen*), and a fuller type with a third (*san-no-zen*). Prices may differ by temple. The association describes its shōjin as evolved from the temples' own hospitality food. On 2026-10-11 its page said lunch bookings were suspended.

## How to eat it now

- **Through temple hospitality.** The Kōya tray sets and the restaurants serving Daitokuji are where the sources show the honzen shape still served. Check that the service is running before you plan around it.
- **Book ahead.** The Kōya association takes lunch bookings through a form on its site when they are open; Kyoto's tourism site recommends booking shōjin restaurants in advance.

The sources for this page name no restaurant serving the full five-tray meal and give no tax-included price, so none appear here.

## Honzen away from Kyoto

Neither the farm ministry nor Kyoto city compares honzen ryōri in Kyoto with honzen elsewhere, and this page makes no comparison. What the sources do show is a matter of setting: in their accounts honzen survives mostly inside other forms — the guests' meal at Daitokuji, Kōya's lunch trays in Wakayama — rather than as a stand-alone restaurant meal.

## What people get wrong

- **"Honzen is an older name for kaiseki."** The sources describe tea kaiseki as keeping to one tray against honzen's several, and give two accounts of the banquet's origin, only one of them honzen.
- **"One soup and three dishes is the formal meal."** The farm ministry calls it the everyday single-tray meal; guests got two trays or more.
- **"The honzen is the whole meal."** It is the first tray; the meal could run to five.
- **"Temple food is what monks eat."** The honzen-style shōjin served at Daitokuji was a guests' meal.
