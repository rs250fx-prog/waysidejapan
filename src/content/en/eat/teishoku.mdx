A *teishoku* — a set meal — is the most ordinary restaurant meal in Japan: a tray with a bowl of rice, a bowl of soup, a main dish such as grilled fish or ginger pork, and a couple of small sides. It looks like a chain-restaurant invention. The arrangement is much older than that, and the farm ministry describes it as the basic shape of Japanese eating. This page explains the shape, how today's chains sell it, and what breakfast looks like. The lineage pages cover the Japanese breakfast and the café "morning" of Nagoya.

## What it is

**The shape.** The farm ministry describes a Japanese meal as rice, soup, side dishes (*sai*) and pickles (*kō no mono*), and dates that structure to the late Heian period. *Ichijū sansai*, "one soup, three dishes", means rice, a soup, a main dish and two sides; another ministry page counts it as rice and soup plus three dishes, with pickles thought of as part of the rice. The soup is miso soup or a clear soup; the dishes are grilled, simmered or dressed.

**Why it lasted.** Build a meal around rice — a main dish that is mostly animal protein, side dishes of vegetables, potatoes or beans, and a soup full of ingredients — and nutritional balance comes fairly easily. The ministry says that is why the pattern has lasted for centuries: it makes menus simple and rational to plan. Its guidebook puts the priority plainly: the soup and the dishes exist to eat the rice.

**A shape, not a rule.** The ministry calls it a pattern, a basic form. I have found no statistics showing how many meals in Japan actually follow it.

## How it is laid out

The farm ministry's layout:

- **Rice** on your left, **soup** to its right.
- **The main dish**, such as grilled fish, behind the soup.
- **A side dish** behind the rice.
- **Small bowls** between rice and soup.
- **A whole fish** with its head to the left.

Each dish has its own plate, the ministry says, so that flavours do not mix — a courtesy to the person eating.

## How the chains sell it

I could not find an official history of the word *teishoku* or of the set-meal restaurant, so this section describes what the chains sell today, not where it came from. What they share is the shape above: choose a main, and rice, soup and sides come with it.

- **Yayoi-ken.** Its brand line is that "Japanese set meals make people happy". Rice is free to refill with set meals and breakfasts, it says; with barley rice, only white rice is refilled.
- **Ōtoya.** Charcoal grilling, raw garnishes such as grated ginger, and a "100% domestic rice" heading on its site. In April 2026 it renewed its menu with combination sets — "everyday", "energy" and "recovery" sets with small side bowls — plus rice toppings and an "add one more dish" section. Contents and prices vary by shop. Its pages say nothing about rice refills.

## Where to eat it

- **Anywhere** — set-meal chains and beef-bowl chains, with breakfast sets in the morning.
- **A ryokan** — the full Japanese breakfast, as the farm ministry's shape on a tray.
- **Nagoya and Ichinomiya** — the café *morning*, toast and egg with your coffee.

## The same meal, a different place

- **The chains run on one shape.** Rice, soup, a main and a small side, at ¥430–640 for breakfast at the chains above.
- **The refill rule is the shop's.** Yayoi-ken states free rice refills; Yoshinoya, Sukiya and Matsuya's breakfast pages say nothing either way.
- **Nagoya does breakfast differently.** There, a café breakfast is what comes free with the coffee. See the morning page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Teishoku is fast food."** It is the farm ministry's basic shape of a Japanese meal, sold quickly.
- **"Every Japanese meal is one soup, three dishes."** It is a pattern the ministry describes, not a count of what people eat.
- **"Rice refills are free everywhere."** At Yayoi-ken, yes. Elsewhere, ask.
- **"The tray is arranged at random."** The ministry gives a layout: rice left, soup right, main behind the soup.
