If you have eaten ramen in a Japanese suburb in the last decade, there is a fair chance it was this: an opaque brown soup that is both pork-bone and soy sauce, a yellow slick of chicken oil, thick short noodles, a clump of spinach, three sheets of nori standing against the rim, and a bowl of rice on the side. *Iekei* — "the -ya lineage" — began as one man's shop in a Yokohama suburb in 1974 and is now the most copied ramen format in the country, copied so widely that the lineage's own members argue about who belongs.

## Where it came from

In 1974 Yoshimura Minoru opened a small ramen shop in Shin-Sugita, a suburb on the south side of Yokohama. His own account is plain: a rich soup on pork bone and chicken, with a house soy *tare*. The Ramen Museum's timeline records the same year and calls the shop the origin of iekei. Yoshimuraya moved to the west exit of Yokohama station in 1999 and into its own two-storey, 30-seat building nearby in March 2023; it serves about 1,500 people a day and, by its own count, some 300 people have trained in its kitchen as apprentices or apprentices' apprentices.

The name came later and from outside. Shops opened by people who had trained at Yoshimuraya took names ending in *-ya* (家) — Rokkakuya, Kondōya — and, in the museum's words, the term "iekei" was born on the internet among enthusiasts to describe the family. Yoshimura's own view, on his website: it just came to be called that.

## What makes it iekei

**The soup** is *tonkotsu-shōyu*: pork bones (the museum notes vegetables and fruit go in too) boiled hard until cloudy, seasoned with soy sauce, and finished with *chi-yu*, chicken oil — the yellow layer on top, which the museum's dictionary describes as rendered chicken fat added in small quantity for aroma and sweetness. It is thicker and saltier than Kyushu tonkotsu, and it is seasoned with soy, which Kyushu's is not.

**The noodle** is thick, short and straight — Yoshimuraya's own words are "thicker and shorter" — the opposite of Hakata's hair-thin strand. A thick noodle in a heavy soup does not go limp, so there is no refill culture; the second course is rice.

**On top:** sliced pork, spinach, and sheets of nori — three in the standard bowl, by the trade-press description; Yoshimuraya's chāshū-men carries three slices of pork in the regular size and five in the medium and large. On the counter: grated ginger, garlic, vinegar, chilli-bean paste.

**The three settings.** Yoshimuraya's menu asks for the bowl in three dimensions — noodle: soft, normal, hard; oil: little, normal, more; flavour: light, normal, strong — and every shop in the lineage asks the same three questions. Say *futsū* three times and you get the house bowl.

## Why Yokohama

There is no fish market or climate story here; iekei is a case of one shop and its apprentices. What Yokohama supplied was a port city of commuters and a tradition of taking in food from outside — Japan's first Chinese restaurant opened here in 1870, by the museum's timeline — and, after 1994, the Ramen Museum in Shin-Yokohama, where Rokkakuya was one of the eight opening shops. Rokkakuya left the museum in 2003 and closed its main shop in 2017; in April 2024 it returned to the museum as "Rokkakuya 1994+", the first iekei there in 21 years.

## Who counts

This is where the lineage gets political, and the categories below are the ones the trade uses.

- **Chokkei (直系), the direct line.** Yoshimuraya's definition is strict: a shop whose owner trained at Yoshimuraya *and* opened *and* runs a successful iekei shop. Finishing the apprenticeship is not enough. On the date of this page the official list has five shops: Sugitaya (Shin-Sugita, Yokohama), Hajimeya (Uozu, Toyama), Jōetsuya (Jōetsu, Niigata), Atsugiya (Atsugi) and Takamatsuya (Takamatsu). Sugitaya, opened in September 1999 back in Shin-Sugita, is "direct-line shop number one", serves about 500 bowls a day, and opens at five in the morning.
- **The Honmokuya–Rokkakuya branch.** The "three great houses" of the early days were Yoshimuraya, Honmokuya and Rokkakuya; Rokkakuya's founder had trained at Yoshimuraya and managed Honmokuya before opening in 1988. This branch and its descendants are iekei by any reasonable definition but not *chokkei*.
- **Ichi-kei (壱系).** Shops descended from Ichirokuya, opened in Isogo in 1992 in admiration of Yoshimuraya but without training there. Fans call this the "Ichi line", and shops with no connection at all "inspired".
- **Shihon-kei (資本系), the capital line.** The trade press's term for iekei run by large restaurant companies. Machida Shōten, started in Machida in 2008 and run by Gift Holdings, and Ichikakuya, run by Garden Co. (listed in Tokyo in 2024, more than 100 Ichikakuya shops and 212 in the group as of May 2026), are the two names you will see from Hokkaido to Kyushu. The museum's summary, written 25 years after 1974, was already "several hundred shops".

None of this changes what is in the bowl much. It changes who is allowed to put the word *iekei* on the sign, and in Yokohama people care.

## How to order it

1. Buy a ticket at the machine. Ramen, size, and rice if you want it.
2. Hand it over and answer three questions — noodle, oil, flavour. *Futsū* for all three is the house bowl; *katame, ōme, koime* (hard, more oil, strong) is the enthusiast's order and not a good first one.
3. Rice is the second course, not a side. The common way to eat it — soak a sheet of nori in the soup and fold it round a mouthful of rice — is something I have watched at every counter and could not find written down by any shop, so take it as observation.
4. The counter condiments are for the second half of the bowl: ginger lightens it, vinegar cuts the fat, garlic makes it heavier.

## Getting there

Yoshimuraya is a few minutes' walk from Yokohama station's west exit. From Haneda Airport, Keikyū's airport line runs direct to Yokohama in 28 minutes, ¥400 (¥397 by IC card); to Shinagawa for Tokyo in 14 minutes, ¥330. For Sugitaya, Keikyū's Sugita station (¥510 from Haneda) is next to JR Shin-Sugita, four minutes from the shop. JR's own fares from Tokyo station changed in March 2026 and I could not confirm them from an official page, so none are given.

## What people get wrong

- **"Iekei is tonkotsu."** It is tonkotsu-*shōyu*; the soy and the chicken oil are the point. The word "tonkotsu ramen" for the white Kyushu bowl is itself a 1970s coinage.
- **"Rice is free."** ¥130 at the original. Free rice is a chain promotion at some branches, some of the time.
- **"Any shop called -ya is iekei."** The founder recognises five direct-line shops. The hundreds of others range from faithful to inspired, and the chains are a category of their own.
- **"It is old."** 1974. The founder is still alive and his shop still has a queue.
