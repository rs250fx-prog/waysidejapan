Ramen is the dish visitors are most likely to eat on their first night and least likely to understand. It looks like one thing. It is a dozen, and the people of Fukuoka, Sapporo and Kitakata would each tell you the others are eating something else. This page is the map: what the word covers, why a bowl costs what it does in Japan, and where each lineage comes from. The lineage pages go deep on one city at a time.

## What it is

Wheat noodles made with alkaline water (*kansui*), which gives them their yellow colour and spring, in a hot seasoned broth, with a few toppings. That is the whole definition, and it is why the regional variants can differ so much: everything except the noodle is up for grabs.

It is also, by Japanese standards, new. The Shin-Yokohama Ramen Museum's timeline starts the modern story in 1884, with an advertisement for "Nanking soba" in a Hakodate newspaper, and gives 1910 — the opening of Rairaiken in Asakusa, Tokyo — as the year ramen became popular. The museum is careful about the wording: not the first ramen shop, but the first to start a ramen boom, with 2,500–3,000 customers a day in its busy season. The dish was called *Nanking soba*, then *shina soba*, then *chūka soba* — the same food renamed as the politics of the word for China changed — and the katakana word *rāmen* only became general after 1958, when Nissin put it on a packet of instant noodles.

The war reset it. Ramen stalls fed the black markets of 1945 as cheap, high-calorie food; the white pork-bone soup of Kyushu appeared in 1947, Sapporo's miso in 1954 by one account and 1958 by another, and dipping noodles (*tsukemen*) in 1955. Nissin's Chicken Ramen launched in August 1958 at ¥35 a packet when a ball of udon cost ¥6; Cup Noodle followed in 1971 at ¥100. Japan now produces about six billion servings of instant ramen a year, 48 per person.

The regional boom came in the 1980s — Kitakata in 1984, Ogikubo in 1985, the Tokyo tonkotsu wave in 1989 — and the museum itself opened in Shin-Yokohama in March 1994 so that people could eat the country's ramen "without getting on a plane". In December 2015 a nine-seat shop in Sugamo, Tsuta, became the first ramen shop in the world with a Michelin star.

## Why it is the way it is

Two mechanics explain most of what you will see.

**The noodle is engineered to the soup.** The museum's dictionary gives the two numbers that matter. *Hydration* is the share of water in the dough: about 35% is typical, Kitakata runs near 40% (soft, slippery, slow to go limp), Hakata 24–28% (dry, hard, quick to soften, hence the refill). *Cutter number* is how many strands come out of a 30 mm band: a low number is a thick noodle (18 and below), Hakata is around 26. When a shop asks how hard you want the noodles, it is asking a question that only makes sense for its own noodle.

**The soup is two layers.** A stock (*dashi*) from animal bones, dried fish, or both, and a seasoning (*tare*) of soy sauce, salt or miso that is added to the bowl, not the pot. "Shōyu ramen" and "shio ramen" describe the tare; "tonkotsu" describes the stock. This is why "tonkotsu-shōyu" (Yokohama's iekei) is not a contradiction, and why "tonkotsu" as the name of a style is, by the museum's account, a coinage of the late 1970s — the Kyushu shops had simply called it ramen.

The old Tokyo bowl — clear chicken-and-pork stock with a Japanese fish dashi folded in, soy tare, a wavy noodle, pork, bamboo shoot, a slice of fish cake, spinach — is the baseline the lineages diverge from. The museum's phrase for what the postwar shops settled on is "soy, wavy noodle, Japanese dashi", and it calls that the thing that made ramen Japanese.

## What it costs

The figures above are kept on the price reference page and update there. What they show is a market in three tiers, and the credit-research firm Teikoku Databank now describes it exactly that way: about ¥500, about ¥1,000, and ¥1,500 and up.

- The **statistical bowl** — "chūka soba, eating out, one bowl", central Tokyo, government survey — was ¥620 in September 2026, up from ¥569 in May 2024. The industry's own figure for the national average is still under ¥700.
- The **chain bowl** is ¥420–750 (Hidakaya, Kourakuen, Gyoza no Ohsho).
- The **specialist bowl** in a city is ¥900–1,200. Tokyo's postwar classic, Harukiya in Ogikubo, is ¥950; the founding iekei shop, Yoshimuraya, is ¥890; Sapporo's miso originator went to ¥1,200 in September 2026; Ichiran's head shop is ¥1,080.
- The **starred bowl** is ¥2,000 at Tsuta.

The phrase everyone in the trade uses is the *¥1,000 wall* — the price above which customers are said to stop ordering a plain bowl. Nobody can tell you who coined it, but the pressure behind it is documented: Teikoku Databank's ingredient-cost index for a pork-bone bowl stood 29% above its 2020 level across 2024, and a record 72 ramen shops went bankrupt that year, up from 53. The market still grew — ¥790 billion in 2024, ¥885.5 billion in 2025 — because the shops that survived raised prices. The 2016 economic census counted 18,041 ramen shops in Japan.

For a visitor the practical reading is this: under ¥700 you are eating what Japanese people eat on a weekday; around ¥1,000 you are at a shop someone chose on purpose; above ¥1,500 you are paying for a name, and the queue is part of the product.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **The Kyushu styles are a family with a quarrel.** Kurume (1937, white soup 1947), Hakata and Kumamoto descend from each other and argue about who was first; Kagoshima is said to owe nothing to any of them. The Hakata page lays out the two origin claims without choosing.
- **The northern styles are about the noodle.** Kitakata's wide, wet, wavy noodle and Sapporo's egg-enriched wavy noodle were both products of a specific noodle maker working with a specific shop. Sapporo's dates from January 1955 and the company still supplies the city.
- **Tokyo has two traditions**, not one: the clear soy *chūka soba* of Ogikubo and the old shitamachi shops, and the heavy pork-bone-and-soy *iekei* that started in a Yokohama suburb in 1974 and has since become a national chain category.
- **Everything is younger than it looks.** The oldest lineage on the table has a documented start in 1884; most are postwar; the Michelin-starred modern "clear" style dates from 2012.

## Where to eat it, and how to get there

Each lineage page ends with the town, the shops that publish their prices, and the way in from the nearest airport or Shinkansen station, taken from the operators' own fare pages. In brief:

- **Hakata** — Fukuoka. The airport is on the city subway: 5 minutes to Hakata station, 11 to Tenjin, ¥260.
- **Sapporo** — JR's Airport rapid from New Chitose, 33–37 minutes, ¥1,230; the Ramen Yokochō alley is three minutes from Susukino station.
- **Kitakata** — the Ban'etsu West line from Kōriyama via Aizu-Wakamatsu; the morning shops open at seven, so stay the night before.
- **Yokohama iekei** — Yoshimuraya is by Yokohama station's west exit; Keikyū from Haneda Airport is 28 minutes and ¥400.
- **Tokyo soy** — Harukiya is two minutes from Ogikubo station on the Chūō line.

## The same name, a different bowl

Every lineage page on this site now ends with a section on what changes when the dish leaves home, because it usually does, and because Japanese people are often as unaware of it as visitors. The pattern across the five lineages written so far:

- **Chains carry a price map.** Ichiran's classic bowl is ¥980 in Fukuoka's Tenjin, ¥1,080 at its head shop, ¥1,180 in Shibuya and Dōtonbori. Same bowl, same ¥210 refill, dearer rent.
- **Tokyo reformulated Kyushu on purpose.** The shop that led the 1989 tonkotsu boom says it invented a "mild tonkotsu" because Tokyo would not accept the real thing.
- **The noodle travels further than the shop.** Sapporo's noodle maker has shipped to Honshu since 1965 and now supplies about 3,500 shops nationwide; the founding Sapporo shops mostly never left.
- **The chain is a different company from the original.** Kitakata Ramen Bannai (Tokyo, 1988) is not Bannai Shokudō (Kitakata, 7 a.m.); the town's association certifies nobody outside the town.
- **Nobody owns the name.** Iekei, Sapporo, Hakata and Kitakata are descriptions, not designations. The only certification in Japanese ramen is Yoshimuraya's private list of five direct-line shops.

And one number worth carrying: in the government household survey, the city that spends most on ramen eaten out is Yamagata (¥21,695 a year per household), followed by Niigata, Sendai, Utsunomiya and Toyama. Sapporo, Fukuoka, Yokohama and Osaka — the homes of the famous styles — are all below the national average. The place that invented a bowl is not the place that eats it most.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ramen is traditional Japanese food."** It is a Chinese noodle made Japanese over the last century, and its most famous styles are postwar. The oldest documented sale is 1884.
- **"Tonkotsu is the default."** It is a Kyushu style that reached Tokyo as a fashion in 1989. Tokyo's own ramen is soy.
- **"Shōyu, shio, miso and tonkotsu are the four types."** Three are seasonings and one is a stock. A bowl is one of each, which is why the combinations exist.
- **"A bowl should be under ¥1,000."** The statistical bowl is ¥620 and the specialist bowl is around ¥1,000; the wall is real, and shops are climbing over it.
- **"Ask for hard noodles anywhere."** The hardness vocabulary belongs to the thin Hakata noodle. In Sapporo or Kitakata the question is not asked.
- **"The Michelin shop is the best ramen in Tokyo."** Tsuta was the first to be starred, in 2015; it held the star for four editions, moved in 2019, and is not in the current guide. It is a good ¥2,000 bowl, and a milestone — not a verdict.
