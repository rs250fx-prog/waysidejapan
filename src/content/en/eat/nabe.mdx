A bowl of miso soup at breakfast, a pot of oden, a hotpot: in Japan these feel like three different things. They are built the same way. Stock goes in a pot, things are simmered in it, and what separates one dish from the next is mostly what it is seasoned with — and, when that is miso, which miso. This page is the map. The lineage pages go deeper on miso soup and its regional misos, and on oden.

For the stock itself, start from the dashi overview; this page does not repeat it.

## What it is

The farm ministry’s materials on Japanese food treat dashi as the base of soups and simmered dishes. The dishes on this page all put ingredients into that kind of stock and cook them. Where they divide is the seasoning:

- **Miso.** Miso soup is a dashi of kombu, dried sardines (*niboshi*) or similar, with miso dissolved in it. Hokkaido’s Ishikari nabe is kombu stock with miso. Tamba-Sasayama’s botan nabe is cooked in a miso broth. Aichi’s miso-simmered udon and miso oden use soybean miso such as Hatcho miso.
- **Soy sauce.** Tokyo’s oden is simmered in a stock of katsuobushi and kombu seasoned with dark soy sauce, according to the farm ministry.
- **Either.** For chanko nabe, the ministry says the pot is seasoned with soy sauce or miso, and that it differs between shops and homes.

That is as far as the sources go. I would not turn it into a rule about east and west, or about which seasoning came first.

## Why it is the way it is

**Miso is three things, by its koji.** Japan’s product standard for miso (JAS 0022:2025) defines rice miso as steamed soybeans with rice koji and salt, barley miso the same with barley koji, and soybean miso as steamed soybeans on which the koji mould itself is grown, mixed with salt. A blended miso mixes these. The farm ministry adds that miso is also sorted by taste (salty, sweetish, sweet) and colour (red, white, light), and that rice miso is about 80 percent of Japan’s production. White miso is a kind of rice miso.

**Where each kind is made.** The same ministry article places them: rice miso nationwide; barley miso mainly in Kyushu, parts of Chugoku and Shikoku, and northern Kanto; soybean miso mainly in Aichi, Gifu and Mie. For soybean miso the ministry’s explanation is the climate: in hot, humid summers, miso made with rice or barley koji spoiled easily, so steamed soybeans were shaped into balls and the koji mould grown on them directly.

**Colour comes from making, not from a single recipe.** Nagano prefecture sorts miso into red, light and white, and explains that the higher the proportion of koji, the sweeter the miso. What "red miso" and "white miso" mean in Kansai, Nagoya and Kanto, I could not find in any public source — only in private explanations — so this page does not lay it out.

**Hatcho miso is protected, twice.** Hatcho miso, a soybean miso, is Japan’s Geographical Indication No. 49, registered on 15 December 2017, with Aichi as the production area. Two producer groups are registered, each with its own specification: one covering all of Aichi, one covering Hatcho-chō in Okazaki, with different ageing rules. Why there are two, the official documents I read do not explain, and this site does not judge between them. The miso lineage page has the details.

## Miso soup at home

The farm ministry recommends miso soup with rice at breakfast. Its guide for one serving is about 150 ml of hot water to one tablespoon of miso, with a little granulated dashi if you want it quick. The small grains you sometimes see are bits of the soybeans and koji, which the ministry says still hold fibre and are worth finishing.

Its Nagano page makes a case for the *gudakusan* (full of ingredients) miso soup: a bowl that gives you a main and a side dish at once, and lets you use less miso. The page’s figures are about 12 g of miso and 1.5 g of salt for an ordinary bowl, against about 10 g of miso and 1.2 g of salt for the full version.

Regional miso soups, and the misos behind them, are on the miso lineage page.

## Three hotpots with a story

**Chanko nabe (sumo).** The Japan Sumo Association, in a quiz on its own site, says that anything a sumo wrestler cooks is *chanko* — not only hotpot; curry made by a wrestler is chanko too. Where the word comes from, it says, has no settled answer. One theory points to a Chinese sheet-metal pot that reached Nagasaki; another to *chan*, father, because the stable master and his apprentices ate together, or because the older wrestler who cooked was affectionately called so. The farm ministry says chanko nabe is said to have begun in the Meiji era, lets wrestlers eat a lot at once, uses a chicken stock seasoned with soy or miso, and avoided four-legged meat such as beef and pork because a wrestler on all fours has lost. It places the dish in Sumida ward, notes that former wrestlers run chanko shops across Tokyo, and adds that the same ingredients cooked at home make an ordinary *yose-nabe*.

**Ishikari nabe (Hokkaido).** A fishermen’s dish from Ishikari, at the mouth of the Ishikari river, according to the farm ministry. Salmon fishing thrived there from the Edo period, and a good catch was celebrated by putting chunks of fresh salmon and its trimmings into a pot of miso soup. In the Shōwa 20s (1945–54) the town’s beach-seine fishing drew visitors, the dish was served to them while they waited, and it spread across Japan. It is kombu stock with miso, finished with sansho pepper; some homes add salmon roe or butter. Other claims about who made it first are not in the official source I used.

**Botan nabe (Tamba-Sasayama).** Wild boar in miso. Tamba-Sasayama city’s tourism site calls the town the birthplace and tells a precise story: it was first called *ino-nabe* and served to guests; after an army infantry regiment was stationed there in 1908, officers and visitors ate boar in miso soup at the inns and word spread; the name "botan nabe" first appears in the words of a folk song in 1931, chosen because *ino-nabe* did not sound right. The site also mentions a theory that boar was called *botan* as a euphemism in the Edo period. It is clear on one point: the peony-like plating came after the name, from around 1945 on. Miso is mostly a mix of red and white, different at each shop; the meat is wild boar; the site suggests 150–200 g per person and finishing with udon or rice porridge.

## Where to eat it

- **Aichi** — miso-simmered udon, miso oden and *nimiso*, cooked with soybean miso.
- **Kyoto** — white miso, though the farm ministry says it is mostly a New Year food at home rather than an everyday one.
- **Shizuoka, Himeji, Kanazawa** — three regional odens, each eaten differently. See the oden page.
- **Ishikari** — for Ishikari nabe, where the ministry places its origin.
- **Tamba-Sasayama** — botan nabe, at shops that each blend their own miso.
- **Tokyo** — chanko shops run by former wrestlers; the ministry places the dish in Sumida ward.

## The same name, a different place

I could not find any public source — ministry, tourism body, local government or trade association — that compares eating these dishes where they come from with eating them in Tokyo on the same terms. So this page does not say one is better. What the sources do show is that the same name is eaten differently by place:

- **Oden in Aichi** is dipped in, or covered with, miso sauce from a pot, and the ministry notes that convenience-store oden in the prefecture often comes with miso sauce.
- **Oden in Shizuoka** is sold by the skewer at sweet shops and finished with green laver and fish powder.
- **Oden in Himeji** is eaten with ginger-soy instead of mustard.
- **Oden in Kanazawa** has a crab item only in winter, and only on days the boats go out.
- **Hatcho miso** has a production area fixed by its GI registration — Aichi, or Hatcho-chō in Okazaki, depending on which specification. How that affects what is sold elsewhere, or at what price, I did not find.
- **Botan nabe** in Tokyo was not compared in any source I found.

## What people get wrong

- **"Miso soup is one thing."** The miso can be rice, barley or soybean, and the farm ministry’s own pages show each region leaning on a different one.
- **"White miso is Kyoto’s everyday miso."** The ministry says Kyoto households use it mainly for New Year *zōni* and do not eat it much day to day.
- **"Hatcho miso has one standard."** There are two registered specifications under one name.
- **"Oden is soy broth."** It started as miso-grilled tofu, and Aichi still eats it with miso.
- **"Chanko means hotpot."** In sumo it means whatever the wrestlers cook.
- **"Botan nabe is named after the plating."** The town’s own tourism site says the name came first.
