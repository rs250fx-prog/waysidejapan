Yakitori is sold everywhere in Japan, from standing bars to refined counters, festival stalls and convenience stores. Ordering it is easy. Two things are worth knowing before you do: what the shop is asking when it says *shio* or *tare*, and that in at least three Japanese towns, the word "yakitori" does not mean chicken.

For how an izakaya evening works, start from the izakaya overview.

## What it is

Japan's national tourism organisation (JNTO) defines yakitori as bite-sized pieces of chicken and vegetables threaded on skewers and grilled over charcoal, seasoned with either salt (*shio*) or *tare*, a sweet soy-based sauce.

## The cuts

JNTO's guides list the skewers you will most often see:

- **Momo** — thigh. Boned thigh meat, which JNTO says is what makes it juicy.
- **Negima** — chicken and spring onion, alternating.
- **Tsukune** — minced chicken.
- **Kawa** — skin.
- **Sunagimo** — gizzard.
- **Hatsu** — heart.
- **Rebā** — liver.
- **Nankotsu** — cartilage.
- **Sasami wasabi** — breast tenderloin with wasabi.

## How to order it

**Salt or tare.** The shop may ask which you want. JNTO's advice is not to worry about getting it right: you are only asked about skewers that suit both. It notes that offal often goes well with tare. If you are not asked, the skewer comes finished another way — with wasabi or pickled plum, for instance.

**Eating.** Straight from the skewer; no chopsticks needed. If there is a pot for used skewers, put them in it.

**Where it fits.** JNTO describes yakitori as a light dish with beer, and lists the places you will meet it: standing "beer bars", shops with refined seating, festival stalls, and convenience stores and supermarkets.

## Where "yakitori" is something else

The farm ministry's local food database records three towns where the word means a local dish, not the chicken skewer of a Tokyo izakaya.

**Higashimatsuyama, Saitama.** Pork *kashira* — the meat from the temple to the cheek — alternated with spring onion on a skewer, grilled slowly over charcoal, salted, and eaten with a miso sauce that differs shop to shop. There are about 50 shops in the city. The ministry says it spread as food for workers at large factories and is mostly served in red-lantern izakaya. Higashimatsuyama counts itself among Japan's "seven great yakitori towns", with Bibai, Muroran, Fukushima, Imabari, Nagato and Kurume.

**Muroran, Hokkaido.** "Yakitori" here is pork and onion on a skewer, eaten with Western-style mustard. In the south of Hokkaido, the ministry says, a menu that says yakitori means pork, and some shops write *buta-sei* to make the distinction from chicken. People eat it at izakaya and specialist shops; it is hardly made at home. The shop regarded as the first, Toriyoshi, opened in Wanishi-chō in 1937.

**Imabari, Ehime.** Chicken skin, not skewered, cooked on a thick iron griddle under a weight. The ministry says it was devised about 50 years ago, and that the city once had more than 100 griddle yakitori shops. Its suggested order for a regular: start with the skin, and finish with *senzanki*, Imabari's fried chicken.

## Eating it at home and away

**At home, a different dish.** Order "yakitori" in Muroran and you get pork and onion with mustard; in Higashimatsuyama, pork cheek with miso sauce; in Imabari, flat griddled chicken skin. The same word on a Tokyo menu means chicken skewers.

**Small places, small groups.** In Tokyo's yokochō alleys many bars have about ten seats, some fewer. Go in twos and threes.

**Fukuoka's stalls.** If you eat at a yatai, the city's tourism site says most charge no otoshi, ask for a drink and a dish per person, and take cash.

## What people get wrong

- **"There is a right choice between salt and tare."** JNTO says there is not; you are only asked when both suit the skewer.
- **"Yakitori means chicken."** Not in Higashimatsuyama or Muroran, where it is pork, or in Imabari, where it is griddled skin.
- **"Use chopsticks to take it off the skewer."** Eat it from the skewer.
- **"Yakitori is a restaurant dish."** It is sold at standing bars, festival stalls and convenience stores as well as at counters.
