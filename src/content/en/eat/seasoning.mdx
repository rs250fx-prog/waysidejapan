Japanese cooking leans on a short shelf of bottles: soy sauce, miso, vinegar, mirin, sugar and salt, with wasabi, mustard, ginger or sansho pepper on the side. This page is the map of that shelf — what each one is, where the official sources say it came from, and how soy sauce is actually made. The lineage pages go deeper on soy sauce by region and type, and on eggs, which in Japan are sold to be eaten raw.

Three neighbours are covered elsewhere and not repeated here: stock is on the dashi overview, miso on the nabe overview and its miso page, and pickles and natto on the rice-sides overview.

## What it is

The farm ministry’s guide to traditional foods says seasonings bring out the five elements of taste — sweet, salty, bitter, sour and umami — and draw out an ingredient’s own flavour. In Japan the familiar ones are fermented seasonings made from grain: miso, soy sauce, vinegar and mirin. Beside them grew a habit of serving sharp condiments — wasabi, *karashi* mustard, ginger, sansho — and the ministry gives an explanation that their antibacterial action has supported eating raw food such as sashimi.

**Sa-shi-su-se-so.** The basic five are remembered by their first syllables: *satō* (sugar), *shio* (salt), *su* (vinegar), *seuyu* (the old spelling of soy sauce) and *miso*. The Japan Soy Sauce Association’s Q&A uses the list to explain an order of adding them: sugar early, because it soaks in slowly; salt not too early, because it draws water out; soy near the end, to keep its aroma. Read that as a rule of thumb, not as a correct method.

The rest of the shelf, in the official sources’ words:

- **Vinegar.** The ministry says it is considered humanity’s oldest seasoning, born from sake turning sour, and thought to have reached Japan around the time of Emperor Ōjin; the Taihō Code of 701 set up an office that made sake, *amazake* and vinegar. In the later Edo period a cheaper vinegar from sake lees (*kasuzu*) spread with the fashion for Edo-style sushi. The ministry divides vinegar into brewed (grain, black, fruit) and synthetic, the latter made in small amounts, mostly for commercial pickling.
- **Mirin.** Steamed glutinous rice and rice koji are put into *shōchū* and left at 20–30 °C for 40 to 60 days, then pressed, filtered and stored for months. There are two origin stories — a sweet drink of the early Muromachi period, or a Ming Chinese honey liquor — and this site does not choose. The ministry lists its jobs as sweetness, helping flavour soak in, shine, keeping food from falling apart, and masking smells.
- **Sugar.** Cane from Okinawa and Kagoshima, beet from Hokkaido. The ministry says it arrived in the era of the envoys to Tang China as an expensive medicine and went into cooking only in the modern era. The Agriculture and Livestock Industries Corporation adds that rationing lasted until 1952 and use per person peaked at 29 kg in 1973, against "about 20 kg" now (the page gives no year). The ministry’s magazine says sugar holds water, slows the setting of egg protein, keeps vinegared rice from hardening, and helps browning.
- **Salt.** Large salt fields were established around the seventh century, the ministry says, and salt’s power to stop food rotting is said to have led to miso and soy sauce.
- **Cooking sake.** The ministry’s guide has no entry for it, and I could not find an official definition or explanation of why it is used.

## How soy sauce works

The soy association explains the brewing in a few steps.

**Three ingredients.** Soybeans (usually defatted), wheat and salt. Enzymes from koji mould break soy protein into amino acids, which give umami, and wheat starch into glucose, which gives sweetness and body. Lactic bacteria and yeast then produce acids that soften the saltiness, and the aroma. Salt keeps the useful microbes working slowly.

**Koji, then moromi.** Steamed soybeans and roasted wheat in roughly equal parts are seeded with koji starter. In the fully brewed method (*honjōzō*) the koji goes into a tank with brine, and the mash (*moromi*) is stirred over about six to eight months while it matures.

**Other methods.** In mixed brewing, amino-acid liquid — soy protein broken down with hydrochloric acid — is added to the moromi and matured for at least a month. Of the 681,019 kL shipped in 2024, 89.8% was fully brewed and 0.5% mixed-brewed; the remaining 9.8% or so (my subtraction) is presumably the third method, simply called "mixed". The association’s own data page says "about 80%" fully brewed, without a year; I use the 2024 spreadsheet.

**Colour, taste, smell.** The reddish-amber colour comes from melanoidins, formed when glucose from the wheat reacts with amino acids from the soy. Umami comes from about 20 amino acids, led by glutamic acid. Dark soy is 15–17% salt, but other components soften how salty it tastes. About 300 aroma compounds have been found, and the aroma helps cover the smell of fish and meat.

**Grades.** Under Japan’s agricultural standard (JAS), umami is measured as total nitrogen. For fully brewed dark soy, 1.20% is standard grade, 1.35% upper, 1.50% special, and 1.65% may be labelled *tokusen*. Licensed inspectors also judge colour and aroma.

**After opening.** Soy darkens as the melanoidins oxidise, faster when warm, so the association says keep it cool and dark. *Mentsuyu* (noodle sauce) is lower in salt and spoils faster: once a glass bottle is open, about 3–5 days for the ready-to-use kind, 2–3 weeks for double strength, 1–1.5 months for triple. *Genen* soy has half the usual salt or less (about 9%), *usu-shio* 80% or less (about 13%); they cost a little more because salt is removed after brewing.

## The five types

JAS divides soy sauce into five. Shares are of 2024 national shipments.

- **Koikuchi (dark)** — 84.9%. Used widely, in cooking and at the table.
- **Usukuchi (light)** — 11.5%. Light in colour, not salt: about 18%. Born in Kansai, used where the food’s colour and flavour should show.
- **Tamari** — 2.0%. Dark and thick; mainly Chūbu. For sashimi, teriyaki and rice crackers.
- **Saishikomi (re-brewed)** — 1.0%. Brewed with raw soy sauce instead of brine; mostly for the table.
- **Shiro (white)** — 0.6%. Paler than usukuchi and sweet. Born in Hekinan, Aichi.

## Where to see it made

The soy association says many soy sauce factories accept visitors. You arrange it with each factory, usually by phone, and conditions differ; most need booking. Yamaroku on Shōdoshima needs none, and asks visitors not to eat natto first, because natto bacteria are said to be the one microbe the moromi cannot cope with. The soy sauce page lists the factories.

## The same name, a different place

I could not find an official comparison of brewery soy sauce and supermarket soy sauce. What the sources show:

- **Where it is made is not where it is bought.** In 2024 Chiba shipped 37.6% of Japan’s soy sauce and Tokyo about 0.05%. Yet in the household survey’s 2023–25 city ranking, Chiba city bought 3,870 ml a household against a national 3,915 ml. The survey counts home purchases only and gives no reasons.
- **Most is made in tanks.** A brewer quoted by the farm ministry’s Kinki office said only about 1% is barrel-brewed — his statement, not a statistic.
- **Each region has a style.** Kikkoman’s regional guide, a company source, describes Kantō as dark-soy country, Kinki as the home of light soy, Chūbu as tamari and white, and Kyūshū as preferring sweet mixed soy.

The sources do not say regional soy is better, and neither does this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Light soy is low-salt soy."** It is light in colour and slightly saltier.
- **"Soy sauce is one thing."** Japan’s standard recognises five types.
- **"Sa-shi-su-se-so is the order you must cook in."** It is a memory aid.
- **"The brewing town buys the most."** Chiba makes the most; Chiba city buys less than the national average.
- **"Mentsuyu keeps like soy sauce."** It goes off much faster once opened.
