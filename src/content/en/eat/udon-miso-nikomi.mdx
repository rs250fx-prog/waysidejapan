Miso-nikomi udon is udon cooked in the pot it is served in. The farm ministry describes firm noodles simmered in a broth made with Hatchō miso, cooked in an earthenware pot, and eaten across the whole of Aichi. Two things set it apart from most udon. The noodle has no salt in it, and the miso is not the rice miso that most of Japan uses but a soybean miso, which the ministry describes as rich, sour and astringent. The two official accounts of where the dish came from do not agree, and this page sets them side by side. For udon in general, start from the [udon overview](/en/eat/udon/).

## Where it came from

- **The farm ministry**: miso-nikomi udon was a home dish. By the Meiji era it is *said* to have been served at restaurants in Ichinomiya, and it later spread with Nagoya at its centre.
- **The Cultural Agency**, which named it a 100-year food in fiscal 2023, in its "modern" category: its origin lies in the hōtō eaten by the warlord Takeda Shingen in the Warring States period, which was passed on to the Tokugawa.

The ministry's version begins in the home and names a town and an era; the Cultural Agency's reaches back to a warlord and to Yamanashi's noodle stew. Both are official pages. This page does not choose. For hōtō and the Takeda Shingen story as Yamanashi tells it, see [hōtō](/en/eat/udon-hoto/).

## What makes it miso-nikomi udon

**The noodle.** The farm ministry says the noodles are made from flour and water alone, without salt, "like hōtō", and that this is why they keep their bite even after long simmering. That runs against the usual rule. The ministry's story of Yamanashi's food explains that udon dough generally has salt added and is left to rest, while hōtō is made without salt and goes straight into the boiling pot, where it breaks down a little and thickens the broth. Gunma's *okkirikomi*, a wide noodle the ministry describes as unsalted and simmered without being boiled first, is also made without salt; see [Kantō's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/). The ministry's pages describe these dishes one by one and do not draw a line between them.

**The miso.** The broth is made with Hatchō miso, the best-known soybean miso. The name comes from the village of Hatchō, eight *chō* (about 870 m) from Okazaki castle, and now part of Okazaki city. The ministry describes soybean miso as made from soybeans, salt and water and matured for a year, sometimes two or three. Its explanation of why Aichi makes miso this way is the climate: in hot, humid summers, miso made with rice or barley koji spoiled and soured easily, so a method grew up of shaping steamed soybeans into balls and growing the koji mould on them directly. The ministry's magazine places soybean miso mainly in Aichi, Gifu and Mie, and says rice miso makes up about 80% of Japan's output. For miso across the country, see [miso by region](/en/eat/nabe-miso/).

**One name, two rulebooks.** Hatchō miso is Japan's geographical indication No. 49, registered on 15 December 2017. Two producer groups are registered, and their specifications differ:

- **The Aichi miso and tamari makers' cooperative.** Production area, Aichi prefecture. Soybeans and salt; miso balls at least 20 mm across and 50 mm long; matured at least a year in tanks under weights, or at least ten months at 25°C or above where the temperature is controlled. It dates the miso-ball method to around 1830–1844, and names miso-nikomi among the "Nagoya-meshi" dishes the miso typically seasons, with miso-katsu, miso oden and miso-nabe.
- **The Hatchō Miso cooperative**, added on 24 January 2025. Production area, Hatchō-chō in Okazaki. Soybeans, salt and water; fist-sized balls in wooden vats of about six tonnes, under about three tonnes of stones piled in a cone, matured for at least two summers and two winters without heating. It dates production in Hatchō village to the early Edo period.

The two documents describe the same name. The official pages do not say how two specifications came about, and this page does not judge between them.

**The pot.** The ministry names the earthenware pot (*donabe*) as a defining feature: the udon is simmered in it and brought to the table in it.

## How it is eaten

Beyond the pot, the ministry's entry says the dish is offered at many shops across the prefecture. Its other Aichi pages fill in the background.

**The miso habit.** Aichi's list in the farm ministry's database has 30 dishes, and seven have miso in their name, among them *ni-miso*, miso oden, miso dengaku and miso-nikomi udon. *Ni-miso* is the home version of the same idea: root vegetables simmered in soybean miso, with as much or as little liquid as each household likes, from a stew to something close to soup. The ministry says the leftover broth goes into rice porridge (*ojiya*) or udon, and that reheating it over several days deepens the flavour. For miso oden, which Aichi dips in miso rather than simmering in soy broth, see [oden](/en/eat/nabe-oden/).

**Aichi's other udon.** [Kishimen](/en/eat/udon-kishimen/) is the flat udon of the same prefecture, served in a soy-based broth and eaten cold in summer. Miso-nikomi and kishimen are both on the ministry's Aichi list and both 100-year foods. For the rest of the region's dishes, see [Chūbu's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/).

## Miso-nikomi udon away from Aichi

**The miso has a fixed home.** Under its GI registration, Hatchō miso is made in Aichi, or in Hatchō-chō in Okazaki, depending on the specification. The dish itself carries no registration of that kind.

**The lists.** Miso-nikomi udon is one of the 30 dishes on the farm ministry's Aichi list and a 100-year food (fiscal 2023, modern). Aichi has 14 100-year foods in all, among them kishimen (fiscal 2021, traditional) and *nikake udon* (fiscal 2025, traditional); *nikake udon* is not in the farm ministry's list. The two lists are chosen separately.

**Miso-katsu is a different case.** It uses the same kind of miso but is not in the farm ministry's list of Aichi dishes; see [miso-katsu](/en/eat/tonkatsu-miso-katsu/).

## What people get wrong

- **"All udon has salt in it."** Miso-nikomi noodles are flour and water, the ministry says, and that is why they stay firm in the pot.
- **"Its origin is settled."** The farm ministry starts it in Aichi homes and Ichinomiya restaurants; the Cultural Agency starts it with Takeda Shingen's hōtō.
- **"It began in Nagoya."** In the ministry's account Nagoya is where it spread from later, not where it was first served.
- **"Hatchō miso is one standard."** Two specifications, two production areas, one name.
- **"Every Nagoya miso dish is a listed local dish."** Miso-nikomi udon, miso oden and miso dengaku are on the farm ministry's list; miso-katsu is not.
