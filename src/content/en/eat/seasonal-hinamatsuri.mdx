Hinamatsuri, the Doll Festival on 3 March, has its own set of foods: *chirashi-zushi*, scattered sushi; a clear soup with clams; *hishi-mochi*, a diamond-shaped mochi in three colours; white sake; and *hina-arare*, a snack first made from the broken-up mochi. The farm ministry gives a meaning for each. It also records one of the clearest regional splits in Japan’s festival food: the same snack is salty in Kansai and sweet in Kanto.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the overview of food for annual events.

## Where it came from

The festival is *jōshi*, one of the five seasonal festivals. The ministry’s *washoku* culture material says *jōshi* was a day for purifying oneself of uncleanness. In the Heian period it joined an older Japanese belief in which a person’s impurities were passed into a doll, a stand-in figure, that was then set afloat on water. These floating dolls, *nagashi-bina*, are described as the original form of the Doll Festival. In the Edo period it became a festival for praying for girls’ growth, and doll displays began.

The ministry’s Hokkaido office gives the same account: the festival began as a purification in which misfortune and impurity were passed to dolls and sent away on water.

## What makes it Hinamatsuri

**Chirashi-zushi.** The ministry says its spring colours suit a celebration for girls, and that many of its toppings, such as prawns, are considered lucky.

**Clam soup.** The ministry says the two halves of a clam shell fit each other perfectly and fit no other shell, so the clam is a symbol of a happy marriage, and the soup is eaten wishing for a good match. The culture material tells a different history: that clams and lacquered food containers came into the festival from the custom of spring outings to the seashore. The two are given side by side here.

**Hishi-mochi.** Three explanations sit in three places:

- The ministry’s Hinamatsuri page says green stands for new buds, white for snow and pink for life, together showing spring as buds come up under the snow and peach blossom opens. The diamond shape comes from the water chestnut, *hishi*, a prolific plant, so it carries a wish for many descendants and a long life.
- The culture material says it was a mochi used at celebrations from the Muromachi period.
- The Hokkaido office says it was once called the "demon’s tongue", in the sense of warding off evil.

**White sake.** The ministry says the peach was thought to ward off evil and bring long life, that sake steeped with peach blossom was drunk, and that white sake, *shirozake*, later took its place. The same page suggests non-alcoholic *amazake* or juice for children.

**Hina-arare.** The ministry says it was originally made by breaking up the diamond mochi.

## How to eat it

The ministry’s page is written for celebrating at home, and gives meanings rather than an order of eating. The one practical note is the drink: amazake or juice instead of white sake for children.

## Not all of Hinamatsuri is this

The ministry says hina-arare in Kansai is round and salty, and in Kanto is shaped like grains of rice and sweet. It gives no reason for the difference, and I have not supplied one.

## Hina-arare away from Kansai, and away from Kanto

This is not a case of a home town and an outpost. It is one festival food that became two different things in two halves of the country. Neither is the original version in the ministry’s account; both come after the broken-up diamond mochi. I could not find an official source on how the two compare in Tokyo or Osaka shops today.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hinamatsuri was always about dolls on display."** The ministry describes floating dolls that carried impurity away on water as the original form; displays for girls came in the Edo period.
- **"The clam has one story."** The ministry gives the matching-shell meaning; its culture material gives a history from seaside outings.
- **"Hina-arare is one snack."** It is salty and round in Kansai, sweet and rice-shaped in Kanto.
- **"Children drink the white sake."** The ministry suggests amazake or juice for them.
