*Konamono* — "flour things" — is the Kansai word for dishes made from a wheat batter cooked on a hot plate: okonomiyaki, takoyaki, and their relatives. In the words of Osaka's tourism bureau they are the city's soul food, and they come with more competing origin stories than almost anything else on a Japanese menu. This page is the map: what the dishes are, what the official sources say about where they came from, and how the people of Osaka, Hiroshima and Tokyo actually eat them. The lineage pages go deep on okonomiyaki and on takoyaki with its Akashi cousin.

## What it is

There is no official definition of *konamono* that I could find; the farm ministry's Osaka okonomiyaki recipe credits a body called the Japan Konamon Association. What the official sources do record is which dishes belong to which place. The farm ministry's database of local dishes lists okonomiyaki and takoyaki as Osaka's two, okonomiyaki also as Hiroshima's, and Akashi-yaki (*tamagoyaki*) as Hyōgo's. Osaka's tourism bureau calls okonomiyaki, alongside takoyaki, Osaka people's soul food, and points out what they share: wheat flour mixed with dashi.

## Where it came from

The official accounts disagree, and they disagree in ways that cannot all be true. This site lists them and does not choose.

**Okonomiyaki.**

- **From a prewar penny snack.** Hiroshima prefecture says the dish began with *issen yōshoku* ("one-sen Western food"), a children's snack before the war: a thin crêpe of flour and water brushed with Worcestershire sauce. In the postwar food shortage it fed people, and around 1950 it took the name *okonomiyaki* — "grilled as you like it" — and added cabbage and Chinese noodles.
- **From a tea-ceremony sweet.** The farm ministry's Osaka entry says the root is said to be *funoyaki*, a sweet served at Sen no Rikyū's tea gatherings in the Azuchi–Momoyama period, which spread to ordinary people by the end of Edo; in the late Meiji era a snack called *yōshoku-yaki* — flour, cabbage and sauce — appeared, and shops developed it into okonomiyaki.
- **From Tokyo.** Osaka's tourism bureau gives two more: that Tokyo's *monjayaki* became the street-stall *dondon-yaki*, which became the sauce-flavoured *issen yōshoku* in the Kansai; or that it grew from a playful dish customers grilled themselves on the plate.

**Takoyaki.** The farm ministry says it is said to derive from *choboyaki*, a snack sold at sweet shops and fair stalls from the late Meiji to Taishō eras, with konjac, dried shrimp or pickled radish in a soy-flavoured batter. After radio broadcasting began at the end of the Taishō era, *rajio-yaki* ("radio grill") appeared, and in the early Shōwa period stalls in Osaka began using octopus. Osaka's tourism bureau adds that takoyaki shops multiplied in town only from around 1955–64 (the Shōwa 30s) — "the history is surprisingly short".

**Akashi-yaki.** The city of Akashi and the farm ministry suggest it began with the egg yolks left over from making *Akashi-dama*, an artificial coral, mixed with flour and local octopus. Akashi's records add that as a trade it is said to have started at a street stall in 1919. The city says there are several theories.

## Why it is the way it is

**Mix or layer.** The farm ministry's two entries explain the basic split in okonomiyaki. In Osaka, cabbage and other ingredients are mixed into a batter of flour, dashi and egg and grilled; the ministry notes that 70% of the result is said to be decided by how you mix and lay it on the plate, since over-mixing draws water from the cabbage. In Hiroshima, a thin round of batter is heaped with cabbage, bean sprouts, spring onion, tempura bits and pork, covered with more batter, turned, set on fried noodles and a thin round of egg, and turned again. But the ministry also says Osaka's early okonomiyaki were mainly *layered*, and mixing spread later. "Osaka mixes, Hiroshima layers" is the present, not the whole history.

**Sauce.** Japan's agricultural standard divides Worcestershire-type sauces by thickness into three: *Worcester*, *chūnō* (medium) and *nōkō* (thick). Takoyaki took its present form — thick sauce, green laver, bonito flakes, a boat-shaped tray and a toothpick — after thick sauce appeared after the war, according to the farm ministry. Osaka's tourism bureau says the sauce, mayonnaise, laver and bonito style came from okonomiyaki. Hiroshima's okonomiyaki uses a thick, sweet, fruit-based sauce. I could not find a primary source for the history of "okonomi sauce" itself.

**Dashi.** Osaka's tourism bureau stresses that both okonomiyaki and takoyaki batter are made with dashi, not water. Akashi-yaki takes it further: it is dipped in dashi instead of sauce.

Tsukiji Gindaco, the national takoyaki chain, posts different tax-included prices for the same food depending on where you eat it: eight standard takoyaki are ¥702 to take away and ¥715 to eat in; eight with spring onion, cheese and cod roe, or teriyaki egg, are ¥810 and ¥825. The gap matches Japan's two consumption tax rates on the same ¥650 base price. Its 16- and 24-piece family packs (¥1,393 and ¥2,041) are takeaway only.

Prices at the famous Osaka, Hiroshima and Akashi shops are not given here: the ones I checked either do not publish them online or do not say whether tax is included.

## Where to eat it

- **Osaka** — okonomiyaki and takoyaki. Osaka's tourism bureau says most okonomiyaki shops there cook it for you rather than having you cook it yourself, and that takoyaki is sold at stalls to eat on the street, turned with a pick and served in a bamboo boat.
- **Hiroshima** — layered okonomiyaki with noodles. Okonomimura, a building in the city, has 23 shops that all use the same brand of sauce, according to the city.
- **Akashi** — about 70 shops serving *tamagoyaki*, many around the Uonotana shopping street.

## The same name, a different plate

The best source on how eating habits differ between cities is a survey, and it is a company's: Otafuku, a sauce maker, surveyed 1,010 people aged 20–69 in Tokyo, Osaka and Hiroshima online in June 2023. Read it with that in mind.

- **Which okonomiyaki.** Asked to picture okonomiyaki, 76% in Osaka thought of the Kansai style and 76% in Hiroshima of the Hiroshima style; in Tokyo the largest answer, 38%, was "both".
- **Rice.** About 40% in Osaka eat it with rice. Hiroshima's prefectural researchers say that is rarely heard of there.
- **Cutting.** About half of Tokyo respondents cut it like a pizza; about half in Osaka and Hiroshima cut it into squares.
- **Sauce.** About 60% in Osaka and Hiroshima cover the whole surface; about half in Tokyo leave gaps, and 40% of Tokyo respondents use medium (*chūnō*) sauce.
- **When.** More people in Tokyo eat it as a snack or with drinks than in Osaka or Hiroshima.
- **The name.** "Akashi-yaki" is the national name. In Akashi it is *tamagoyaki*; the farm ministry says the other name spread after city staff used it to promote the dish around 1988.

## What people get wrong

- **"Osaka style is the original."** The sources do not agree on an original. Osaka's own early okonomiyaki were layered, by the farm ministry's account.
- **"Takoyaki is an old tradition."** Octopus in the batter dates to the early Shōwa period, and the shops spread from the mid-1950s.
- **"Akashi-yaki is takoyaki without sauce."** It has more egg, uses wheat starch, is cooked in copper, and is dipped in dashi.
- **"You cook it yourself."** In Osaka, the tourism bureau says most shops cook it for you.
- **"Eat-in and takeaway cost the same."** At Gindaco they do not, by a few yen, because of the tax rate.
