An *ekiben* — *eki*, station, and *bentō*, boxed meal — is a meal in a box sold at a railway station. It was made for the journey, and was once sold by vendors on the platform while the train stood in the station. That world has mostly gone: the trains got faster, the windows stopped opening, the station filled with other shops, and the trolley left Japan’s busiest shinkansen line in 2023. What is left is a regional food that most buyers now eat at home.

For ready-made food in Japan in general, start from the buying-food overview.

## Where it came from

Several stations claim the first ekiben, and the evidence does not settle it. The trade association of station caterers, the Nihon Tetsudō Kōnai Eigyō Chūōkai, sets out four accounts on its own site, taken from a 2000 book on ekiben:

- **Utsunomiya, 16 July 1885** — the common view. Sold on the Nippon Railway’s Tōhoku line by Saitō Kahei of the Shirakiya inn for five *sen*, and often said to have been two rice balls rolled in black sesame with pickled radish, in bamboo sheath. The association notes that its source says nothing about the contents.
- **Umeda (now Osaka station), 1877**, date unknown, on the government railway.
- **Kobe, July 1877**, on the government railway.
- **Ueno, 28 July 1883**, on the Nippon Railway.

JR West’s 2026 research report adds Kumagaya (28 July 1883) and Nagahama (1884). Records did not survive, it says — many makers lost their papers in wartime bombing — so it concludes only that the ekiben was born in the second decade of the Meiji era (roughly 1877–1886). The earliest confirmed use of the word *ekiben*, it says, is in a 1917 book. JR East and Tochigi prefecture both name Utsunomiya while adding that there are other theories; the Agriculture and Livestock Industries Corporation said in 2016 that there is no firm evidence. This site does not choose.

**The makunouchi box.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says that in 1889 a *makunouchi* ekiben, modelled on the boxed meals of the kabuki theatre, went on sale at Himeji station and spread nationwide. JR West’s report adds that it came in a thin wooden box with a paper cover (*kakegami*), said to be the origin of the printed wrappers ekiben still carry.

## What makes it an ekiben

**A member’s mark.** In 1988 the association created an *ekiben* mark, a registered trademark that only its members may put on the ekiben they make and sell, to promote trust in the product. The design is a wooden-box frame, a cross-shaped divider, a red circle and the word in kabuki-style lettering, with roman letters added, the association says, in the hope of making ekiben known abroad.

**A shrinking trade.** The association was founded in 1946. On 1 July 2023 it had 83 members across the six JR passenger railways. JR West’s report counts more than 400 ekiben makers at the peak, over 300 in 1987, about 100 in 2018 and about 80 now; the two counts are made differently.

**Made to be eaten cold.** *Aff* says ekiben are made to taste good at room temperature, and warns that polystyrene containers are generally not microwave-safe; some makers explain on their sites how to heat theirs. JNTO adds that some ekiben heat themselves.

**A day of its own.** The association made 10 April Ekiben Day in 1993, because 弁 looks like a 4 combined with 十 (ten), and 当 is read *tō*, which sounds like ten.

## How the station changed

JR West’s report traces three steps. The shinkansen (1964) shortened stops and cut platform sales, so on-board sales and advance orders took over. Faster express trains got sealed windows, so passengers could no longer buy from platform vendors. And with the breakup of Japanese National Railways in 1987, makers lost their exclusive right to sell in stations.

**The trolley.** JR Central ended trolley sales on Nozomi and Hikari trains on the Tokaido Shinkansen on 31 October 2023. It cited better shops around stations, more passengers bringing their own food, requests for a quieter train and future labour shortages. Since 1 November 2023, Green Car passengers have mobile ordering, ordinary cars have no on-board sales, and platform vending machines were expanded. I could not read JR Central’s current page, so later changes are not confirmed. On the Tohoku Shinkansen, JR East’s help page (updated 15 August 2024) says Hayabusa trains have on-board sales, with some exceptions, and Hayate, Yamabiko and Nasuno have none.

## Two boxes, and what they charge

Prices are tax included, as listed on the makers’ own sites on 10 October 2026.

- **Tōge no Kamameshi, Oginoya — ¥1,500.** First sold on 1 February 1958 at Yokokawa station; Oginoya says about 170 million have been sold. Tea-cooked rice with chicken, burdock, shiitake, bamboo shoot, quail egg, chestnut, apricot, green peas and red ginger, in a Mashiko-ware clay pot. *Aff* listed it at ¥1,000 in 2017; I have no source explaining the change.
- **Shiumai Bento, Kiyoken — ¥1,180.** Kiyoken began in 1908 inside the first Yokohama station and created this box in 1954, in its words to make an ekiben only Yokohama could offer. Five *shiumai*, rice with a pickled plum and black sesame, grilled marinated tuna, fried chicken, omelette and more. *Aff* listed it at ¥830 in 2017.

JNTO gives a range from under ¥1,000 to around ¥3,000 for boxes with wagyu.

## How to eat it

**On the right train.** JNTO says eating is generally accepted on long-distance trains such as the shinkansen, while short local trains are not suited to a meal.

**Where to buy it.** Large stations sell ekiben from several regions; small stations sell their local box at a window or counter (JNTO). JR West’s report lists permanent shops at major terminals that gather boxes from across Japan: Ekibenya Matsuri at Gransta Tokyo (JR East), Tabi-bentō Ekiben Nigiwai (JR West), PLUSTABento (JR Central) and JR Kyushu’s own.

**At a fair.** The first ekiben fair was at Osaka Takashimaya in 1953, the report says, and today’s "big three" are at Tsuruya in Kumamoto (from 1965), Keio in Tokyo (from 1966) and Hanshin in Osaka (from 2001). Keio’s 61st fair ran from 6 to 21 January 2026 in Shinjuku, with about 40 makers selling on site and more than 380 kinds, according to the store’s announcement on a press-release site.

## Not all of Japan’s ready-made food is this

- **Department store food floors**, often joined to stations, are counted by JR West’s report among the ekiben’s competitors.
- **Convenience stores** have their own overview page.
- **Supermarkets** count bento among their prepared food, 11.3% of their sales in 2025.

## Ekiben away from the station

For ekiben, away from home is the main story. From JR West’s report:

- **Why fairs began.** People who had moved to the cities wanted the taste of home when chilled food could not easily be sent.
- **Cooked on site, or shipped.** The fair’s signature is the maker cooking on the spot. Increasingly boxes are made in the maker’s kitchen and shipped (*yusō ekiben*) to fairs, supermarket events and the permanent station shops.
- **Eaten at home.** In the report’s 2026 survey of 6,388 recent buyers, 62.8% had eaten an ekiben at home and 36.0% on a train (more than one answer allowed). 17.8% said they bought because it was only available at a station or department store event.
- **Few buy at the home station.** For Kanagawa’s shūmai box and for *masu-zushi*, fewer than 40% of buyers bought at a station in the home prefecture.

Oginoya’s own timeline shows the same movement: shinkansen sales from 1997, after the Yokokawa–Karuizawa line closed; a shop in GINZA SIX in 2017, its first in Tokyo; then Yūrakuchō, Sasazuka and Kanda.

I could not find any official comparison of the same box at its home station and in Tokyo — price, contents, or whether cooked on site or shipped — so I do not say one is better or dearer. Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## What people get wrong

- **"Utsunomiya invented the ekiben in 1885."** It is the common view; six stations are named across the sources, and none is proven.
- **"Any boxed meal at a station is an ekiben."** The association’s ekiben mark is for its members’ boxes only.
- **"You can always buy one from the trolley."** Trolleys ended on the Tokaido Shinkansen in 2023.
- **"Heat it in the microwave."** Polystyrene containers are generally not microwave-safe, the farm ministry warns. Ekiben are made to be eaten cold.
- **"Ekiben are eaten on trains."** More recent buyers in JR West’s survey ate them at home.
