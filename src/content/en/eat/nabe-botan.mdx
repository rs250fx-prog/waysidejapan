Botan nabe is wild boar simmered in miso, with vegetables, and *botan* means peony. Tamba-Sasayama in Hyōgo calls itself the dish’s birthplace, and its tourism site tells the story in unusual detail: who first ate it at the inns, the year the name first appeared, and why the peony plating is not where the name came from. The farm ministry tells a slightly different version of the start. This page sets both out, then the rules for cooking wild meat.

For soups and hotpots in general, start from the [soups and hotpots overview](/en/eat/nabe/). For the rest of Hyōgo’s and Kinki’s local food, see [Kinki’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/).

## Where it came from

**Tamba-Sasayama’s account.** The city’s tourism site says the dish was first called *ino-nabe*, boar pot, and was food for entertaining guests. In 1908 the army’s 70th Infantry Regiment was stationed in Sasayama. Officers and visitors ate boar in miso soup at the town’s inns, and its reputation spread.

**The farm ministry’s account.** The ministry also dates the start to around 1908 (Meiji 41) and the same regiment, but puts it this way: boar caught during training went into miso soup, and was later taken to a *ryokan*, a traditional inn, and made into a miso hotpot.

The two agree on the year, the regiment and the inns. They differ on what came before: the tourism site has *ino-nabe* as a dish for guests already, while the ministry starts with soldiers’ boar in miso soup. This page does not choose.

**The name.** Both sources put the word "botan nabe" in the Shōwa era. The tourism site says it first appears in the lyrics of a folk song in 1931, and that *ino-nabe* did not sound good, so a five-syllable name was chosen. The ministry ties it to a 1931 call for lyrics for the *Sasayama Kouta*, a local song. The tourism site also mentions another theory, that in the Edo period boar was called *botan* as a way of not naming it.

**The peony.** It is common to hear that the dish is named after the way the slices of boar are laid out like a peony. The tourism site says this has it backwards: the name came first, and the peony-style plating dates from around 1945 or later.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs recognised botan nabe as a 100-year food in fiscal 2021, in the "modern" category, with a special award.

## What makes it what it is

**Wild boar.** The tourism site says the meat is wild boar, and that some shops cut it by hand. It notes that boar build up fat before winter. As a guide, it suggests 150–200 g of meat per person.

**Red and white miso.** The broth is miso. The mainstream is a blend of red and white miso, says the tourism site, and each shop has its own; the ministry describes the same mixed miso with boar and vegetables.

**Sansho, and something to finish.** The site mentions sansho pepper, and finishing the pot with udon or rice porridge.

## How to eat it

The farm ministry calls botan nabe a winter dish, made for New Year and for guests. The tourism site gives no season, beyond the note about boar putting on fat before winter.

**Cook the meat through.** Boar is wild game, and the official rules are the same for it everywhere in Japan:

- The farm ministry warns that eating raw or undercooked wild deer or boar carries a risk of food poisoning from hepatitis E virus, enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli* and parasites. Its advice is to cook the meat right through to the centre, and to disinfect utensils that touched it raw.
- The health ministry gives the figure: heat to 75°C at the centre for one minute or more. It calls eating wild game raw or underdone very dangerous, and says freezing does not make it safe.
- Restaurants and shops must buy meat butchered at facilities licensed for meat processing, the health ministry says.

There is more on why wild meat is treated this way on the [basashi page](/en/eat/meat-basashi/) and the [meat overview](/en/eat/meat/).

## Not all boar hotpot is this

Boar in miso is not only Sasayama’s. Mie’s tourism federation describes *sōhei-nabe* at Yunoyama Onsen, a hotpot the warrior monks of the temple there are said to have eaten for stamina, with mountain food and meat such as boar simmered in several misos. It is on [Kinki’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/). Botan nabe is the one with the peony name and the army story.

## Botan nabe away from its town

No public source compares eating botan nabe in Tamba-Sasayama with eating it in Tokyo or elsewhere on the same terms, and this page does not say which is better. What the sources show is how the town describes its own: the birthplace, wild boar, hand-cut meat at some shops, and a miso blend that differs from shop to shop. Wherever it is eaten, the rule on licensed meat applies.

None of the official sources used gave tax-included prices; the tourism site gives none in yen. Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"It is named for the peony plating."** The tourism site says the name came first; the plating dates from around 1945 or later.
- **"It is an ancient dish."** Both accounts start around 1908, and the name appears in 1931.
- **"There is one botan nabe miso."** It is mostly red and white blended, and each shop does it differently.
- **"Boar can be served pink."** The health ministry’s rule is 75°C at the centre for at least a minute.
- **"Freezing makes wild meat safe."** The health ministry says it does not.
