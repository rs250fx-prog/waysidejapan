Toyosu is the market that replaced Tsukiji, and it is not the Tsukiji of old photographs. It is a modern, enclosed wholesale market with a gallery for visitors, a restaurant and shopping area with fixed hours, and, next door, a separate commercial complex that opens every day. Knowing which is which decides whether your early start is worth it.

For how a Japanese wholesale market works, start from the markets overview.

## Where it came from

**Tsukiji, 1935.** Tokyo's market history records that the city's central wholesale market began trading at Tsukiji in 1935, absorbing the fish market at Nihonbashi and the vegetable market at Kyōbashi. It was the oldest of Tokyo's 11 central wholesale markets, handled fish and produce, ran 24 hours a day, and was, in the city's words, the centre of price-setting for fish.

**The move.** Because the buildings were ageing and cramped, the city decided in 1999 to relocate, and a 2001 plan named Toyosu as the site. Tsukiji closed on Wednesday 10 October 2018. Toyosu opened the next day, 11 October.

**What Toyosu was built to be.** Tokyo calls it the core market for the capital region "looking fifty years ahead", with Tsukiji's functions plus an enclosed building in which temperature can be controlled.

## How it works

The fish side, in Tokyo's own description: the wholesaler receives the catch, lays it out on the trading floor and runs the auction, which begins at about 5 a.m. Buyers — mostly intermediate wholesalers — signal their bids with their fingers, and the highest bid wins. The intermediate wholesaler then sells from its own stall to fishmongers, sushi shops and others, who sell to the public. Wholesalers and intermediate wholesalers both trade under a licence from the governor of Tokyo.

The farm ministry adds that auctions are used for expensive goods and goods of uneven quality, such as tuna, while other goods are priced by one-to-one negotiation.

## Inside the market: what visitors can do

Tokyo publishes the visitor arrangements. As of the date on this page:

- **Visitor route**, including the restaurant and shopping area: 05:00–17:00.
- **PR corner**: 08:30–14:30, Saturdays until 11:30.
- **Rooftop garden**: 05:00–23:00.
- **Closed** on Sundays, public holidays and market holidays: the visitor route, the PR corner, and the restaurants and shops inside. The rooftop garden keeps its normal hours even on market holidays. Tokyo points visitors to its market calendar for the holidays.
- **Shops keep their own hours**, and some close before 17:00.
- **No eating or drinking** on the visitor route, benches included.
- **No visitor parking**, for cars or bicycles.
- **You cannot buy in the wholesale halls.** Tokyo says the shops there sell mainly to supermarkets, retailers and restaurants, and ordinary customers cannot buy.

Watch for one-off restrictions. For the Tokyo Bay fireworks in 2026, Tokyo announced that from 14:00 on Saturday 24 October the visitor route and the restaurant and shopping area would be closed to new entry, with everyone out by 15:00, and that the rooftop garden would close from 23:00 the night before.

## Next door: Senkyaku Banrai

Senkyaku Banrai is a commercial complex of shops, restaurants and a foot-bath garden on the outer area next to the market. It was called Toyosu Jōgai Edomae Ichiba until 1 September 2025. The name is a registered trademark of its operator, Manyo Club, which says its aim is to carry on Tsukiji's bustle while working with the market itself.

The difference that matters to a visitor: its FAQ says it has no closing days and opens on market holidays. Most of its shops trade from around 10:00 to around 22:00, though the published table was not clear enough to give hours shop by shop. Tokyo's own page says its restaurants cook with ingredients chosen by the market's intermediate wholesalers, and shows a seafood bowl among them.

## Tsukiji's outer market

The shopping streets that grew up around the old market did not move. Tsukiji's outer market is run by a local merchants' association, and its own site says many of its shops close on the days the Tokyo central wholesale market is closed. So the market calendar still governs Tsukiji, even though the wholesale market has gone.

## Toyosu and the fishing-town markets

The comparison visitors most often make is with the morning markets of fishing towns. On the facts available:

- **Toyosu is where prices are made.** Fish arrives from across Japan and is auctioned to licensed buyers. Hakodate's morning market started in 1945 with farmers selling direct to the public.
- **Toyosu keeps market hours.** Its inner restaurants follow the market calendar and close on Sundays and holidays; Hakodate's morning market trades every day of the year from early morning.
- **The fish is someone's choice.** Tokyo says Senkyaku Banrai's kitchens use fish chosen by the market's own intermediate wholesalers. Fishing-town shops advertise local catch. Neither claim is a measure of quality, and I have found no official comparison.
- **No price comparison.** I could not get verified tax-included prices in either place.

## What people get wrong

- **"Toyosu is the new Tsukiji, so it works the same way."** The trading floors are enclosed and viewed from a gallery, and the restaurants inside keep market hours.
- **"I can go on Sunday."** The inner market is closed on Sundays, holidays and market holidays. Senkyaku Banrai next door is open.
- **"I can buy a fish to take home."** Not in the wholesale halls. Buy in the shopping area, next door, or at Tsukiji's outer market.
- **"Tsukiji closed, so there's nothing there."** The outer market's shops still trade, mostly on market days.
- **"Senkyaku Banrai is the market."** It is a privately run complex next to it, with different hours.
