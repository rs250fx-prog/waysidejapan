Japanese bakeries sell bread filled with sweet bean paste, bread filled with curry, and bread split open and stuffed with fried noodles. The best-documented starting point is a Ginza bakery in 1874. This page is short, because the record I could reach is short: one bakery’s history, the government’s labelling definitions, an industry body’s description of the market, and a household spending survey.

For Western foods made Japanese in general, start from the Western foods overview.

## Where it came from

**Anpan, Ginza.** Kimuraya Sōhonten’s official history says that in 1869 (Meiji 2) Kimura Yasubei (木村安兵衛) opened a shop called Bun’eidō in Shiba Hikagechō, and that in 1874 (Meiji 7) its shop in Ginza 4-chōme was completed and Yasubei devised and began selling the *sakadane* anpan. On 4 April 1875 (Meiji 8), with the backing of Yamaoka Tesshū, the shop presented sakadane cherry-blossom anpan to the Emperor Meiji at the Mito domain’s lower residence in Mukōjima. The "navel" of that bun held a salted double cherry blossom from Yoshino in Nara. Kimuraya says 4 April is Anpan Day. In 1900 (Meiji 33) the third-generation owner, Gishirō (儀四郎), began selling jam bread.

**Who devised it.** The Chūō ward tourism association’s map gives the same year and place (Ginza 4-5-7) but says it was the founder’s son, Eizaburō (英三郎), who devised the sakadane anpan. Kimuraya’s own history says Yasubei. This site gives both.

## What makes it Japanese

**The dough.** The tourism association describes *sakadane* as dough raised with a starter made from rice and kōji. Kimuraya calls its anpan a Japanese sweet bread, unlike Western bread.

**The legal names.** Japan’s food labelling standard, as set out by the Consumer Affairs Agency, divides bread three ways:

- **Shokupan** (食パン) — dough baked in a loaf tin, square or cylindrical.
- **Kashi-pan** (菓子パン) — other than shokupan, bread baked with bean paste, cream, jam and similar wrapped in, folded in or placed on top, or baked bread filled, sandwiched or spread with bean paste, jam, chocolate and so on.
- **Other bread** — everything else.

The labels allowed are "shokupan", "kashi-pan" and "pan" (and "cut bread" for dough rolled out, cut and shaped).

The Japan Bakers Association, an industry body of 19 member companies founded in 1963, gives examples. Kashi-pan: anpan, jam bread, the cream horn (*korone*), melon bread, the *koppepan* roll with jam or cream, and the croissant. Shokupan: square and domed loaves, raisin loaves, sandwich loaves. Other bread: rolls, French bread, bagels. It puts yakisoba bread, sandwiches and hamburgers under "prepared bread", a sausage roll under "savoury bread", and curry bread under fried doughnuts, as a savoury doughnut — because the bread definitions cover baked goods. The association describes kashi-pan as "bread with a highly Japanese taste" and shokupan as having spread as the nation’s staple. Those are its own words about its own industry.

**Savoury bread.** The Morinaga Angel Foundation’s course on Japanese bread says curry bread is regarded as starting in 1927 at Meikadō (名花堂, now Cattleya) in Kōtō ward, Tokyo. It says yakisoba bread may have started at Nozawaya in Minami-Senju, Arakawa ward, and croquette bread at Chōshiya in Higashi-Ginza — both phrased as "it is said" or "perhaps". It also names local breads: leek bread in Kumamoto, kelp bread in Toyama, salad bread in Shiga and potato-crisp bread in Yokosuka.

## The shops

I have no confirmed official prices for bread, so none are given.

The Japan Bakers Association, in a paper to the Consumer Affairs Agency dated 27 November 2024, describes the bread market in four parts:

- **Wholesale** — mostly large makers, with a growing share, and a rising share sold through drugstores.
- **Convenience stores** — a steady number of shops and a high share of own-brand products.
- **Bakeries** — 12,000 shops, including a good number of high-end ones.
- **Fast food and café chains** — demand growing, eaten in or taken away.

## How to read the label

Look for 食パン, 菓子パン or パン on the package. A bun with bean paste or cream is kashi-pan; a loaf baked in a tin is shokupan. Curry bread, being fried, is outside the bread definitions altogether.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Rice and bread

The government’s household survey (households of two or more, yearly spending per household) shows the gap narrowing. In 2002 households spent about 1.4 times as much on rice as on bread. In 2011 bread spending overtook rice. In 2012 rice was ahead again, ¥28,731 against ¥28,282, partly because rice prices rose. Across 2002–2012, bread stayed roughly level while rice fell.

I could not get the figures for the 2020s, so this page says nothing about where rice and bread stand now. Nor could I find a figure for how much bread Japan eats in total.

## Kimuraya away from Ginza

The Kimuraya official pages I read do not describe shops of the same name elsewhere or how their bread compares with Ginza’s, so I could not confirm any relationship or difference. I found no official source comparing anpan at the first shop with anpan elsewhere.

## What people get wrong

- **"Anpan was invented by Kimura Yasubei" — or "by his son".** Kimuraya says the father; the Chūō tourism association says the son. This site does not choose.
- **"Curry bread is a kashi-pan."** In the industry body’s table it is a fried, savoury doughnut.
- **"Shokupan just means sliced white bread."** In the labelling standard it means bread baked in a loaf tin.
- **"Japan now eats more bread than rice."** The survey figures I could reach show bread spending passing rice in 2011 and rice ahead again in 2012. They measure money spent, not amounts eaten, and I could not get recent years.
