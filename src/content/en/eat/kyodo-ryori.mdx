*Kyōdo ryōri* — "home-region cooking" — is how Japan describes the dishes that belong to one place: Akita's rice-stick hotpot, Okinawa's slow-braised pork. Much of this site is about such dishes. This page explains how Japan itself records them, because there are two official lists, run by two different agencies on different principles, and they do not always agree on what counts. The lineage pages cover Akita and Okinawa as examples.

## The farm ministry's database

**What it is.** *Uchi no Kyōdo Ryōri* ("our local dishes — flavours to pass on") is a database of dishes chosen region by region, with their history, recipes and background, meant for home cooking, restaurant menus, products and research. The ministry opened it in 2019, as part of passing on regional food culture — a feature of *washoku*, registered as UNESCO intangible heritage in 2013 — in the face of changing diets and households.

**The definition.** Dishes protected and handed down by a community, made in the area with locally obtained ingredients. Each region selects its own. Ingredients need not be local any more; distribution brings them from elsewhere.

**The criteria.** Required: (1) uses locally available ingredients; (2) has historical, cultural or customary features, or features rooted in climate and landscape; (3) is made and handed down at home or in the community. As an exception, a dish that meets none of these but should be preserved for history may be included, up to one or two per list. Recommended: popularity and affection locally; reasonable balance across the prefecture; recipes adapted for today; and new value such as restaurant menus.

**How it is organised.** By prefecture (47 pages), season, type and syllabary. Each prefecture has a different number of dishes — Akita 29, Okinawa 30 when I counted — and each dish page gives the main area, ingredients, history, occasions, method and the local source it was drawn from, such as an Akita rural women's group's book. I could not find the national total.

**An earlier list.** Akita prefecture records that the ministry's "100 Local Dishes of Farming, Mountain and Fishing Villages" were announced on 18 December 2007, with kiritanpo hotpot and Inaniwa udon chosen from Akita, and Yokote yakisoba in a separate "popular local dishes" list. The ministry's own page for that list could not be opened, so I give neither its total nor its criteria.

## The Cultural Agency's "100-year foods"

**What it is.** A scheme naming food cultures handed down across generations, and working with local governments and groups to keep them alive.

**The criteria** (all three): (1) a distinctive local character rooted in climate, history or custom — nationally uniform ingredients and processed foods do not qualify; (2) handed down across at least two generations in a community and still alive — one person's or one shop's dish is not enough; (3) a group that declares it will carry it on beyond 100 years.

**Three categories.** *Traditional* (local dishes from the Edo period), *modern* (born in the Meiji and Taishō eras), *future* (aiming for 100 years).

**How it is chosen.** Local governments, councils, food groups, tourism bodies and private groups apply — not individuals — one entry each. Documents are checked and an expert committee certifies; the best receive a special award. The agency's criteria page says 329 have been certified; its top page says 250 in one place and over 300 in another.

## Two lists, two ideas

The farm ministry's database is chosen by each region, as a record of what is cooked at home. The 100-year foods are applied for by groups and certified by experts, with a category for foods that are still young. So Yokote yakisoba, born soon after the war, is a "future" 100-year food and a "popular local dish" in the 2007 list, but the ministry's database criteria describe something older and home-made. I found no official definition of *B-kyū gurume*, "B-grade" local fast food, so I do not draw the line more sharply than the sources do.

## Eating local dishes away from home

Prefectures run shops in Tokyo to sell their food:

- **Akita's Akita Bisaikan**, in Takanawa near Shinagawa, is set up by the prefecture and run by a contractor. The prefecture lends space in and in front of it, in principle free, to towns, farm and business groups and companies for promotion and trade talks. It sells pickles, fish sauce, sweets, sake and crafts, and its 74-seat restaurant serves Inaniwa udon, kiritanpo, hatahata and Hinai chicken, 11:00–23:00, closed on New Year's Day.
- **Okinawa's Ginza Washita Shop**, in the Tokyo Kōtsū Kaikan in Yūrakuchō, is run by the prefecture's trading company, with about 4,000 products, 10:00–20:00, and an eat-in corner (10:30–18:45). The company lists 10 shops nationwide.

What changes in Tokyo, on the facts available: the season (a Tokyo restaurant serves winter hotpots all year), the occasion (home, guests and ceremonies at the source), and the form (retort pouches and gift sets to take away). No official source compares the taste.

## What people get wrong

- **"Local dish means very old."** The 100-year scheme has a category for foods still growing up.
- **"There is one official list."** There are two, run on different principles, plus a 2007 selection.
- **"The ministry decides."** In its database, each region chooses its own dishes.
- **"Local dishes use only local ingredients."** The ministry says ingredients may now come from elsewhere.
