Japan eats a lot of food that came from somewhere else and stayed long enough to change. Tonkatsu and the beef and katsu bowls have their own pages. This page is about three smaller groups that are easy to overlook as Japanese food — spaghetti and pizza, the hamburger, and milk — and about what the written record shows of how each one arrived and what changed. The lineage pages go deeper on each.

The record is uneven. For some of these foods there is a hotel’s own history, a company timeline or a government statistic. For others there is only what gets repeated, and where I could not reach a primary source I have said so rather than fill the gap.

## What it is

Three groups, each with a different way in:

- **Spaghetti and pizza.** The Japan Pasta Association says pasta was first made in Japan around 1883, when a French missionary, Father de Rotz (マリク・マリ・ド・ロ in the association’s Japanese), built a brick macaroni works in Sotome, in what is now Nagasaki city — "it is said", in the association’s words. The best-known Japanese spaghetti dish, *Napolitan*, is credited by the Hotel New Grand in Yokohama to its own kitchen after the postwar occupation. Pizza, on the record I could reach, enters through a delivery chain in 1985.
- **The hamburger.** Sasebo city’s tourism site says shops there began making hamburgers around 1950 from recipes heard directly from the US naval base. McDonald’s Japan opened its first restaurant in Ginza in 1971, and MOS Burger its first shop in Narimasu, Tokyo, in 1972.
- **Milk.** The dairy board J-Milk says Tokyo’s first milk shop opened in Akasaka in 1870, at first selling only to foreigners, and that the new Meiji government campaigned for meat and milk from its first years.

## Why it is the way it is

The pattern in all three is the same: a foreign food arrives through one door, and then Japanese makers adjust it until it fits.

**Pasta was blended to Japanese taste.** The pasta association says domestic production grew from the early Shōwa period, but on small batch machines, so pasta was something you ate at a few restaurants. It became ordinary only after fully automatic machines were imported, from the Shōwa 30s onward; Shōwa 30, that is 1955, is called the "first year of pasta". The domestic pasta of that time blended several kinds of flour to suit Japanese taste and texture. Durum semolina-only domestic pasta reached home kitchens from about 1986, which the association links to more travel abroad and a boom in Italian restaurants.

**Napolitan was a hotel chef’s answer to a soldier’s plate.** The Hotel New Grand says that while the hotel was requisitioned by the occupation (1945–52), its staff saw American soldiers eating boiled spaghetti with salt, pepper and ketchup, and that after the requisition ended, the second head chef, Irie Shigetada, arranged it into a dish of his own. The hotel’s version is made with a sauce of garlic and onion cooked slowly, fresh and canned tomatoes, tomato paste, bay leaf and olive oil.

**The chains added Japanese flavours.** The clearest evidence of adaptation is on company timelines. Domino’s Pizza Japan lists a tuna pizza developed in Japan (1986), its first Japanese-style pizza, charcoal-grilled teriyaki chicken (1992), and a mayonnaise-and-potato pizza (1994). MOS Burger launched a teriyaki burger in 1973 and a rice burger in 1987. McDonald’s Japan launched its teriyaki burger in 1989 and calls it a Japan-original product.

**Milk was promoted.** J-Milk’s history describes a government campaign for meat and milk, a pamphlet written by Fukuzawa Yukichi in 1870 praising milk as a medicine for many ills, and a newspaper report in 1871 that the 19-year-old Emperor Meiji drank milk twice a day. It also says older people were reported to dislike animal milk. Milk, along with the beef hotpot, became a symbol of the new civilisation.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Pizza’s early history is missing.** Stories about the first pizza restaurant in Japan circulate, but I could not reach a primary source for any of them. The record here starts with Domino’s in 1985, and "first" is the company’s own word.
- **Sasebo defines its burger by place.** The city’s tourism site lists conditions for a Sasebo burger: made after the order is taken, from a company with its head office in Sasebo, using fresh vegetables and an original sauce.
- **Milk is split by region.** Hokkaido produces most of the country’s raw milk and sends most of it to dairy products rather than drinking milk; other prefectures produce mainly for drinking.

## Where to eat it

- **Yokohama** — the Hotel New Grand’s Napolitan. Yokohama city’s tourism site says the original can be had at the hotel’s café, The Café.
- **Sasebo** — the city tourism site’s burger shops, including the two it names as the origins of the jumbo burger and the bacon-and-egg burger.
- **Anywhere** — the national chains’ Japan-only menus: teriyaki and rice burgers, and Domino’s Japanese pizzas.
- **Hokkaido** — where most of the milk comes from, though the farm ministry and J-Milk figures are about production, not about where to drink it.

## The same name, a different place

- **Napolitan in the hotel and in the coffee house.** The hotel’s own sauce is built on tomatoes and tomato paste, while the hotel says the idea came from soldiers’ ketchup. How the hotel dish relates to the ketchup Napolitan of Japan’s coffee houses, I could not find in any official source. The page on coffee houses and shaved ice mentions the café Napolitan too, and stops at the same point.
- **Sasebo burgers away from Sasebo.** The tourism site’s rule about a Sasebo head office is a way of telling a Sasebo burger from one sold under the name elsewhere. It does not describe how the burgers differ in practice.
- **American burgers and Japanese ones.** Neither chain’s official history compares its menu with the United States. What can be said is when the Japan-only items appeared.
- **Hokkaido milk and Tokyo milk.** J-Milk describes milk and raw milk being shipped from Hokkaido, Tōhoku and Kyushu to Kanto and Kansai, mostly by sea. I found no official source on whether it tastes different when it gets there. The farm ministry says that when a place of origin is in a product name, such as "Hokkaido milk", the product uses 100% raw milk from that place.

## What people get wrong

- **"Napolitan comes from Naples."** Yokohama city’s tourism site calls it a pasta born in Yokohama, not Naples, and the hotel credits its own chef. Other origin stories exist, but I could not confirm any from an official or shop source, so none is given here.
- **"The original Napolitan has no ketchup."** The hotel’s page describes a tomato sauce, and also says the idea came from soldiers eating spaghetti with ketchup. I found no source for the "no ketchup" claim.
- **"Pasta came to Japan after the war."** The pasta association dates the first macaroni works to around 1883, and Japanese-made macaroni to the Taishō period. What came after the war was mass production.
- **"Teriyaki burgers are a McDonald’s invention."** On the two companies’ own timelines, MOS was selling one 16 years earlier. Whether anyone came before MOS, I did not check.
- **"Japanese people have always drunk milk."** It was a Meiji-era campaign, and J-Milk says older people at the time were reported to dislike it.
