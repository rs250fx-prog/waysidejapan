Ishikari nabe is salmon in a miso hotpot: chunks of the fish and its trimmings, vegetables, kombu stock, miso, and a pinch of sansho pepper at the end. It comes from the mouth of the Ishikari river in Hokkaido, where salmon fishing thrived, and its story has two stages. First it was what fishermen ate. Then it was what they served to the people who came to watch them fish, and that is when it got its name.

For soups and hotpots in general, start from the [soups and hotpots overview](/en/eat/nabe/). For the fish itself, see [salmon](/en/eat/seafood-salmon/), and for the rest of Hokkaido’s local food, [Hokkaido’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokkaido/).

## Where it came from

Two official accounts tell the story, and they fit together.

**The farm ministry.** It calls Ishikari nabe a fishermen’s dish born in the town of Ishikari, at the mouth of the Ishikari river, where salmon fishing had thrived since the Edo period. To celebrate a big catch, fishermen are said to have put chunks of fresh salmon and its trimmings into a pot of miso soup. In the Shōwa 20s (1945–54), Ishikari’s beach-seine fishing for salmon drew visitors. The dish was served to them while they waited, it became popular, and that is said to be how it spread across Japan.

**The Agency for Cultural Affairs.** Its 100-year food list, which recognised Ishikari nabe in fiscal 2023 in the "future" category with a special award, adds two details. The dish was once called *dainabe*, food eaten in the breaks between fishing work. And the name Ishikari nabe was given after the war, when it was named and served for visitors. The agency also notes that cabbage and some of the other vegetables in it spread in Hokkaido only after the Meiji era, so the pot reflects the settling of the island.

Who made it first, or which shop served it first, is not in either official source, and this page does not name one.

## What makes it what it is

**Salmon, fresh, and all of it.** The farm ministry describes chunks of fresh salmon together with its trimmings, the *ara*, with vegetables.

**Kombu stock and miso.** The broth is kombu stock, seasoned with miso. The ministry’s recipe uses a blended miso.

**Sansho at the end.** A sprinkle of sansho pepper finishes the pot.

**Different in every home.** What else goes in varies by household, the ministry says. Some homes put salmon roe on top; some add butter for flavour.

**Not sanpei-jiru.** The ministry draws this line itself. *Sanpei-jiru*, eaten across Hokkaido, is salted salmon or herring simmered with carrot, daikon and other vegetables, seasoned only by the salt in the fish. Ishikari nabe is miso, and fresh salmon. The two should not be confused.

## How to eat it

It is a winter staple at home, and it follows the salmon, which runs from autumn into winter. The ministry notes that several shops in the Ishikari district are known for it.

Ishikari city launched an "Ishikari nabe revival project" in 2007 and made 15 September Ishikari Nabe Day, timed to the salmon catch and chosen as a play on the numbers.

The same year, the farm ministry’s page on jingisukan notes, Ishikari nabe was chosen with jingisukan and salmon *chanchan-yaki* as one of the "100 local dishes of farming, mountain and fishing villages".

## Not all of Hokkaido’s salmon is this

Ishikari is also home to salmon *chanchan-yaki*, salmon and vegetables steam-grilled in butter and seasoned with miso. Sanpei-jiru, above, is the salted-fish dish of ordinary winter days. Frozen, thinly sliced *ruibe* is another way with the same fish. All three are on [Hokkaido’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokkaido/). Ishikari nabe is the one built around fresh salmon in a pot of miso.

Japan’s other miso hotpots stand on their own. Botan nabe in Tamba-Sasayama is wild boar in miso; chanko nabe in Tokyo is chicken stock seasoned with soy sauce or miso.

## Ishikari nabe away from its town

No public source compares eating Ishikari nabe in Ishikari with eating it elsewhere on the same terms, and this page does not say which is better. What the sources do show is a difference of season: at home, the dish follows the salmon from autumn into winter, and a shop that serves it all year offers it outside that season. In the Ishikari district, the ministry says, several shops are known for it.

None of the official sources used gave tax-included prices, so there are none here. Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ishikari nabe is an old name."** The Agency for Cultural Affairs says it was named after the war, for visitors; before that it was *dainabe*.
- **"It is the same as sanpei-jiru."** Ishikari nabe is miso and fresh salmon; sanpei-jiru is salted fish and its own salt.
- **"There is one recipe."** The ministry says each home differs, and some add salmon roe or butter.
- **"It began as a restaurant dish."** It began as fishermen’s food, celebrating a catch, and spread after it was served to visitors watching the beach-seine fishing.
- **"Everything in it is traditional."** The agency notes that cabbage and some other vegetables arrived only after the Meiji era.
