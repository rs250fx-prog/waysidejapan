faq:
  - q: Is Utsunomiya gyoza on the farm ministry's list of Tochigi dishes?
    a: No. Utsunomiya gyoza is not in the farm ministry's list. It is a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2021, future). The ministry's Tochigi story does mention gyoza, saying soldiers sent to China came to know them and made them again at home, while the 100-year food page says officers and men of the 14th Division brought the method back from Manchuria after the war. This page gives both.
  - q: Can I eat pond snails in miso at Iiyama Onsen?
    a: Do not count on it. The farm ministry calls tanishi no miso-ni a local dish of Iiyama, a hot-spring village in Atsugi known as Tokyo's back parlour, but it also says local pond snails have become hard to get and the inns around the springs no longer serve it. It belongs to the spa's past.
  - q: Why is namerō called namerō?
    a: The farm ministry gives two meanings side by side. It is said to be so good you lick (nameru) the plate, and it is also so sticky that it clings to the plate and you have to lick it off. This page gives both.
  - q: Where does the name kenchin-jiru come from?
    a: The farm ministry gives two theories. One is that it comes from kenchan, a dish of Chinese-style temple cooking, and the other is that it is a corruption of Kenchō-jiru, the soup of Kenchō-ji temple in Kamakura. The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page follows the temple story. This page does not choose.

Kantō is the plain around Tokyo: Ibaraki, Tochigi and Gunma to the north, Saitama and Chiba on either side of the capital, Tokyo itself, and Kanagawa to the south-west. The official descriptions of its dishes keep returning to the same few things — wheat on volcanic-ash uplands, soy sauce from Noda and Chōshi, and the feeding of Edo. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Ibaraki

**Ankō no tomozu.** Boiled monkfish dipped in vinegared miso mixed with the fish's own liver, from the north of the prefecture. The farm ministry says monkfish is eaten elsewhere too, but the *tomozu* dip is particular to Ibaraki, and that in the Edo period the Mito domain is said to have presented monkfish as a gift. One theory says *dobu-jiru*, monkfish cooked in miso without water, is named for the liver clouding the broth like a ditch (*dobu*). *Ankō-nabe*, monkfish hotpot, is not in the farm ministry's list.

**Soboro nattō.** Leftover nattō and dried daikon strips steeped in salt or soy sauce for about a month, eaten with rice or drink. For why Mito's nattō beans are small, see [tofu and nattō](/en/eat/rice-sides-tofu-natto/).

**Tsuke-kenchin.** A vegetable soup with a separate basket of thick soba to dip in it. The ministry says Ibaraki has grown soba since the Edo period and is fourth in Japan for the crop, after Hokkaido, Nagano and Tochigi. Eating soba at the old-calendar New Year, around today's Setsubun, is said to have spread from the Mito domain.

**Hoshi-imo.** Dried sweet potato, dried in strong sea winds around Hitachinaka, Tōkai and Naka. The ministry says Ibaraki has the top share of production and that the method began in Shizuoka; the Cultural Agency, which made it a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, modern), says it reached Ibaraki in the late Meiji period. The two do not conflict.

**Unagi no hobiki-ni.** Eel from around Lake Kasumigaura and the Tone river, kept frozen for unexpected guests and dressed with bamboo leaves to look like the lake's sail-trawling boats. The ministry adds that Ushikunuma marsh is "also said" to be the birthplace of the eel bowl. See [Kantō-style eel](/en/eat/unagi-kanto/).

### Tochigi

**Shimotsukare.** Made from leftovers — the head of the New Year salted salmon, the roasted soybeans from Setsubun — with daikon, carrot, fried tofu and sake lees. The farm ministry says it was once made only as an offering to the Inari shrine on the first Day of the Horse in the second month of the old calendar, with a taboo against making it at other times. Leftovers are not fit offerings, so making the dish only for the gods is thought to have turned it into something special, the ministry says.

- **The name.** Ibaraki's page, where it is also listed, gives several theories: from "the customary dish of Shimotsuke province", hence *shimotsukare*, or from *su-mi-zuke*, hence *sumitsukare*. This page gives both.
- **The custom.** It is shared with neighbours, and eating it from seven houses is said to keep illness away.
- **The taste.** Tochigi's page says it smells less when cold; Ibaraki's says opinion is divided.

It is also a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, traditional).

**Mimi udon.** Ear-shaped pasta from Sano and Utsunomiya, made at the year's end for New Year guests. In Sano people hold the "ears" to their own and wish to hear good news through the year.

**Kanpyō.** Dried gourd strips, cooked with egg or rolled in nori for outings and sports days. The ministry's pages give Tochigi's share of national output differently — more than 90 per cent on one, more than 99 per cent on another. Growing is told to have begun in 1712, when Torii Tadahide, lord of Mibu, sent to Ōmi for seeds.

**Agemaki yuba.** Fresh soy-milk skin (written 湯波 in Nikko), rolled, sliced, fried and simmered. The ministry says monks brought it as temple food and that it is used in offerings at the spring and autumn grand festivals of Nikko Tōshōgū.

**Utsunomiya gyoza.** Not in the farm ministry's list; it is a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, future). The ministry's Tochigi story says soldiers sent to China came to know gyoza and made them at home; the 100-year food page says officers and men of the 14th Division brought the method back from Manchuria after the war. This page does not choose. See [Utsunomiya gyoza](/en/eat/gyoza-utsunomiya/).

### Gunma

**Okirikomi.** Wide noodles, unsalted and not boiled first, simmered with vegetables. The name is said to come from cutting into (*kirikomi*) dough rolled round the pin. In 2014 "Gunma's flour-food culture, okirikomi" was selected as a prefectural intangible folk cultural property for recording. The ministry ties Gunma's wheat to well-drained soil and the dry winter wind off the Niigata border.

**Gunma's udon.** Three kinds in one entry: Kiryū's very wide *himokawa*, Tatebayashi's *kama-tama* made with "hundred-year wheat", and the glossy Mizusawa udon of Shibukawa, usually eaten cold. The ministry says they "are said to be" among Japan's three great udon. Tatebayashi's and Kiryū's udon are both 100-year foods.

**Yaki-manjū.** Buns on a skewer, brushed with sweet miso and grilled. Said to begin with a "miso-dipped bun" made in Maebashi at the end of the Edo period. Sold at cherry-blossom parties, summer festivals and the Maebashi New Year market.

**Sauce katsudon.** From Kiryū, Maebashi and Isesaki. The ministry says the first is said to have been an eel restaurant, and that Kiryū's weavers ordered it as a takeaway that tasted good cold. See [katsudon](/en/eat/donburi-katsudon/).

### Saitama

**Udon.** The ministry gives Saitama second place for udon production (2009 figures), with wheat grown as the second crop after rice, and counts more than 20 kinds in the prefecture. In summer it is dipped in *hiyashiru*, called *suttate* in Kawajima: sesame, miso, cucumber, shiso and myōga ground in a mortar and thinned with cold water.

**Nibōtō.** Wide noodles and vegetables simmered without boiling first, around Fukaya. The ministry says it differs from Yamanashi's [hōtō](/en/eat/udon-hoto/). It is known as a favourite of the Fukaya-born businessman Shibusawa Eiichi, and a "Nibōto-kai" is held on 11 November, the anniversary of his death. A 100-year food (fiscal 2022, traditional).

**Jelly fry and fry, in Gyōda.** Jelly fry is an oval cake of okara and potato. It is said to have been devised by a Gyōda teahouse owner who served in the Russo-Japanese War. *Fry* is not fried at all but a thin flour pancake, popular with the women of the *tabi*-sock factories. For its name the ministry gives several theories — "cloth coming" (布来), the frying pan, and a pun on "come, wealth". This page gives them all. Together they are a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, modern).

**Shōten-zushi.** From Menuma in Kumagaya, a town that grew rich on Tone river shipping. Three *inari-zushi* and four slices of rolled sushi make one serving; until the 1955–64 period, farmers brought rice to exchange for it. The 100-year food "Menuma inari-zushi" (fiscal 2022, traditional) comes from the same district under another name.

### Chiba

**Futomaki-zushi.** Thick rolls whose cut face shows a picture, made at home in Kazusa and Shimōsa for festivals, celebrations, weddings and funerals. The ministry dates it back to around the Kansei era (1789–1801) and says some trace it to *mehari-zushi*, the lunch of Kishū fishermen. Before the war men holding honorary village posts made it; afterwards women took over and it grew showier. A 100-year food as "futomaki matsuri-zushi" (fiscal 2021, traditional, special award).

**Namerō and sanga-yaki.** From the Bōsō coast. The ministry says fishermen devised namerō to prepare fish on a rolling boat, and used miso because soy sauce would spill in rough seas. *Sanga-yaki* was namerō packed into an abalone shell, carried into the hills and grilled in a mountain hut; in old Chiba dialect "X's house" was "X-ga", so *san-ga* is the mountain house. Both belong to the 100-year food "Minami-Bōsō horse-mackerel culture" (fiscal 2023, traditional).

**Boiled peanuts.** Chiba grows more than 70 per cent of Japan's peanuts. The ministry says the Kantō loam, ash from Mt Fuji and the Hakone volcano, suits the crop, and that growing began with a trial in 1876 in Sanbu district. Boiled peanuts can only be made from freshly dug ones.

### Tokyo

**Fukagawa-meshi.** Spring onion and raw clams simmered in miso and poured over rice, in Kōtō ward. The ministry says Fukagawa's fishermen gave up their rights in the 1955–64 period, and restaurants revived the dish when visitors began coming to places such as the Bashō museum.

**Edo zōni.** The New Year soup was once made with miso, the ministry says, but when soy brewing took off at Noda and Chōshi in the Genroku era, it became a clear soup of the dark soy Edo liked. For square rice cakes it gives several explanations. See [New Year food](/en/eat/seasonal-new-year/).

**Dojō-jiru and Yanagawa-nabe.** Loach, in Asakusa, Komagata and Takabashi, whole, gutted, or cooked with egg as Yanagawa-nabe. Two theories for the name — earthenware pots from Yanagawa in Kyushu, or a small restaurant in Nihonbashi or Asakusa's Senzoku village. The ministry also says the shop name *dozeu* dates from after the great fire of 1806, when four kana were thought unlucky and three preferred.

**Shima-zushi.** From Hachijōjima and the Ogasawara islands: sliced fish marinated in a soy-based sauce and pressed onto vinegared rice, at first so the fish would keep on boats. Without wasabi, islanders used mustard paste or soy with *shimatō*, island chilli. The ministry's Tokyo story says Edomae sushi's *zuke* came from the Izu islands; see [Edomae sushi](/en/eat/sushi-edomae/).

Tokyo's list of 30 also has tsukudani, *nigiri-zushi*, soba, eel, *monjayaki* and oden. See [pickles and tsukudani](/en/eat/rice-sides-tsukemono/), [Edo soba](/en/eat/soba-edo/), [monjayaki](/en/eat/konamono-monja/) and [oden](/en/eat/nabe-oden/).

### Kanagawa

**Sanmāmen.** A Yokohama noodle soup topped with stir-fried bean sprouts, Chinese cabbage and pork in a thickened sauce. The ministry says there are several theories about the characters, one being 生馬麺, "noodles topped with fresh, crisp ingredients", and also that Chinatown's meat noodles were too dear before the war, so a vegetable version was made as staff food. The 100-year food page (fiscal 2021, future) says *san* and *mā* are Cantonese readings.

**Nama-shirasu-don.** Raw whitebait on rice, from the Shōnan coast, eaten only on the day of the catch. Fishing is closed from 1 January to 10 March.

**Odawara kamaboko.** Steamed fish paste on a board, made by the Muromachi period and flourishing in the late Edo period. A 100-year food (fiscal 2021, traditional, special award).

**Maguro kabuto-yaki.** A tuna head roasted whole, at Misaki port. Ocean fishermen once hung heads they could not sell from the ship's funnel to steam-roast them; restaurants began serving it about 40 years ago.

**Kenchin-jiru.** Kamakura's vegetable soup, eaten at Kenchō-ji for more than 700 years, the ministry says. The name has two theories (see the questions above). A 100-year food (fiscal 2025, traditional, special award).

**Wakasagi no kanroni.** Lake Ashi smelt, grilled plain, then simmered and glazed with syrup, in Hakone. Smelt eggs were brought from Kasumigaura in 1918.

Kanagawa's list also has Ōyama tofu, Yokosuka navy curry, *gyūnabe* and *shūmai*. Iekei ramen is not in the farm ministry's list; see [iekei ramen](/en/eat/ramen-iekei/).

## What makes it Kantō

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as counted for this page: Ibaraki 30, Tochigi 30, Gunma 27, Saitama 29, Chiba 28, Tokyo 30, Kanagawa 23 — 197 in all. Both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages were counted, and the counts matched. No official source checked for this page explains why Gunma and Kanagawa have fewer.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as counted for this page: Ibaraki 5, Tochigi 4, Gunma 5, Saitama 7, Chiba 4, Tokyo 4, Kanagawa 6 — 35 in all. Some are on both lists, among them shimotsukare, sauce katsudon, yaki-manjū, nibōtō, futomaki, sanmāmen and Odawara kamaboko. Others are only 100-year foods, among them Utsunomiya gyoza, Tokyo's *sakura-nabe* horse-meat hotpot, Sōka rice crackers, Sayama tea and Katsuura *tantanmen*. The two lists are chosen separately.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: ankō-nabe, Utsunomiya gyoza, iekei ramen and Sōka rice crackers. Mizusawa udon sits inside "Gunma's udon"; konnyaku, kanpyō and peanuts appear only as dishes made from them. That is all this says.

## What the seven share

**Volcanic ash and wheat.** The ministry's stories place Ibaraki, Tochigi, Saitama and Chiba on the Kantō plain and its loam, and tie Chiba's peanuts and Tochigi's gourds to the ash soil. Flour food runs through them too: Gunma, Saitama, southern Tochigi, and Tokyo's *noshikomi* udon in Okutama and Musashino.

**Soy from Noda and Chōshi.** The ministry's Chiba story says Noda began making *tamari* in the Eiroku era (1558–1569), and that in Chōshi, Tanaka Genba began brewing in 1616 and shipped soy to Edo by river boat. Its Tokyo story says that as soy production grew, *zuke* took hold in Edo and raised the value of tuna. See [soy sauce by region](/en/eat/seasoning-shoyu/).

**Feeding Edo, and the Tone river.** When Tokugawa Ieyasu set up his government in Edo, demand for fish leapt, the Chiba story says, and Kansai fishermen were brought in to establish sardine fishing at Chōshi and Kujūkuri. Ieyasu also turned the Tone, which once ran into Tokyo Bay, towards the Pacific, and the Tone and Edo rivers became the main routes for goods.

No official source checked for this page describes Kantō as one food culture; the sources speak prefecture by prefecture.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the official pages describe them:

- **Monkfish**: coastal restaurants and inns, presented by the prefecture and its tourism association as a winter tradition.
- **Soboro nattō**: supermarkets, and Mito's nattō shops as a souvenir.
- **Shimotsukare**: restaurants, roadside stations and supermarkets across Tochigi.
- **Yaki-manjū**: festival stalls, and boxed sets to cook at home.
- **Fukagawa-meshi**: mostly restaurants now.
- **Maguro kabuto-yaki**: seafood restaurants around Misaki port, by reservation only.

**Prices.** No official source checked for this page gives a dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** Monkfish from November to March, best December to February. Shimotsukare for the first Day of the Horse in the second lunar month. Mimi udon at the year's end and New Year. Futomaki at festivals and celebrations, shima-zushi at Setsubun and family occasions, Edo zōni at New Year. Boiled peanuts from September to late November. Raw whitebait from 11 March to 31 December. Lake Ashi smelt from about August, at its peak around November, with fishing closed from mid-December to February.

## Not all of Kantō is this

**One soup, many recipes.** Shimotsukare changes with the place: salmon head, sake lees and more in central Tochigi and along the lower Kinugawa, but only daikon and soybeans in some areas, among them eastern Saitama and northern Chiba.

**One noodle, many names.** Unsalted wide noodles simmered raw are *okirikomi* in Gunma, *nibōtō* in Fukaya, *himokawa* in Iruma and *okkirikomi* in Chichibu. The ministry says such noodles are everyday food in Saitama, Yamanashi and Nagano as well as Gunma.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Iiyama Onsen and pond snails, in the past.** The farm ministry places *tanishi no miso-ni*, pond snails simmered in miso, around Iiyama Onsen in Atsugi, a spa village it calls Tokyo's back parlour. It is one of the few dishes the ministry ties to a hot spring. But it also says local snails have become hard to get and the inns around the springs no longer serve it. It was the spa's dish; this page cannot say you can eat it there now.

**Ikaho and Mizusawa udon.** The Shibukawa Ikaho tourism association's Ikaho food page lists a shop on the stone steps described as a sister of one in the Mizusawa udon street, as well as shops at Mizusawa. So you can eat Mizusawa udon at Ikaho. The ministry places the udon in Shibukawa without mentioning the spa, so this page does not call it Ikaho's dish.

**Hakone and Lake Ashi smelt.** The ministry says the smelt is served by local inns (*ryokan*) and restaurants during the season, and its Kanagawa story puts Hakone's hot springs and the smelt in one section. The dish page does not use the word "onsen", so it is a dish you may meet at a Hakone inn, not a hot-spring speciality. See [Hakone](/en/soak/hakone/).

**Kusatsu and runner beans.** Simmered *hana-ingen*, large runner beans, are on Gunma's farm ministry list, and Kusatsu's tourism association also mentions the bean. What the two share, and what they do not, is set out on the [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/) page.

**Nikko, Kinugawa and Nasu.** The ministry's Tochigi story names Nasu, Shiobara and Kinugawa among its hot springs, and Nikko's yuba in the same section, but nothing ties yuba to a spring. See [Kinugawa](/en/soak/kinugawa/) and [Nasu](/en/soak/nasu/).

**No link found.** No official source checked for this page names a dish together with Daigo Onsen, the springs of Chichibu, Chiba's seaside springs or Okutama. Katsuura city says its *tantanmen* took hold as a dish for fishermen and divers to warm up after the sea, but names no spring. For [Shima](/en/soak/shima/), [Minakami](/en/soak/minakami/) and [Yugawara](/en/soak/yugawara/), later checks also found no official source tying a dish to the spring. Atami is in Shizuoka; see [Atami](/en/soak/atami/).

**Getting there.** No official journey times were found for this page. For routes to Kusatsu, Hakone, Kinugawa and Nasu, see their pages.

## Kantō food away from its home

- **Tochigi.** Tochimaru Shop, fourth floor of Tokyo Solamachi's East Yard in Tokyo Skytree Town (1-1-2 Oshiage, Sumida-ku), open 10:00–21:00, says its site.
- **Ibaraki.** IBARAKI sense, 1-2-1 Ginza, about 3 minutes' walk from the Kyōbashi exit of JR Yūrakuchō station; shop 10:30–20:00. A December 2023 prefectural article captions a photo of its "popular" monkfish hotpot.
- **Gunma.** A 2018 prefectural bulletin announced Gunma-chan-chi's move to 7-10-5 Ginza.
- **Chiba.** A limited-time shop every year since fiscal 2022; in 2025 it was in Hankyū Osaka-Umeda station, 27 August to 15 September.
- **Kanagawa.** Kanagawa-ya is in Yokohama, not Tokyo: second basement of Sogo Yokohama (2-18-1 Takashima, Nishi-ku), 10:00–20:00, renewed on 7 August 2025.

**What changes.** At the source these dishes have seasons and settings — monkfish in winter, raw whitebait only on the day it is landed, shimotsukare for an offering, futomaki for festivals. The shops open all year.

No official source checked for this page says how Gunma-chan-chi runs in 2026, gives a permanent Tokyo shop for Saitama or Chiba, or compares taste, price or portion between the source and the shops, and this page makes no such comparison.

## What people get wrong

- **"Utsunomiya gyoza is Tochigi's official local dish."** Utsunomiya gyoza is not in the farm ministry's list; it is a 100-year food.
- **"Ankō-nabe is Ibaraki's listed dish."** Ankō-nabe is not in the farm ministry's list; ankō no tomozu is.
- **"You can still eat pond snails at Iiyama Onsen."** The ministry says the inns no longer serve them.
- **"Nibōtō is just hōtō."** The ministry says it differs from Yamanashi's hōtō.
- **"Kanagawa's shop is in Tokyo."** It is in Yokohama.
