*Korokke* is the Japanese croquette, coated and deep-fried. It did not start as an everyday food. It arrived as a Western recipe printed in a women’s magazine, and it took a cheap potato, a butcher’s idea and a popular song to make it ordinary.

For B-grade local food and town fried food in general, start from the B-grade overview.

## Where it came from

The account here is mostly from the research journal of the Kikkoman food culture research centre, a soy sauce maker’s research institute, with one of its exhibition panels and a farm ministry page on tempura.

**A French croquette in a magazine.** The original is the French *croquette*. The Kikkoman journal says recipes were introduced in women’s magazines around the time of the Sino-Japanese War, and that the 5 December 1895 issue of *Jokan* gave one: *shiba* shrimp bound with butter, flour and milk, coated and fried in beef fat. Other introductions of the time used mashed potato or minced meat.

**Meat by hand.** In the Meiji era there were no mincers, the journal notes; meat was chopped fine with a kitchen knife.

**Potato or shrimp first?** The same institute’s exhibition panel on Western food says korokke was a potato korokke from the start, and that “korokke” is the name of a Western dish. It does not give a Meiji-era origin. So between the early magazine style — shrimp or meat, bound with butter, flour and milk — and the potato korokke, the sources I opened do not settle which came first. This site does not choose.

## What made it cheap

Two changes, as the Kikkoman journal tells it:

- **The butchers.** Butchers took meat scraps that did not sell and leftover fat, and began selling a potato-based *imo korokke* (potato korokke). The journal says only that this was “afterwards”, with no date.
- **The potato.** Around 1907 (Meiji 40), an American variety, the Irish Cobbler, spread under the name *danshaku-imo*, “baron potato”. Potato growing rose sharply, and potatoes became the cheapest ingredient for Western cooking.

The same year, by the journal’s account, mincers came into use and minced-meat dishes such as the *menchi* ball went on sale, and a breadcrumb maker started up; before that, households crushed bread to make their own crumbs.

**The sauce came with it.** The journal also says butchers gave away sauce with their korokke and cutlets, and that this helped the sauce spread. The tonkatsu overview covers that sauce from the cutlet side.

## The song that named it

Around 1918 (Taishō 7) the *Korokke no Uta*, “The Korokke Song”, was a great hit. Its writer was Masuda Tarōkaja, eldest son of Baron Masuda of the Mitsui business group, and its subject is a newly married household served nothing but korokke. Both Kikkoman sources say that this song is what spread the word “korokke” — the name of a Western dish most people did not know — across Japan.

The journal adds a detail that changes how the song reads. To a very rich family like the Masudas, korokke was the simplest Western dish. To ordinary households, a proper korokke was still a laborious, high-class food.

There are further stories about the song’s first performance. I found them only in search summaries, so they are not here. The lyrics are not reproduced.

**How popular it became.** In a 1922 (Taishō 11) survey at a Tokyo infantry regiment, the soldiers’ favourite dishes were, first, *furai* (fried dishes), second, the cutlet, and third, korokke.

## The older street fry: tempura

Korokke is not the first fried food to move between the street and the restaurant. The farm ministry’s Tokyo entry on tempura and the Kikkoman journal tell that story in two versions.

**The farm ministry.** Tempura is said to trace back to Portuguese-influenced *nanban* cooking in the mid-sixteenth century, and to have spread in the Edo period after rapeseed oil production increased. In Edo, a new city, standing at stalls became a popular way for ordinary people to eat out, and the invention of the *shichirin* charcoal brazier helped. The oil did not drain well, so tempura was skewered and eaten with grated daikon. In the later Edo period the frying and the ingredients were refined, and crisp tempura developed as a high-class dish; from Meiji on, restaurants and tempura specialists established that status. After the Great Kantō Earthquake, cooks who had lost their work moved around the country and spread Edo-style tempura. The ministry also notes that in Edo and in Kamigata (the Kyoto–Osaka region) “tempura” meant different things — battered seafood in Edo, fish paste in Kamigata — and that Edo-style tempura was fresh Edo seafood fried in sesame oil and eaten as it came out of the pan.

**The Kikkoman journal.** It describes tempura as Japan’s first Western dish, reaching Nagasaki on the *nanban* ships in the sixteenth century and Kyoto early in the Tokugawa period. Osaka’s *tsukeage*, fish fried in sesame oil, reached Edo around the end of the eighteenth century, was called tempura, and became a star of street food; tempura appears in a list of stalls from the An’ei era. Cheap small fish and shellfish from Edo Bay were the ingredients, and people crowded around. *Ozashiki* tempura, served in a room rather than at a stall, appears only in the Taishō era, the journal says.

The two disagree on when tempura moved up: the ministry from the later Edo period, the journal from Taishō. This site gives both. Tempura on rice, as *tendon*, is covered in the donburi overview.

## A town that defined its own: Mishima korokke

Shizuoka prefecture describes a korokke created by a local government. In 2008, at the urging of Mishima city office, the town linked its local brand vegetable, the Mishima potato (a May Queen variety), with the everyday korokke, and *Mishima korokke* was born. A Mishima Korokke association was set up that July.

The definition is short. The prefecture says the only condition for certification is using certified Mishima potatoes. The filling, the shape and the way it is served are up to each shop.

The association entered the B-1 Grand Prix in 2009 and 2010 (the latter held in Atsugi) and placed in both years; the prefecture does not give the ranking. Two prefecture pages give very different figures for the number of certified shops, so I have not used either.

## Not all korokke is the butcher’s

The sources show at least three kinds of korokke with different histories: the magazine recipe with shrimp or meat; the butcher’s potato korokke made from scraps; and a defined town brand like Mishima’s, where only the potato is fixed. In Nagasaki some shops add a korokke to Toruko rice, a one-plate dish covered in the B-grade overview. For the other everyday fries, menchi and ham cutlet (*hamukatsu*), I could not find an official history.

## Korokke away from home

I could not find an official source comparing butchers’ korokke with restaurant korokke, or korokke in the potato-growing regions with korokke in the cities. For Mishima, the definition itself means there is no single Mishima taste to compare with korokke elsewhere; the prefecture gives no comparison.

## What people get wrong

- **“Korokke was always a cheap snack.”** It began as a magazine recipe with shrimp, butter and milk, and ordinary households still saw a proper korokke as laborious around 1918.
- **“Korokke is the same as a French croquette.”** The croquette is the original; the potato-heavy butcher’s korokke is the Japanese turn.
- **“The first korokke was potato.”** One Kikkoman source suggests so, another cites an 1895 shrimp recipe. Unsettled.
- **“Mishima korokke has a recipe.”** Only the potato is defined.
- **“Tempura was always restaurant food.”** In Edo it was stall food; the sources differ on when it became high-class.
