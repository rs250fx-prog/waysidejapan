In most of Japan, katsudon is a pork cutlet simmered in broth, set with egg and laid on rice. In Fukui and in Komagane, Nagano, katsudon is something else: a cutlet dipped in Worcestershire-type sauce and laid on rice, no egg. Both towns say theirs is the original. So do several people in Tokyo. This page sets out the claims, the local rules, and what to say when you order.

For rice bowls generally, start from the donburi overview.

## Where it came from

A Waseda University feature collects the claims, all from the Waseda district of Tokyo, and does not pretend to settle them.

- **Sauce, 1913 — Yōroppa-ken.** Takabatake Masutarō, from Fukui, trained for six years in Germany, presented a sauce katsudon at a cooking demonstration in 1913, and on 28 November that year opened Yōroppa-ken in Waseda Tsurumaki-chō. His grandson, the third owner, says the founder never told him he was the originator; he learned it from a magazine interview. The shop is said to have moved to Yokosuka in 1917 and later left Waseda.
- **Egg, 1918 — Sanchōan.** At this soba shop in Waseda's Babashita-chō, the story is that founder Katō Asajirō had cutlets left over from a banquet, and on a student's suggestion simmered them in soba broth and set them in egg. The evidence is the second owner's book and family tradition; the feature points out that the university history it is often linked to mentions the shop but not katsudon.
- **Sauce, 1921 — Nakanishi Keijirō.** In February 1921 an 18-year-old Waseda student is said to have put a cutlet on rice in a bowl at a café near Anahachiman shrine and poured over a thick sauce of Worcestershire and flour. The sources are his own 1967 article in a Waseda history bulletin, a 1981 letter to the Asahi newspaper and a 1983 book.
- **A witness.** Takeuchi Yoshitarō recalled that around 1917 a diner in Tsurumaki-chō served katsudon "with sauce or broth", no egg — and, elsewhere, that he ate one with egg at another shop near Anahachiman.

The feature itself leans towards Yōroppa-ken. That is its judgement. The Komagane association cites the two sauce claims and says the first katsudon was seasoned with sauce; it does not mention the egg claim.

## What makes each kind

**Egg (the common katsudon).** Cutlet set in egg on rice. Yamanashi calls this *ni-katsudon* and treats it as a separate dish.

**Fukui sauce katsudon.** Fukui's tourism association: thin slices of pork in fine breadcrumbs, fried, dipped in sauce while hot and laid on rice. Usually no cabbage — just rice, cutlet and sauce. It credits Takabatake: he returned from Germany in 1912, adapted German-style Worcestershire sauce to Japanese taste, showed it in Tokyo in 1913, and later went home to Fukui, where the taste has been passed down. How exactly it moved from Waseda to Fukui, the sources I have do not say. The association adds a local rite of passage: Fukui people leaving the prefecture are surprised to be served katsudon with egg.

**Komagane sauce katsudon.** The local association's page — read here from a page whose security certificate did not match, so treat details as the association's own — describes shredded cabbage on rice, topped with a cutlet dipped in a house sauce. It says it has been served in the city since the beginning of Shōwa and that in Komagane "katsudon" means this. Restaurant owners formed the association in 1993. Its rules for customers:

- served in a *donburi*;
- pork loin as standard, at least 120 g;
- coated in breadcrumbs and fried;
- cabbage finely shredded, soaked in water and drained, laid on the rice;
- fried in oil kept clean of crumbs;
- the association's sauce as the minimum standard;
- dip then cut, or cut then dip, either is fine;
- no nori or vegetables other than cabbage;
- a lid is optional.

"Komagane sauce katsudon" is a registered regional collective trademark (no. 5942880), held, according to the patent information service, by the Komagane chamber of commerce. The association names Ichinose Shōichi, who opened the café Kiraku, as the pioneer, but its own page gives his start as 1928 in one place and around 1936 in another, from interviews rather than documents.

**Yamanashi.** Cutlet on shredded cabbage, sauce added before eating.

**Niigata.** *Tare-katsudon*: cutlets dipped in sweet soy tare, two or more to a bowl, no egg; said to date from the early Shōwa era.

## How to order it

- **In Fukui**, plain *katsudon* is sauce katsudon. For egg, ask for *jō-katsudon* or *tamago-katsudon*. Yōroppa-ken's cutlets spill over the bowl, and the tourism association says Fukui people put one or two on the lid to eat. Grill Yamada offers small, medium and large.
- **In Komagane**, *katsudon* is sauce katsudon. For egg, ask for *ni-katsudon* or *tamago-toji katsudon*.
- **Elsewhere**, *katsudon* means egg; look for *sōsu katsudon* on the menu.

## Not all of Japan's cutlet bowls are this

- **Fukui** also has Ōno's soy-sauce katsudon, with grated radish (from 2010), and Takefu's *Volga rice*, whose origin is disputed.
- **The Komagane association** sorts other towns as it sees them: Fukui and Kiryū with thin sauce and no cabbage, Aizu-Wakamatsu with cabbage and a thicker sauce. That is its classification, not checked here.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

Fukui city for Yōroppa-ken and its sauce katsudon; Komagane for the association's shops. Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Katsudon away from home

- **The word changes meaning.** The same order brings egg in Tokyo, sauce in Fukui and Komagane. Fukui's tourism association calls the surprise of the egg version outside Fukui a rite of passage.
- **The source protects its version.** Komagane has rules and a trademark; Fukui has a shop that has kept its founder's sauce.
- **The story leads back to Tokyo.** Yōroppa-ken was founded in Waseda in 1913, not in Fukui.
- **A chain gives you both.** At Katsuya you choose egg or sauce, same price.

## What people get wrong

- **"Katsudon means egg."** Not in Fukui or Komagane, and not in Yamanashi's local version or Niigata's.
- **"Someone definitely invented it."** Three claims, from 1913, 1918 and 1921, all from Waseda; none is settled.
- **"Fukui and Komagane serve the same thing."** Fukui's has no cabbage and thin cutlets; Komagane's sits on cabbage, with a 120 g minimum.
- **"Sauce katsudon is a regional novelty."** Two of the three origin claims are sauce versions.
