A Japanese breakfast is not a plate but a tray of small bowls: fish, pickles, rice and soup, first thing in the morning. It is the clearest everyday example of the shape the farm ministry calls the basis of Japanese meals — rice, soup and several small dishes — and it is served from ryokan to station chains at very different prices.

For the set-meal shape in general, start from the set-meal overview.

## What it is

**JNTO's description.** Japan's national tourism organisation says a ryokan breakfast is a combination of several small dishes rather than one large plate. The typical tray: rice and miso soup, with fish, pickles, tofu, vegetables, nori and an egg dish.

**Tokyo's description.** Tokyo's tourism site says a Japanese breakfast often comes with white rice, miso soup and grilled fish; that staying at a ryokan almost guarantees one; and that many hotels serve one too, with a Western breakfast available at most.

**The shape behind it.** The farm ministry's *ichijū sansai* — rice, soup, a main and two sides, with pickles alongside the rice — overlaps with the ryokan tray. I have not found an official source saying the ryokan breakfast *is* ichijū sansai, so I say only that the two share a shape.

**How to sit to it.** The ministry's layout: rice on the left, soup on the right, the main dish behind the soup, a side behind the rice, small bowls between. A whole fish lies head to the left.

## Where to find it, and what it costs

- **A ryokan.** Breakfast usually comes with the room. I have not found an official breakdown of what it adds to the price, or an industry definition of what it must include.
- **A Tokyo hotel buffet.** The Tokyo Station Hotel's Atrium serves a Japanese and Western buffet of over 100 items, 06:30–11:00 (last order 10:30): adults ¥6,400, children 7–12 ¥3,200, under 7 free, tax and service included. It is for hotel guests only. Its lobby lounge serves Western sets to anyone for ¥4,800–5,700.
- **A hotel set.** The Imperial Hotel offers a Japanese breakfast at its Nadaman restaurant, with grilled fish or cold roast beef as the main, alongside a Western and Japanese buffet. Its page does not make clear whether tax and service are included for these, so I give no price.
- **A chain.** Yayoi-ken and Yoshinoya sell simplified versions — rice, soup, and fish, egg or nattō — for ¥430–640, tax included. At Yayoi-ken rice refills are free.

## How to eat it

- **Small amounts, many bowls.** It is meant to be eaten across the tray, not dish by dish.
- **At a buffet, take what you will finish**, JNTO says.
- **Rice first in mind.** The farm ministry's guidebook says the soup and dishes are there to eat the rice with.

## The same breakfast, a different place

- **Ryokan, hotel, chain.** The same shape — rice, soup, fish, pickles, egg — runs from a guest-only Tokyo hotel buffet at ¥6,400 to a ¥580 grilled-salmon breakfast at Yayoi-ken. The difference is the number of dishes and the setting, not the idea.
- **Tokyo's tourism site leans Western.** Its breakfast guide recommends pancakes, homemade toast and family-restaurant eggs and sausage alongside the Japanese option.
- **Nagoya has its own morning.** See the café morning page.

## What people get wrong

- **"A Japanese breakfast is one big plate."** It is many small dishes.
- **"Every hotel serves only Japanese food."** Most also serve Western breakfast, Tokyo's tourism site says.
- **"It is always expensive."** The chain version is a few hundred yen.
- **"The ryokan breakfast is defined by tradition."** It shares the ministry's shape, but I found no official definition of what it must contain.
