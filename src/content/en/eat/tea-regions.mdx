Three of the four tea regions on this page tell a founding story about a Buddhist monk. What actually separates the regions is less romantic and more useful to know: each built its name on one technique — shading, finishing, or steaming — and the map of where the most tea is grown has quietly moved on from the names on the packets. This page covers four regions with a clear identity: Uji and Nishio for matcha, Yame for gyokuro, Shizuoka for sencha.

For tea in general — the kinds, how the leaf is priced, and why bottled tea overtook the teapot — start from the tea overview.

## Where it came from

Each region tells its own origin. They are stories about different places, not rival claims to one beginning, and each is given here as the region's own account.

- **Uji (Kyoto).** The farm ministry says tea in Japan is held to begin with the monk Eisai bringing tea back from Song China in 1191. The monk Myōe is said to have planted it at Kōzan-ji temple in Toganoo, Kyoto, from where it was later planted out in Uji. In the 15th century the tea won the regard of the Ashikaga shoguns, and gardens were laid out in Uji with the shogunate's encouragement; one of them, the Okunoyama tea garden, survives. Kyoto prefecture's tea ordinance is more cautious: it says only that tea growing in Kyoto is said to have begun in the Kamakura period.
- **Nishio (Aichi).** Nishio city's draft matcha ordinance of 2021 says tea there began when Shōichi Kokushi, the founder of Jissō-ji temple, built in Bun'ei 8 (1271), sowed the first seeds in its grounds. Production became serious in the Meiji era, and from the late Taishō era tencha, the leaf for matcha, became the main crop. The city speaks of 750 years of history. This is the city's account; I did not find a second source for it, and I could not confirm whether the draft ordinance was passed.
- **Yame (Fukuoka).** Fukuoka prefecture's magazine says Yame tea is held to begin in 1423, when the Zen monk Eirin Shūzui, back from China, founded Reigan-ji temple with the help of the village headman Matsuo Tarogorō Hisaie, and passed on the seeds and the methods he had brought.
- **Shizuoka.** I could not find how tea came to Shizuoka in the prefecture's own documents, so this page does not give a story.

Two later steps in Uji shaped what all of Japan drinks. In the 16th century, the ministry says, shade growing was developed to meet the demand of the flourishing tea ceremony, producing matcha. And in 1738 Nagatani Sōen is said to have devised the method of steaming new shoots and rolling them by hand over a heated drying table, inspired by the pan-fired tea brought by the monk Ingen; the ministry calls this the source of later Japanese tea making. Kyoto's ordinance gives no year, saying only that sencha by the Uji method, and then gyokuro, began in the Edo period.

The top three prefectures hold about 70% of the land under tea. Kagoshima produced the most crude tea in 2024 and 2025, a first since records began. Aichi, home of Nishio, is not in the ministry's table. The variety *Yabukita* covers 64% of tea land nationally, 86% in Shizuoka, 49% in Kyoto and 28% in Kagoshima, where *Yutakamidori* (26%) and *Saemidori* (16%) are also grown.

So the image and the volume do not line up. The names people associate with matcha are Uji and Nishio; the largest tencha producer is Kagoshima.

### Uji: defined by its finishing

The ministry's definition of Uji tea is worth reading closely: green tea made from leaf grown in Kyoto, Nara, Shiga and Mie prefectures, finished by firms in Kyoto prefecture using methods that come from the Uji area. The growing area crosses prefectural lines. What fixes the name is where and how the tea is finished.

The ministry describes the growing land along the Uji and Kizu rivers as fertile, well drained, rainy, and sloping, with large swings between day and night temperatures. The first picking, from late April to late May, goes mainly to tencha and gyokuro; the second as well as the first are used for sencha. Each stage is now mechanised, but traditional Uji tea is still rolled by hand. Shaded fields here are covered with straw or similar material to keep out the sun, which, in Kyoto's explanation, keeps the leaf high in theanine and low in astringent catechins. Gyokuro is shaded for 20 days or more.

Kyoto prefecture's share is small: 2,280 tonnes of crude tea in 2025, about 3% of the national total.

### Nishio: a town that turned to tencha

Nishio describes itself as producing matcha (tencha) in quantities among the highest in Japan, but none of the city documents I read gives a number. The Nishio tea cooperative says its members hold to traditional methods such as shading on overhead frames and grinding with stone mills, and that the tea fields spread out from the Inariyama tea garden in the hills on the left bank of the Yahagi River. The city's tourism association calls Nishio "the kingdom of matcha", without a figure.

In 2009, Nishio's matcha became the first regional brand in Japan limited to matcha to be accepted under the patent office's regional collective trademark system. The city's draft ordinance says matcha is an everyday thing for its residents, and that school pupils pick tea from early to late May.

### Yame: gyokuro shaded by hand

Fukuoka prefecture describes gyokuro as shaded from about 20 days before picking. The traditional kind, *Yame dentō hon-gyokuro*, is covered for about 20 days with straw laid on frames built over the bushes. The prefecture stresses that the shade is not put on at once but in at least two stages, and that too early or too late is no good. The bushes are pruned only once a year and left to grow naturally, and the leaf is picked by hand. In 2015 it became the first Japanese tea registered as a geographical indication (GI).

The prefecture describes the area along the Yabe River as having moderate rain, big day–night temperature swings and frequent mist. Sencha is grown mostly on flat land and low hills, gyokuro in the hill country. Fukuoka prefecture as a whole is third in Japan for gyokuro; figures for Yame alone I could not find.

### Shizuoka: the most tea land, and deep steaming

Shizuoka has more land under tea than any other prefecture but, in 2024 and 2025, produced less crude tea than Kagoshima. Its tea is mainly sencha, above all deep-steamed sencha.

The prefecture's 2020 plan explains the scale. In the 1960s and 1970s, with rapid economic growth driving demand, shared tea factories and large processing machines were built and Shizuoka became the leading producer. By 1985 the variety Yabukita covered 70% of its tea land; in 2025 it was 86%. The prefecture also described itself, in 2020, as the market where 55% of Japan's tea gathers. It says its people drink about twice the national average.

**Deep steaming.** The ministry's registration of Fukamushi Kikugawa-cha as a geographical indication (31 March 2023, No. 130) tells the story. Tea from around Kikugawa had been considered too astringent. Research from around 1952 found that steaming the leaf for a long time made a better-tasting tea, and Kikugawa became known as the birthplace of deep-steamed tea. It set up a tea farmers' cooperative ahead of the rest of the country and prompted the national tea competition to add a deep-steamed sencha category. The ministry describes the brewed tea as deep yellow-green and mellow. The registration page does not give a steaming time; the "two to three times" figure comes from the ministry's general description of deep-steamed sencha.

## The tea ceremony behind the matcha

Urasenke, one of the schools of the tea ceremony, gives its history on its official site. Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591) brought the tea ceremony to completion. The house later divided: the third head, Sōtan, handed the main house to his third son, Kōshin Sōsa, and his fourth son, Sensō Sōshitsu, took over the retirement house — the beginning of Urasenke — while his second son, Ichiō Sōshu, founded Kankyūan. The three lines are called Omotesenke, Urasenke and Mushakōjisenke, together the *san-Senke*. The eleventh head of Urasenke, Gengensai, devised *ryūrei*, a form of the ceremony using tables and chairs.

How thick and thin tea are used and how the schools' procedures differ, I could not confirm from the schools' own pages, so this page does not describe them.

## Not all of Japan's tea is this

- **Kagoshima** grows the most crude tea and the most tencha, with a mix of varieties unlike Shizuoka's.
- **Mie** is first for gyokuro and kabusecha, and its leaf can become Uji tea under the ministry's definition.
- **Miyazaki, Kumamoto and Saitama** appear in the ministry's list of leading prefectures by tea area.

## Regional tea away from its region

**Tea is finished where it is sold.** The ministry describes leaf processed into crude tea near the field and finished and blended in the consuming areas. "Uji tea" is a blend of up to four prefectures' leaf by definition, and Shizuoka is a market as well as a grower. A tea bought in a tea town is not necessarily that town's leaf alone.

**The home cities buy more leaf.** In the household survey (two-or-more-person households, 2023–25 average), Shizuoka city bought 1,215 g of leaf green tea a year and Kyoto 1,030 g, against 647 g nationally and 571 g in central Tokyo. Fukuoka bought 946 g. That is home buying only, and it does not say why.

**Prices I could not compare.** I found no official, tax-included prices for the same tea in a region and in Tokyo.

## What people get wrong

- **"The best matcha all comes from Uji."** This site does not rank tea. And by volume, Kagoshima grows the most tencha.
- **"Uji tea is grown in Uji."** It can be grown in four prefectures; the name follows the finishing.
- **"Shizuoka grows the most tea."** It has the most land under tea. Kagoshima produced more in 2024 and 2025.
- **"The famous regions are the big ones."** Kyoto prefecture made about 3% of Japan's crude tea in 2025; Aichi is not in the ministry's table of leading prefectures at all.
- **"Tea came to Japan once."** Each region has its own founding monk and its own date. They are local accounts, and this site does not choose between them.
