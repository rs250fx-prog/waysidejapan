This page is about mackerel, bonito and tuna. What the official sources say about them is mostly not about taste but about method: how a fish is caught and bought, how it is seared, which part was once thrown to the cheap end of the menu, and how much of it Japan is allowed to catch. Raw cuts, parasites and vinegared mackerel are on the sashimi overview, saba-zushi and the mackerel road to Kyoto are on the saba sushi page, and the auction system is on the market overview; this page does not repeat them. For seafood beyond sushi and sashimi in general, start from the seafood overview.

## Mackerel

**Seki saba.** Oita prefecture’s fisheries plan for the Saganoseki area says the Saganoseki Peninsula and Ehime’s Sadamisaki are only 13.9 km apart, and the strait between them is called Hayasui no Seto. The current is fast, and line fishing makes up 79% of the area’s catch. *Seki aji* and *Seki saba* are its brand fish. The plan describes the handling: the fish are caught on single lines without scattering bait, kept alive in a holding tank on the way back, and bought by *tsura-gai* — the buyer sets a price by eye without touching the fish. Line fishing, tsura-gai and *ikejime* together are what the plan calls distinctive.

**Heshiko.** The farm ministry’s traditional food guide says heshiko is mackerel gutted, washed, salted, then pickled in rice bran for several months to about a year. It is put down in spring when mackerel are caught, or from autumn into winter when new rice bran is available. It is said to have begun in the Wakasa area as protein for winter, when boats could not go out, and to have been made by the mid-Edo period. There are two explanations of the name: from *heshikomu*, pressing into the barrel, or from a corruption of *hishio*, the liquid that comes out during salting. This site does not choose. In Fukui, the ministry says, the Reinan area mostly makes mackerel heshiko, while Reihoku, said to be influenced by Ishikawa, mostly makes sardine *konuka-zuke*.

## Bonito

**The catch.** Kochi prefecture says almost all of its bonito fishing is pole-and-line, one fish at a time. From March to November the boats follow bonito migrating from south of Okinawa past Tosa, Bōsō and Sanriku; distant-water and offshore boats stay out for about ten months. Kochi’s production in 2011 was 18,390 t, about 7% of Japan’s. On the 2010–2012 average, annual bonito consumption per household in Kochi city was 5,163 g, the most in Japan.

**Tataki, two ways.** The two official accounts differ, and I give both.

- **Kochi prefecture:** fillet the fish, split it at the dark meat into back and belly blocks, sprinkle with salt and pat it in by hand. Sear it over a straw or *kaya* grass fire so that only the surface cooks and the inside stays nearly raw. Put it in cold water straight away, drain, and eat with leek, ginger, shiso and garlic in *nihaizu*, two-part vinegar.
- **Farm ministry:** at home the block is seared quickly, not cooled in ice water, patted with salt and sliced while still warm. The whole surface, not just the skin, is seared over a strong fire of straw or similar; too long and it becomes grilled fish, too short and it smells fishy.

**The dressing differs by region.** The farm ministry says central Kochi uses nihaizu of soy and brewed vinegar; the west mainly soy mixed with mirin and similar; the east soy cut with yuzu juice; the mountain areas *sanbaizu*. Sliced garlic is essential, which the ministry explains as old wisdom about its antibacterial effect. Tataki is a staple of *sawachi* platters and indispensable at weddings, funerals and shrine rites. More on the garlic and the origin stories is on the sashimi overview.

**Yaizu and Makurazaki.** Yaizu city calls itself first in Japan for bonito landings, without giving a year or a figure. Makurazaki’s katsuobushi is covered on the dashi overview.

## Tuna

**The parts, as one buyer describes them.** In a 2017 farm ministry magazine article, a fish buyer says lean *akami* is best a deep red in bluefin and a bright red in bigeye, and that pieces with fewer visible sinews feel better in the mouth. *Chūtoro* should be a deep pink in bluefin and a pale pink in bigeye, with fine-grained fat. The article does not describe *ōtoro*; it mentions it only in passing, calling bonito belly skin "the ōtoro part, if it were tuna". I could not find an official source that says which part of the body each cut comes from.

**Fat.** The Japan Fisheries Association, an industry body, quotes a buyer at the wholesale level: of two fish of the same length, the rounder and heavier is fatter; fat tends to be higher in winter than summer; in frozen fish more fat means softer ice, so a hand hook goes in easily. Ōtoro is the most expensive part, and rough handling can split it.

**Two meanings of akami.** The same association defines a red-fleshed fish as one with 10 mg or more of pigment protein per 100 g, citing a 1976 source; below that it is white-fleshed. Tuna and bonito are red-fleshed fish that swim long distances. So *akami* means both a class of fish, by composition rather than colour, and a cut of tuna.

**Toro was once the cheap end.** The farm ministry says the fatty belly now called toro was, in the Edo period, a low-grade food that could not be served to guests, and was usually cooked because it spoiled fast. *Negima-nabe*, simmered with leek and soy sauce, is said to have come from finding a way to eat it.

**The catch rules.** The fisheries agency says Japan has tightened management since 2010 under agreements of the Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission (WCPFC), and since January 2015 has halved the catch of bluefin under 30 kg from the 2002–2004 annual average. Its March 2024 document gives the figures:

- Fish under 30 kg: half the 2002–04 average; for Japan, 8,015 t to 4,007 t.
- Fish of 30 kg and over: 115% of the 2002–04 average after a 15% rise agreed in December 2021; for Japan, 4,882 t to 5,614 t.
- From 2024, up to 30% of the small-fish quota (previously 10%) may be moved to the large-fish side; for Japan, about 1,202 t, counted at 1.47 times.

Citing the 2022 ISC assessment, the same document says some bluefin mature at three and all by five, growing from about 0.2 kg at age 0 to about 85 kg at age 5. By number, most of the catch is young fish: age 0 is 56.7% and age 1 29.6% (2011–2020 average). In 2021 the total Pacific bluefin catch was 14,964 t, of which Japan took 8,529 t.

**The ports.**

- **Yaizu** (Yaizu and Ogawa ports): in 2025 about ¥46.9 billion landed, first in Japan by value, and 117,214 t, fourth by volume, according to the city.
- **Shizuoka prefecture**, using the fisheries agency’s 2025 distribution survey, is first in Japan for frozen southern bluefin (93.3%), albacore (70.4%), bigeye (64.6%) and yellowfin (59.0%) landed through wholesale markets. These are market landings, not catches.
- **Misaki** (Miura city): the market opened in 1922. Since 2018 frozen tuna, mainly bigeye, has been traded at the city’s low-temperature market. I could not find its volumes.
- **Sakaiminato**: a newspaper reported on 20 May 2026 that the season’s first fresh bluefin, about 80 fish averaging around 140 kg, were landed by purse seiners fishing west of Oki, and that last season’s landings were about 1,130 t, second in Japan after Shiogama in Miyagi. That is a newspaper figure; I could not confirm an official one.
- **Ōma**: *Ōma maguro* is a regional collective trademark, number 5051377, registered on 1 June 2007, held by the Ōma fisheries cooperative. The fisheries agency’s document says the Tsugaru Strait fishery, by hand lines and similar, takes large fish of four years and older. I could not find an official source that says Ōma’s tuna is caught on a single line.

## Away from the port

I could not find an official source comparing taste or freshness at the port with Tokyo, for any of these fish, so this page does not say one is better. What the sources do show is that "where" means different things:

- **Seki saba’s** quality is attributed to a chain of handling at the port — line, live tank, tsura-gai, ikejime. No source compares it in Tokyo and in Oita.
- **Yaizu and Misaki** are mainly frozen, distant-water tuna ports; Sakaiminato’s first bluefin of 2026 were fresh fish from domestic purse seiners. The same word, *hon-maguro*, can arrive by quite different routes. I do not claim one causes a difference in the plate.
- **Ōma maguro** is a trademarked name managed by its cooperative, not just a place.

Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Toro has always been the best part."** In the Edo period, the farm ministry says, it was not served to guests.
- **"Akami just means red meat."** It is also the name of a fish class defined by pigment protein.
- **"There is one correct tataki."** Kochi prefecture and the farm ministry describe different steps, and the dressing changes across Kochi itself.
- **"Big tuna are what gets caught most."** By number, most bluefin caught are in their first or second year.
