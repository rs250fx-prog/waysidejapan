The Japanese convenience store — *konbini* — is, in JNTO's words, a place to eat relatively cheaply at any hour: a rice ball, fried chicken, a bento heated at the counter. It is also a piece of national infrastructure with a rulebook visitors never see: a tax line that changes if you sit down, payment rules that differ by chain, and food engineered to survive the shelf. This page is the map. The lineage pages cover the two foods most worth understanding: the rice ball and oden.

## What it is

**The scale.** The Japan Franchise Association's statistics cover seven member chains — Seicomart, Seven-Eleven, FamilyMart, Poplar, Ministop, Daily Yamazaki and Lawson. In 2025 they had 56,054 stores at year end, sales of ¥12.06 trillion (up 2.2%, the fifth rise in a row), 16.3 billion customer visits and an average spend of ¥737.9 (figures before tax). The association thinks record visitor numbers and the Osaka Expo helped, and names rice balls, counter foods, sweets and soft drinks as strong sellers. In August 2026 there were 56,189 stores, and fresh daily food — including counter coffee, fried food, steamed buns, rice balls, bento and sushi — was 35.3% of sales.

**The big three**, each by its own latest count (different dates, so not to be added up):

- **Seven-Eleven** — 21,956 stores in Japan at the end of September 2026. First store May 1974 in Toyosu, Kōtō, Tokyo; first 24-hour store June 1975 in Kōriyama, Fukushima.
- **FamilyMart** — 16,455 in Japan on 31 August 2026. First store September 1973 in Sayama, Saitama; first franchise 1978 in Funabashi, Chiba.
- **Lawson** — 14,697 in Japan at the end of February 2026, including Natural Lawson and Lawson Store 100. First store 14 June 1975, Sakurazuka in Toyonaka, Osaka; at the start, it says, it mostly sold American-style party food.

**When it began.** Two dates are given. A 2004 business-history paper says the first Japanese convenience store is said to be Mammy Toyonaka, opened in 1969 by a wholesaler-led voluntary chain — while placing the real start of the era with Seven-Eleven's 1973 founding and 1974 first store. Seven-Eleven's own history begins in 1973–74. A 2020 trade-ministry report says only "over forty years". This site gives both.

## Why it is the way it is

**Open all night, mostly.** Seven-Eleven started 24-hour opening in 1975. The trade ministry's 2020 report says 24-hour opening was applied almost uniformly, on the idea that the reassurance of an always-open store brought in daytime customers too; it then recommended moving to decisions store by store, citing users' views and owners' ageing and staff shortages. JNTO says *most* are open 24 hours.

**More than a shop.** The same report describes stores handling deliveries, cash machines, utility payments and even resident certificates, and being expected to serve as crime-prevention and disaster-supply points.

**The tax line.** This is the rule that most affects visitors' receipts. Under Japan's consumption tax (as of the date of this page):

- Food and drink to take away is taxed at the reduced 8% rate.
- Food eaten in a store's eat-in area is "providing a meal", taxed at the standard 10%.
- For things like hot snacks and bento, which could go either way, the store must confirm your intention — but, the tax agency says, a store where most people take away can do this with a sign such as "please tell us if you will use the eat-in corner".
- Seven-Eleven and FamilyMart publish tax-included prices at 8%, assuming takeaway. Seven-Eleven shows them to two decimal places (¥182 before tax is ¥196.56) and calculates tax on the total at the till, dropping fractions of a yen.

**A change is proposed, not law.** In September 2026 the tax agency published a Q&A on a government plan to cut the tax on food to 1% for two years from 1 April 2027, explicitly conditional on a bill being passed. Until then, the rules above apply.

**Eat-in.** JNTO says larger stores have tables, chairs and space to eat. Not all do.

## Paying and heating

**Payment.** Each big chain publishes a list. All three take transport IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA and others), major credit cards, and a range of barcode apps, plus Apple Pay and Google Pay at FamilyMart and Lawson. One difference: Seven-Eleven says its code payments work by showing a barcode on your phone, and that QR codes cannot be used at the counter. Its contactless card payments stop above ¥10,000. None of the lists mentions cash, so I make no claim about it either way.

**Cash machines.** JNTO says some stores have ATMs that accept selected international cards, with major chains — mainly Seven-Eleven, mainly in cities — offering multilingual machines, and that service can depend on the time and card.

**Heating.** JNTO points to the store microwave. Seven-Eleven heats bento, side dishes and noodles that show a time for its 1,500 W ovens; items marked only with household 500 or 600 W times are, as a rule, not heated, because the strong oven may burst them.

## Where to find it

JNTO's budget guide says there is a konbini on almost any corner in the big cities. FamilyMart is known for its fried chicken, *Famichiki*, by JNTO's account.

## The same store, a different region

- **The shelf changes by region.** Seven-Eleven marks each rice ball and oden item with the regions it is sold in. FamilyMart blends its oden broth four ways — east, west, Kyushu, Okinawa — and sells different items in different regions. See the lineage pages.
- **Payment and heating rules are the chain's**, not the town's.

## What people get wrong

- **"The price on the shelf is what I pay."** It is the takeaway price. Eat in, and the higher rate applies.
- **"Every konbini is open 24 hours."** Most are; the government has encouraged stores to decide for themselves.
- **"Any QR app works."** Seven-Eleven says QR codes cannot be used at its counters.
- **"They will heat anything."** Only items marked for the store's oven.
- **"Seven-Eleven opened the first Japanese convenience store."** It is one of two dates; a 1969 store is also cited.
