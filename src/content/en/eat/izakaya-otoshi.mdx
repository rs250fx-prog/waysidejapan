You sit down, order a beer, and before it arrives a small dish appears: a few pieces of simmered vegetable, some edamame, a spoonful of potato salad. You did not order it, and it will be on the bill. This is the *otoshi*, and Japan's consumer affairs center names it as a typical source of disputes with visitors. It has not, in the ordinary case. But the official sources do not agree on everything about it, and the consumer authority's advice is worth knowing.

For how an izakaya evening runs from the door to the bill, start from the izakaya overview.

## What it is

The descriptions from official tourism bodies line up on the essentials:

- **Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO):** a compulsory starter served to each customer, which works as a table charge. Another JNTO page says it is not free and is regarded as a table charge, and to ask staff if unsure. A JNTO blog post describes it as a small dish served before you order — often light, such as edamame, pickles or potato salad — that cannot be swapped.
- **GO TOKYO, Tokyo's official tourism site:** the otoshi is covered by the seat charge (*otōshi-dai*), can be anything from simmered vegetables to fresh fish, is charged per person, and as a rule cannot be refused.
- **Kyoto's tourism association:** a small dish brought automatically, about ¥300–500 per person.

## Where it came from

Two official tourism sites give two different explanations, and neither cites a source. GO TOKYO says the custom is said to have begun as a sign that the customer had been seated. Kyoto's tourism association says it comes from a tradition of hospitality: not keeping the customer waiting with nothing until the ordered food arrives. This page gives both and does not choose.

## Can you refuse it?

Here the official sources disagree. GO TOKYO says it generally cannot be declined. Kyoto's tourism association says that if you do not want it, you can ask staff for something else, decline it, or check with the shop before going in.

I could not find a primary official source — from the Consumer Affairs Agency or a local authority — on whether there is a legal obligation to pay for it or a right to refuse it. The furthest an official body goes is the National Consumer Affairs Center's advice to visitors: think of it as the table charge for your seat.

The practical reading: in Tokyo, assume it comes with the seat; anywhere, if you do not want it, ask at the door.

## What visitors have complained about

In December 2018 the National Consumer Affairs Center opened a hotline for visitors to Japan. Two of the cases it published:

- **February 2019, in Chinese.** Two people at an izakaya were charged about ¥400 each for a dish they had not ordered — the otoshi — and were also charged for two portions of a hotpot when they had ordered one.
- **September 2019, in Japanese, from a resident of South Korea.** A meal that should have come to ¥2,500 was billed at ¥4,300. The manager said the difference was the otoshi. The customer knew about otoshi, but nothing was said when ordering and it was not on the menu.

The Center's analysis is that the otoshi is a typical case of trouble caused by different customs: widely known to Japanese people, but visitors may question paying for something they did not order. It names shops' failure to explain as a cause too. In its first year, the hotline received 24 consultations about eating out and food delivery, second only to accommodation (49). It does not give a separate count for otoshi.

## The other lines on the bill

**A cover charge on top.** The Center's advice: if you are charged a separate "charge" in addition to the otoshi, or the amount seems wrong, ask staff before paying.

**Per-person prices with a minimum.** Hotpots, grilled meat and set courses may say "¥— per person" on the menu but only be orderable for two or more, and this is often in small print. The photo may not show one portion. Read the menu, or ask.

**Late-night charges.** JNTO says some izakaya add a surcharge for late-night customers. I could not find official figures for the hours or amounts.

**All-you-can-drink.** GO TOKYO says many izakaya offer a fixed price for unlimited drinks for a set time, usually one to two hours, sometimes all night, and that early-evening happy hours are common.

**Service charge.** GO TOKYO says service is usually included. There is no tipping custom.

**Tax.** Kyoto's tourism association gives an example of a menu priced before the 10% consumption tax, which is added when you pay. Menus differ.

## Fukuoka's stalls are different

The one place where official guidance says most venues charge no otoshi is Fukuoka's yatai. The city's tourism site says most stalls charge neither an otoshi nor a seat charge, though bar-style stalls may. Instead it asks for at least one drink and one dish per person. Most stalls are cash only, with about one in ten taking cards. Stall prices must be posted, as the Hakata ramen page explains.

## In Tokyo and away from it

There is no official comparison of otoshi between cities, or between tourist areas and neighbourhoods. What the official sources show is that the advice itself varies by city: Tokyo's site says the otoshi generally cannot be refused, Kyoto's says you can ask, and Fukuoka's says most stalls have none. Wherever you are, the consumer centre's advice holds: if a charge is unclear, ask before you pay.

## What people get wrong

- **"The otoshi is a scam."** It is a standard charge that official sources call a table charge. The disputes the consumer centre records are about it not being explained.
- **"It's a free appetiser."** It is charged, per person.
- **"Everyone agrees you cannot refuse it."** Tokyo's tourism site says so; Kyoto's says you can ask. The law is not spelled out in any official source I found.
- **"One per person per hotpot."** Some dishes priced per person can only be ordered for two or more. Read the small print.
- **"Fukuoka stalls charge it too."** Most do not, by the city's account; bar-style stalls may.
