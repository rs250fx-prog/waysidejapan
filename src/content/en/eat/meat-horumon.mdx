*Horumon* and *motsu* are the two everyday Japanese words for offal: the tripe, intestines, heart and liver that go on the yakiniku grill, into a Hakata hotpot or onto a skewer. The official word is longer and duller, and the everyday ones come with more than one story. This page covers the names, the two rules every visitor should know (raw beef liver and raw pork are banned), and the places where offal is a local dish: Hakata, Osaka, Kokura and Sayo.

For meat in general, start from the meat overview.

## Where it came from

**The words.** The Japan Meat Information Service Center’s glossary uses *chikusan fukuseibutsu*, livestock by-products, for everything left when the carcass and hide are taken away. It says this is the unified name for what used to be called by the trade words *motsu* and *horumon*, and that it is also called "variety meat". Its entry for *horumon* notes that, besides the physiological meaning, in the meat trade the word refers to internal organs. It gives no etymology.

**Two accounts of the name, and this site does not choose.**

- **A trade word.** The glossary records *horumon* as the same word as the physiological "hormone", used in the meat trade for offal, without saying why.
- **"Things thrown away."** For Sayo horumon *yaki-udon*, from Sayo in Hyōgo, the Kinki regional office of the farm ministry says the dish used fresh offal that had been discarded as *hōru-mon*, things thrown away. It gives this as the local story. The ministry says the dish is thought to have been born after the war, in a town where livestock and butchery were thriving.

Other explanations exist, but I could not find them in any public source I opened, so they are not given here.

**The regions.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says Kansai has cooked beef offal for a long time, and that some parts have different names there from Kanto. The meat glossary says that in the Kanto area pork offal is used for grilled skewers. The two sources are about different things and do not compare the regions directly. Read together, they show beef offal in Kansai and pork offal on skewers in Kanto.

## What makes it horumon

**The parts.** The glossary’s list of beef by-products, with the *aff* guide’s notes on the names:

- **Mino** — the first stomach, the largest and thickest of the four.
- **Hachinosu** — named for its honeycomb lining; *trippa* in Italian, says *aff*.
- **Senmai** — the third stomach, "a thousand sheets", for its folds.
- **Hatsu** — heart. **Rebā** — liver. **Mame** — kidney, "bean", from its grape-bunch shape.
- **Harami** and **sagari** — both the diaphragm.
- **Himo** — small intestine. **Shimachō** — large intestine.
- **Tan** — tongue. **Kashiraniku** — head meat. **Tēru** — tail.

The glossary says most are grilled as yakiniku or simmered in *motsu-nikomi*, a stew. Pork large and small intestines go into the same stew.

**One part, two Kansai names.** The small intestine, *kopuchan*, has two names in Kansai, according to *aff*. Left whole it is *maruchō*; split open it is *kotecchan*. The article does not give the Kanto names.

**Not raw.** The health ministry has banned selling or serving beef liver to be eaten raw since July 2012; Chiba prefecture gives the start date as 1 July 2012. The ministry’s reason is that there is no way to eat raw beef liver safely. Enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli* such as O157 can cause food poisoning in small numbers and has been reported inside the liver as well. Its guide for cooking is 63°C at the centre for 30 minutes or more, or 75°C for one minute or more. Raw pork meat and offal have been banned since 12 June 2015, because of the risk of serious food poisoning from hepatitis E virus and bacteria, with salmonella, campylobacter and parasites also named. At a yakiniku table, the liver is for grilling.

## Hakata motsunabe

**The dish.** Fukuoka city’s nutrition pages say that after the Second World War a soy-flavoured dish of offal and garlic chives cooked in an aluminium pot spread in Hakata, and that miso and salt versions came later.

**Three origins, and this site does not choose.**

- **Postwar Hakata.** The city’s tourism site Yokanavi says the root seems to be offal and chives cooked in soy in an aluminium pot after the war. A feature on the same site says offal was simmered in aluminium pots and seasoned with soy just after the war, as a way to eat well from parts not normally eaten in a time of food shortage.
- **The coal miners.** The first Yokanavi page adds that it is said to have been started by people working in the coal mines. It hedges both lines.
- **Kokura’s maruchō.** Kitakyushu city’s Kokura Yakiniku-dōri map says a shop in Kyōmachi, Kokura, once fed its staff *maruchō*, small intestine turned inside out, and passes on a story that an employee opened his own shop in Hakata, which became the root of Hakata motsunabe.

None of the three comes with a shop name or a year that the sources confirm.

**Not on the farm ministry’s list.** The ministry’s regional food database lists eleven dishes for Fukuoka, including *mizutaki*. Motsunabe is not among them.

**"Nabe" means two things in Fukuoka.** A column on Fukuoka city’s promotional site says that when people in Fukuoka hear *nabe*, the young think of motsunabe and long-time Hakata residents think of *mizutaki*. Mizutaki, it says, is said to have begun when Hayashida Heizaburō, from Nagasaki, put up the sign of Suigetsu in Fukuoka in 1905.

## Osaka, Sayo and Kokura

- **Osaka.** The Osaka tourism board describes yakiniku shops grilling beef and offal, called *horumon*, over a direct flame and dipping it in sauce. Its offal hotpots are *chiritori-nabe*, in a shallow square pan shaped like a dustpan, and motsunabe, with tongue, *tecchan*, *mino*, onion, bean sprouts and chives. The board says Tsuruhashi, the largest Korean district in Kansai, has rows of yakiniku shops and both hotpots. The yakiniku page covers its postwar history.
- **Sayo, Hyōgo.** Horumon *yaki-udon*, fried noodles with offal; the farm ministry’s regional office tells the *hōru-mon* story about it.
- **Kokura, Kitakyushu.** The city’s 2025 map says about a kilometre around Asaka-dōri is so dense with yakiniku shops that it is called Kokura Yakiniku-dōri. There was once a private group, Maruchō Shinsengumi, promoting the freshness and taste of offal from Kokura Kita ward.

I could not confirm the history of Tokyo’s *motsu-yaki* skewer bars, or any of their prices, so they are not covered here yet.

## How to eat it

The way Yokanavi describes one shop’s motsunabe:

1. **Choose the soup**: soy, miso or salt.
2. **Wait for the vegetables.** Beef small intestine, *mino* and *senmai* go in with cabbage and chives. It is ready when the vegetables have softened.
3. **Ask for maruchō** if you like it. The shop recommends the small intestine with its fat left on.
4. **Finish with noodles.** Hakata’s *champon* noodles go in the leftover soup. The finish is ordered separately.

At a yakiniku table, the liver is cooked, not eaten raw.

## Not all horumon is this

Offal is eaten differently by region in the sources: beef offal with its own names in Kansai, pork offal on skewers in Kanto, motsunabe in Hakata, offal with noodles in Sayo, and a yakiniku street in Kokura. I could not confirm the origins of Osaka’s *horumon-yaki*, or prices at Osaka’s offal specialists.

## Motsunabe away from Hakata

**One chain, two cities.** Maedaya’s shop list has several shops in Fukuoka, including its main shop and branches in Hakata, Nishi-Nakasu, Daimyō, Imaizumi and Kokura, and two in Tokyo, in Shibuya and at Shinjuku east exit. Its menu page is shared, with a note that prices vary somewhat by region. The Shibuya shop’s page lists no prices, so how much Tokyo differs I could not confirm.

**Who spends more on yakiniku.** The government household survey records what households of two or more people spend a year eating yakiniku out. On the 2023–25 average the national figure is ¥8,444. Kōchi is first at ¥19,541. Central Tokyo is 20th at ¥9,181, Osaka 24th at ¥8,729, Fukuoka 25th at ¥8,595 and Kitakyushu 40th at ¥7,410. The survey covers yakiniku eaten out, not offal or motsunabe. Kitakyushu has a street named for yakiniku and is below the national average; the two facts sit side by side without explaining each other.

I found no official source comparing motsunabe in Hakata with motsunabe in Tokyo, and this site does not rank them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Horumon means hormone because offal is good for you."** I found no public source for that account. The two on record are a trade word, and the Sayo story of *hōru-mon*, things thrown away.
- **"Raw liver is a delicacy you can still order."** Raw beef liver has been banned since July 2012 and raw pork since June 2015.
- **"Motsunabe is an official Fukuoka local dish."** The city’s own pages describe it, but the farm ministry’s list for Fukuoka does not include it.
- **"Motsunabe has one known birthplace."** Public sources give three stories and confirm none of them.
- **"Offal has the same names everywhere."** The small intestine is *maruchō* or *kotecchan* in Kansai, depending on whether it is split.
