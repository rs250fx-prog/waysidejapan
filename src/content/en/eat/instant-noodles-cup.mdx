The cup is the form of instant noodle that visitors meet first: a convenience-store shelf of them, a hot-water dispenser, and a lid with a line printed inside. It is also the larger part of the Japanese market — 66.8% of production in fiscal 2025, by the industry association's figures. Nissin launched Cup Noodle in 1971, thirteen years after its [packet noodles](/en/eat/instant-noodles-bag/), and by 1989 cups had overtaken packets.

For instant noodles in general — the definition, the numbers, prices and how to bin the cup — start from the [instant noodles overview](/en/eat/instant-noodles/).

## Where it came from

Nissin's chronicle tells it this way: in 1966 Momofuku Ando went to the United States and Europe to sell Chicken Ramen to the world. At a supermarket, the buyers broke the noodles into small pieces, put them in paper cups, poured on hot water and ate them with forks. Ando concluded that America had neither noodle bowls nor chopsticks, and that a world food had to get past differences in eating habits. He set out to make noodles packed in a cup and eaten with a fork. JNTO and the Cup Noodles Museum in Ikeda tell the same story of an American trip; none of the official pages I read names the city or the store.

The timing mattered. JIFFA, the industry association, says packet production reached 3.6 billion servings in 1970, only 102.9% of the year before: the market was saturated.

Cup Noodle went on sale on **18 September 1971**, by Nissin's and JNTO's dates; JIFFA says September 1971. JIFFA describes the first product as 84 g, in a foamed polystyrene cup, at ¥100, with a fork for each serving and a shrink-wrapped outer film.

## What makes it a cup noodle

**The cup does three jobs.** JIFFA's description: the container is the packaging, the cooking pot and the bowl.

**The shape.** Nissin says the ideal was a cup you could hold in one hand. It compared nearly 40 prototypes and chose a tall cup like an enlarged paper cup. For the material it chose foamed polystyrene — light, insulating and economical — which Nissin says was unusual in Japan then; it brought in American technology to make its own cups and says it built a cup far above the standards of the US FDA.

**The noodle hangs in the middle.** Nissin calls it the "mid-air hold": the noodle block is made wider than the bottom of the cup, so it wedges halfway up. JNTO's explanation is that hot water then flows underneath and the noodles soften evenly. Putting the block into the cup made it tilt; Nissin's answer was to lay the block face down and lower the cup over it, which made mass production possible.

**The lid.** Nissin's FAQ says its cup lids use aluminium. Once the outer film is off, it says, an unopened lid still keeps the noodles good until the best-before date. Some products have a separate sachet of oil or spice glued to the top of the lid with a hot-melt adhesive.

## Why it sold

Not at first, by Nissin's account. At ¥100 when a packet cost ¥25 it was expensive, and some objected that eating standing up was bad manners, so shops would not stock it. Nissin installed 20,000 hot-water vending machines around the country in a year and sold it at tastings in Ginza's pedestrian street, up to 20,000 servings on a busy day. Then, in February 1972, television broadcast riot police surrounding the Asama-Sansō siege eating Cup Noodle, and it started to sell. WINA tells the same episode.

JIFFA's production figures follow: 100 million cups in 1972, 1.1 billion by 1975. By 1973, 14 companies besides Nissin had entered, with 27 brands. In 1989 cups passed packets for the first time, 2,405 million to 2,225 million.

## The cups that followed

- **1973:** Acecook's Curry Noodle. Acecook's own history calls it the first ramen in a cup container; its page does not explain how that fits with Nissin's 1971 date, and I have not reconciled the two.
- **1975:** Maruka Foods' Peyang Sauce Yakisoba, which JIFFA calls the first square container; Toyo Suisan's cup kitsune udon and cup tempura soba.
- **1976:** Nissin's U.F.O. and Donbei kitsune udon.
- **1977:** Myojo's Donburi-kun, the first bowl-shaped container (JIFFA).
- **1978:** Toyo Suisan's Akai Kitsune udon, in August; Midori no Tanuki tempura soba followed in 1980.
- **1988:** Acecook's Super Cup 1.5, which JIFFA says was popular with young people gathering at convenience stores.

## The shops, and what they charge

Nissin's Cup Noodle range runs from Mini to standard to Big, plus a cold-water version and other lines. The industry's fair-labelling code, as JIFFA summarises it, allows "large serving" only for at least 30% more; I could not open the code itself.

Nissin publishes suggested prices before tax only, so I give shop prices. Seven-Eleven's site on 13 October 2026, tax included at the 8% takeaway rate:

- Cup Noodle: ¥267.84 (¥248 before tax), nationwide.
- Cup Noodle Big: ¥321.84 (¥298).
- Mini Cup Noodle: ¥168.48 (¥156).

Eating in at the store is taxed differently; see the [konbini page](/en/eat/conbini/). At both Cup Noodles Museums, the My Cup Noodles Factory is ¥500 a cup, tax included.

## How it is eaten

JIFFA's example instructions: peel the lid back halfway, pour boiling water to the line inside, close the lid, wait three minutes, peel it off and stir well. Cup Noodle takes 280 ml. Other products differ — Donbei udon takes 350 ml — so read the side of the cup.

**Boiling means boiling.** Nissin's FAQ says water kept warm in a thermos pot (95°C or below) does not rehydrate the noodles properly; water just off the boil is best.

**Cold is possible.** Nissin's chilled Cup Noodle uses 330 ml of cold water and five minutes. The maker says it uses a patented "Cold Rehydrate" method.

**Not in the microwave, no extra oil.** Unless the package says otherwise, the cup does not go in a microwave: JIFFA warns it can deform or spark, and the lid contains aluminium. JIFFA and Nissin both say not to add oils such as perilla, *egoma* or coconut oil, which can damage a polystyrene cup until hot water leaks from the bottom. The national JAS standard requires a cup that does not spill its hot contents if damaged or deformed.

**Fork or chopsticks.** The first Cup Noodle came with a fork. The JNTO-published konbini article says stores sometimes leave chopsticks by the hot water. On the sound of eating, see [slurping noodles](/en/column/slurping-noodles/).

## Nearby, and different

- **Donbei kitsune udon** is sold as two products, eastern (except Hokkaido) and western Japan.
- **Akai Kitsune udon** now comes in four regional broths, Toyo Suisan says, "to suit regional tastes". Hokkaido is bonito and Rishiri kelp; the east has bonito, kelp and frigate-mackerel flakes with dark soy; Kansai adds mixed dried fish such as mackerel and round herring with light soy; the west adds dried sardines with light soy. On 30 June 2025 the company released all four nationwide at once as a "taste comparison".
- **Nissin's regional souvenir Cup Noodles**, eaten from a bowl, come in eight flavours, from Tokyo monja to Okinawa taco rice and Tōhoku beef tongue. The Tokyo monja version is sold in souvenir corners in and around Tokyo and at the Yokohama museum.
- **Cup Okinawa soba** from Maruchan (¥267.84) and from Myojo (¥272.16), tax included, are listed by Seven-Eleven for Okinawa only.

## Cup Noodle away from Japan

Nissin took Cup Noodle to the United States in 1973 as "Cup O'Noodles". It says it does not export the Japanese taste unchanged but makes each country's version with the soup and toppings people there prefer. JNTO counts sales in more than 100 countries and over 150 flavours, among them Chili Crab in Singapore, Galinha Caipira in Brazil and Mazedaar Masala in India.

WINA's notes on the United States say Americans do not slurp, so noodles are cut short and eaten with a spoon and fork, and that microwaveable products are popular there. I found no official comparison of ingredients, water or time between the Japanese cup and its versions abroad.

## What people get wrong

- **"Pull the lid all the way off."** Halfway, then closed while you wait.
- **"Fill it to the top."** Fill to the line inside; the amount differs by product.
- **"Pot water is fine."** Nissin says water below boiling does not rehydrate properly.
- **"Cup noodles were always cheap."** Cup Noodle launched at ¥100 when, by Nissin's account, a packet cost ¥25.
