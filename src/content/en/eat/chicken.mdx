Chicken is everywhere in Japan — skewered at the yakitori counter, fried as karaage, simmered in a Hakata hotpot — and most visitors never think about which chicken it is. Japanese menus do. Words like *jidori* and *meigara-dori* are printed on the board as if they were a promise, and one of them is: *jidori* has a national standard behind it. This page is the map: what those words mean, how the parts are named, and where the two best-documented chicken dishes, karaage and mizutaki, come from. The lineage pages go deep on each.

## What it is

Japanese chicken falls into three groups, and only one has a legal definition.

- **Broiler** (*buroirā*). The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* describes it as a general term for young chickens bred to grow large in a short time — not a breed. Full-scale production is said to have begun in the United States in the 1940s. An older issue of the same magazine (December 2016) says broilers are foreign breeds, are usually shipped at around 50 days, and make up almost all the chicken on the market.
- **Brand chicken** (*meigara-dori*). In the ministry’s words, there is no standard for it as there is for jidori. It generally means chicken raised with some extra care over feed or conditions.
- **Jidori.** A defined term, set by a Japanese Agricultural Standard (JAS). More on it below.

The 2016 issue put birds of purely domestic breeds — jidori and some brand chicken — at about 2% of the total, and Japan’s self-sufficiency in chicken over the previous decade at roughly 60–70%. Those are 2016 figures; I did not find newer ones.

The trade has its own age bands too. The ministry’s trading standard for chicken meat calls a bird under three months *wakadori* (young chicken), three to five months *hiikukei*, and five months or older *oya* (parent hen or cock). That is why recipes say *wakadori* — it is a trade category, not a breed.

## Why it is the way it is

### What jidori has to be

The standard is the JAS for jidori meat, first set in 1999. The version I read was last revised in August 2015 (in force from September that year); I could not confirm whether there has been a newer revision. It sets four conditions:

- **Parentage.** The chick must be at least 50% descended from a *native breed*, with records to prove it. Native breeds are those established in or brought to Japan by the end of the Meiji era, and the standard lists 38 of them — among them the Nagoya breed, Cochin, Hinai-dori, Satsuma-dori, Shamo, Rhode Island Red, Aizu-jidori and the silkie (*ukokkei*). The percentage is worked out by halving: a native breed counts as 100%, a non-native breed as 0%, and a cross takes half of each parent’s figure.
- **Time.** At least 75 days from hatching.
- **Method.** From 28 days old, floor-reared (*hiragai*) — on the floor of a shed or on open ground, free to move about. Keeping birds outdoors in the daytime is a separate term, *hanashigai*.
- **Space.** From 28 days old, no more than 10 birds per square metre.

The standard also controls the label. Meat sold as jidori must show the name, the cross (for example "father X × mother Y"), the rearing period, the rearing method, the quantity and the producer’s name and address. It may not carry words implying a competition prize (with some exceptions), words suggesting government endorsement, or anything contradicting the rest of the label. The ministry’s feature on chicken explains jidori simply as a bird that carries at least half Japanese native blood, and notes that some producers work to stricter rules of their own.

I saw a search summary claiming a ministry document gives 80 days. I did not open that document; the standard itself says 75.

### Three famous birds, three prefectures

- **Nagoya Cochin (Aichi).** Aichi prefecture says it was bred by the Kaifu brothers, former retainers of the Owari domain, by crossing native chickens with the Chinese Buff Cochin, and was recognised on 10 March 1905 by the Japan Poultry Association as the first practical domestic breed. It began as a dual-purpose bird and is now kept in meat and egg lines; the meat type is shipped at about 130–150 days. Aichi calls it a nationally famous pure-bred jidori, with breeding done at the prefecture’s agricultural research centre, and says Aichi produces 62.8% of Nagoya Cochin meat (the page does not give a year).
- **Hinai-jidori (Akita).** The table bird is a first cross of an Akita Hinai-dori cock and a Rhode hen. Akita traces the line to the Hinai-dori of the Edo period, paid as tax in what was then called Hinai, in the north of the prefecture. The pure Hinai-dori was designated a natural monument in 1942; the ornamental bird is called Hinai-dori, the table line Akita Hinai-dori. Akita’s own certification is stricter than the JAS: hens 150 days or more, cocks 100 days or more, and no more than five birds per square metre from 28 days. DNA identification was added in 2009. Akita describes the meat as rich in flavour, with more red meat and sweet fat, and more inosinic acid than a broiler.
- **Satsuma (Kagoshima).** The *Satsuma-dori* itself is a fighting bird of the old Satsuma domain, designated a national natural monument in August 1943 and now kept only for show. What Kagoshima sells as meat are three crosses: *Satsuma wakashamo* (bred in 1971), *Satsuma jidori* (2000) and *Kuro Satsuma-dori* (2006). In fiscal 2023 they shipped 74,000, 3,000 and 128,000 birds. "Satsuma jidori" and "Kuro Satsuma-dori" are registered trademarks; the prefecture’s pages do not use the spelling 薩摩地鶏, and they do not give rearing days, so I could not confirm whether each meets the JAS definition of jidori.

These three are often called Japan’s "three great jidori". I could not find who decided that. Kagoshima uses the phrase; Akita’s page says "three great tasty chickens" instead.

### How the parts are named

Japanese menus name chicken by part, and the ministry’s trading standard gives the definitions:

- **Wings.** *Teba* is the whole wing; *tebamoto* the upper-arm section; *tebasaki* the rest of the wing; *tebanaka* the middle section; *tebahashi* the tip.
- **Meat.** *Mune* is breast, *momo* thigh, and *shōniku* the two together. *Sasami* is the tender — the deep breast muscle, with its tendon.
- **The rest.** *Kawa* (skin), *koniku* (meat trimmed from the neck), *kimo* (heart and liver, or separately *hāto* and *rebā*), *sunagimo* (gizzard), *nankotsu* (cartilage of the breastbone and joints), *kubi* (neck) and *gara* (bones).

The ministry’s magazine adds three names you will meet at yakitori counters: *furisode*, between breast and wing root; *seseri*, neck meat, only a little per bird; and *bonjiri*, at the base of the tail, the fattiest part. It also says breast meat is said to be rich in imidazole dipeptides, which it describes as helping recovery from fatigue.

## What it costs

There is no single price for chicken as a dish, and I did not find published, tax-included prices from karaage specialists or restaurant chains. Two convenience-store hot snacks are given as examples, tax included, as posted on 2026-10-10:

- **Lawson, Karaage-kun regular** — ¥288, five pieces.
- **FamilyMart, Famichiki** — ¥248. FamilyMart lists it as fried chicken, not karaage, and states the price is at the 8% reduced rate.

As of 2026-10-10, consumption tax in Japan is 8% on takeaway food and 10% when you eat in. Neither page gives a weight, so I have not compared them per gram.

A few things the table cannot show:

- **Karaage is a method, not a cut.** The Japan Karaage Association defines it as frying in oil after a light dusting of wheat flour or starch, and says it is not limited to chicken.
- **Oita is the karaage prefecture.** Usa is where the association places the first karaage specialist shops; Nakatsu, also in Oita, is the town with 60 or more of them. Oita city also led a 2019 government survey of household chicken consumption, according to the farm ministry.
- **Mizutaki is a Fukuoka hotpot with a documented founder** — and a second, broader origin story that the ministry tells alongside it.

## Where to eat it

- **Nakatsu and Usa (Oita)** — karaage specialist shops, fried to order.
- **Beppu (Oita)** — *toriten*, chicken fried in tempura batter, which the ministry traces to a Beppu restaurant.
- **Fukuoka city** — mizutaki, at old specialist houses and newer, more casual shops.
- **Aichi, Akita and Kagoshima** — the home prefectures of Nagoya Cochin, Hinai-jidori and the Satsuma birds.

## The same name, a different place

What I could find is about the home towns; what I could not find is a source comparing them with the same dish elsewhere.

- **At home**, Nakatsu’s specialists fry to order and each shop has its own marinade; Hakata’s mizutaki comes in cloudy and clear styles depending on the shop, and is eaten all year.
- **Away from home**, I found no official source comparing the taste, quality or price of Nakatsu karaage or Hakata mizutaki in Tokyo with the original, and this site does not say one is better.
- **For jidori outside its home prefecture**, the one check I can point to is the JAS label: name, cross, rearing period, rearing method and producer. A word on a menu board is not the label.

## What people get wrong

- **"Jidori just means local chicken."** It has a national standard: half native blood, 75 days or more, floor-reared from 28 days, at most 10 birds per square metre.
- **"Brand chicken is a kind of jidori."** Brand chicken has no standard at all.
- **"Broiler is a breed."** It is a general term for fast-growing young chickens.
- **"Satsuma jidori is the Satsuma-dori."** The Satsuma-dori is a protected show bird; the meat on sale comes from three crosses bred from it.
- **"The three great jidori are an official list."** I could not find who made the list, and the prefectures do not even use the same phrase.
- **"Karaage means chicken."** By the association’s definition, fish, vegetables and other meats can be karaage too.
