Rice usually arrives white, plain and unpriced, folded into a set meal. Behind that bowl is a lot of structure: a kind of rice chosen for stickiness, one variety that covers nearly a third of the ordinary rice planted, a grading system that says nothing about taste, a law that tells you where the rice was grown, and a wholesale price the government publishes every month. This page is the map. The lineage pages cover two things Japan does with rice that travel well: the rice ball, and curry rice.

## What it is

Japan eats short-grain rice of two kinds. Ordinary rice is *uruchimai*. Glutinous rice, *mochigome*, is the rice for mochi and other sticky dishes. For the 2025 crop, the Rice Stable Supply Support Organization (a public-interest corporation) counts 95.4% of the rice planted as ordinary rice other than for brewing, 1.3% as rice for brewing sake, and 3.3% as glutinous.

The difference is in the starch. The farm ministry explains that rice is 70–80% carbohydrate, almost all of it starch, and that starch comes in two forms, amylose and amylopectin. In polished ordinary rice the ratio is about 2 to 8. Glutinous rice is almost 100% amylopectin. The less amylose, the stickier the rice, and the less it hardens as it cools.

Within ordinary rice, the ministry gives 17–23% amylose as usual, with Koshihikari at about 17%. Rice at 15% or less is called low-amylose; 24% or more is high-amylose, which the ministry describes as less sticky and separate-grained, suited to pilaf, fried rice and spice curry.

**How much.** The farm ministry's food balance sheet puts rice supply at 53.3 kg per person in fiscal 2024 (final figures, about 146 g a day) and 52 kg in fiscal 2025 (provisional figures published on 7 August 2026, about 142.6 g a day). Domestic production in the same table was 7.868 million tonnes in fiscal 2024 and 8.043 million in fiscal 2025. When the ministry explained why Japan's calorie-based food self-sufficiency fell to 37% in fiscal 2025, down one point, it pointed in particular to lower rice consumption and higher private stocks. Older peak figures for rice eating circulate widely; I could not confirm them from a primary source, so none is given here.

**How much land.** The ministry put the area planted with rice for eating at 1.367 million hectares for the 2025 crop, as of 15 September 2025, up 108,000 hectares on the previous year's 1.259 million.

## Why it is the way it is

**One variety dominates.** For the 2025 crop of ordinary rice, Koshihikari took 31.4% of the planted area, mainly in Niigata, Ibaraki and Fukushima. Then came Hitomebore (8.5%), Hinohikari (6.7%), Akitakomachi (6.5%) and Nanatsuboshi (3.5%), with Haenuki, Masshigura, Kinumusume, Yumepirika and Tsuyahime completing the top ten. Those ten took 67.0% of the area; the top twenty, 77.8%. The top five were in the same order as the year before.

The farm ministry's magazine *aff* (January 2016) says Koshihikari was born in Fukui in 1956, describes it as sweet, sticky and good even cold, and says it had been the most widely planted variety since 1979.

**How many varieties?** It depends on the count. A farm ministry page says about 1,000 varieties were registered as of 2023, about 300 are actually planted, and the top twenty cover about 80%. The 2016 *aff* article says more than 500 had been developed, counting rice for eating and for sake. These are different definitions in different years; this site does not choose between them.

**Sasanishiki is no longer national.** It is not in the 2025 national top twenty. In Miyagi it is third, at 7.1%, behind Hitomebore (72.5%) and Tsuyahime (11.4%).

**The same name can taste different.** The ministry notes that the same variety changes with the year, region, soil, growing method, how the brown rice is prepared and how it is polished. Blends, it says, can keep prices down or the taste even, and some use uninspected or below-standard rice.

**The grade is physical.** Under the national inspection standard for brown ordinary rice, first grade needs at least 70% whole, well-formed grains, second grade 60%, third grade 45%, all at 15.0% moisture or less, with limits on damaged, dead, coloured and foreign grains (15%, 20% and 30%). Rice that fails all three is "outside standard". It is a measure of the grain, not a taste score.

**The bag tells you three things.** Since July 2021, according to the Consumer Affairs Agency, polished rice can show its origin, variety and crop year even without an inspection certificate, as long as the seller keeps documents to back them up. A single-origin bag says so, with the three items; a mix says it is blended, with proportions. A line saying the details are certified by inspection is optional.

**Every rice ball is traceable.** The rice traceability law requires anyone who sells, imports, processes, makes or serves rice and rice products, farmers included, to record transactions and pass on where the rice was grown. Records can be on paper or electronic and are kept, in principle, for three years, so that a problem can be traced quickly. The Kinki regional farm office lists what is covered: rice itself, rice flour and *kōji*, cooked-rice products from bento and rice balls to rice burgers and packaged cooked rice, and processed foods including mochi, dango, rice crackers, sake and mirin.

How Japanese households cook rice — the soak, the water line, the rest after the switch — is covered in a separate column on cooking rice, not repeated here.

## What it costs

There is no shop price for a bowl of rice: in restaurants it comes inside a meal. And I could not find an official statistic for the shelf price of a 5 kg bag. What the government does publish is the wholesale price.

**The figure on the news.** The farm ministry's monthly "relative trading price" is the weighted average of contract prices between shippers and wholesalers for first-grade brown rice, **including freight, packaging and consumption tax**, per 60 kg. For the 2025 crop, in August 2026 the all-variety average was ¥31,556 per 60 kg of brown rice, ¥930 (3%) lower than the month before, on 57,000 tonnes traded (published 18 September 2026).

The ministry's release for the first month of the 2025 crop, September 2025, is a preliminary figure with a later correction sheet, so I have not quoted it here.

It is not the price of a bag in a supermarket. The ministry says it began publishing supply and price information in March 2014, as part of a review of rice policy, applied from the 2018 crop, meant to let farmers and collectors decide for themselves how much to grow according to demand.

**In a restaurant, you choose the amount.** At CoCo Ichibanya, the curry chain, the standard is 300 g of rice. You can go down to 150 g (¥90 off) or up to 900 g and beyond; 350 g costs ¥65 more and 400 g ¥130 more, then ¥130 for every extra 100 g, for its pork, mild pork and vegetable curries (tax included, checked 2026-10-10; beef and hayashi are priced differently). The amount of sauce changes with the rice. Some set-meal chains offer free refills of rice instead. I could not find a statistic on how many people take them.

## Where to eat it

Everywhere, which is the point. The useful thing to look for is not a shop but a label: under the traceability law, restaurants must tell you where the rice in their rice dishes was grown, on the menu, a notice or a sign, or through staff on request. If it is the staff, there should be a notice saying to ask. The farm ministry's examples of rice dishes include plain rice, rice porridge, pilaf, paella, fried rice, rice balls and rice burgers. A restaurant that cooks bagged rice bought at a supermarket still has to keep a record of it.

I could not find a requirement for restaurants to name the variety, so a menu that says only "domestic rice" is not hiding anything the law asks for.

## The same name, a different place

The rice you are served depends on where you are, because each prefecture plants something different. On the rice organisation's 2025 tables (shares of ordinary rice planted, estimated):

- **Hokkaido** — Nanatsuboshi, 51.7%
- **Aomori** — Masshigura, 79.5%
- **Iwate** — Hitomebore, 68.4%
- **Miyagi** — Hitomebore, 72.5%
- **Akita** — Akitakomachi, 77.3%
- **Yamagata** — Haenuki, 60.1%
- **Niigata** — Koshihikari, 67.0%
- **Ishikawa** — Koshihikari, 57.4%
- **Fukuoka** — Yumetsukushi, 42.5%
- **Kumamoto** — Hinohikari, 44.6%
- **Kagoshima** — Hinohikari, 58.5%
- **Okinawa** — Hitomebore, 81.7%

Glutinous rice has its own map. Hokkaido grows 17.9% of Japan's glutinous rice, led by a variety called Kazenoko-mochi.

So in a rice-growing region, the everyday rice is likely to be the local leader; in Tokyo, the bag or the menu tells you the origin. I could not find an official comparison of whether shops in growing regions and in Tokyo use the same varieties or price them differently, and nothing official says the rice is better at the source. ## What people get wrong

- **"Sticky rice is glutinous rice."** Ordinary Japanese rice is sticky because it is low in amylose. Glutinous rice is a different kind, about 3% of what is planted.
- **"First grade means best tasting."** The grade measures whole grains, moisture and defects.
- **"The rice price on the news is what I pay."** It is a wholesale price for brown rice per 60 kg, including freight, packaging and tax.
- **"Koshihikari is all Japan eats."** It is about a third of ordinary rice planted. Several prefectures are led by other varieties by a wide margin.
- **"A restaurant must tell you the brand of rice."** It must tell you where it was grown. I found no rule about the variety.
