An izakaya is a Japanese pub, and its bill is one of the misunderstandings Japan's consumer affairs center names as typical for visitors. Usually nothing is wrong. The bill is built differently from a restaurant's: a small dish you did not order arrives and is charged, food comes in small rounds rather than courses, and in some places a drink-only order is not quite enough. This page explains how the evening runs and why. The otoshi page goes into the bill in detail; the yakitori page covers the most common kind of specialist izakaya.

## What it is

Tokyo's official tourism site explains the name by its three characters — *i* (to stay), *saka* (sake), *ya* (shop) — a place where you settle in to drink. It compares izakaya to tapas bars and gastropubs. They range from one-person shops to huge chains, from bar-like to restaurant-like, with tatami seating on cushions or Western chairs and tables.

**Where it came from.** The history is less settled than it is usually told. A reference answer from the National Diet Library's collaborative database cites a popular guide to Edo life that dates the izakaya to the Kyōhō era (1716–36) and says it began at sake shops; there were no chairs or tables, and warm sake was served in a metal jug called a *chirori* rather than a flask. Another illustrated history it cites says such places were also called *nawa-noren* (rope curtain), *niuri-sakaya* and *shirikake-sakaya*, and that the chirori later gave way to the flask. The same database records that Edo's authorities restricted *niuri* — shops selling cooked food — after dark in the 1660s and 1670s, though one source notes the basis for the stated hour is unclear. These are secondary books, so this page treats the Kyōhō date as one account, not the origin.

Drinking at a sake shop's counter survives. In Kitakyushu it is called *kaku-uchi*: a private tourism magazine, Fukuoka Now, ties it to workers from the Yawata steelworks, which began operating in 1901, stopping at a liquor shop on the way home. Most are standing only, many cash only, a drink for a few hundred yen. I could not confirm this from the city's own materials, so treat it as a magazine's account.

## How the evening runs

**At the door.** Wait to be shown to a seat, even if you can see an empty one, even if you have booked. You will be asked how many you are. If the place is full, some shops have a list to write your name and number on.

**When you sit.** Water is usually free (sometimes self-service). The *oshibori*, the damp towel, is for your hands, not your face. Then a small dish arrives that you did not order — the *otoshi*. Japan's national tourism organisation says it tends to come straight away, so tell staff about any dietary restrictions as soon as you sit down.

**The first drink.** Many groups start with beer; Kyoto's tourism association says it is because beer is the quickest to bring, and that groups often toast with the same drink. The same article says many Kyoto locals start with sake. The national tourism organisation notes that starting to drink before the toast is considered bad manners.

**Ordering.** Wait for staff, press the call button, or use the tablet that most big chains have. At busy times you may wait. Tokyo's official site says it is usual for each person to order at least one dish, and that pointing at pictures or using a translation app is fine; many places have multilingual menus, though small shops often have only handwritten Japanese ones. The rhythm is small: a few dishes, then a few more with the next round of drinks.

**Sharing.** Dishes are for the table. Use the small plates (*torizara*) to take your share.

**The arc of a night**, as Kyoto's tourism association describes it: something light first — salad, sashimi — then simmered, fried and grilled dishes as the drinking goes on; often a second place (*nijikai*); and finally a *shime*, a closing dish such as rice porridge, rice with tea, grilled rice balls or ramen.

**The bill.** Some places bring it to the table, some have you pay at the register by the door. Tokyo's official site gives both in different articles and, in a third, says you can ask for the bill or make an X with your fingers, that it usually arrives without a breakdown, and that groups usually split it evenly. If unsure, ask. There is no tipping.

**The hours.** Izakaya are evening places, generally not open at lunch. Many close around midnight, with last orders 30 to 60 minutes before.

## The bill, and why it surprises people

The figures above are kept on the price reference page and update there. Three things make an izakaya bill differ from what visitors expect, and the otoshi page explains each in full:

- **The otoshi.** Every customer gets one and pays for it. Japan's tourism organisation calls it a table charge; Kyoto's tourism association gives ¥300–500 per person as a guide. The National Consumer Affairs Center lists it as a typical misunderstanding among visitors, and blames shops' failure to explain it as well as the cultural difference.
- **Extras.** Some izakaya add a late-night charge. All-you-can-drink deals (*nomihōdai*) are common, usually for one to two hours.
- **Portion minimums.** Hotpots, grilled meat and set courses are sometimes priced per person but can only be ordered for two or more, in small print.

Kyoto's tourism association also notes that some menus show prices before the 10% consumption tax, which is then added at the till. Check which kind of menu you have.

## Where to go

- **Tokyo's yokochō** — narrow alleys of small bars, marked by red or white lanterns and a curtain with the shop's name. Many seat about ten; some in Shibuya's Nonbei Yokochō take four or five. Go in a small group. Drinking and smoking in the street are not allowed.
- **Kyoto** — where, by the tourism association's account, a local is as likely to start with sake as with beer.
- **Fukuoka's yatai** — street stalls, covered on the Hakata ramen page and below.
- **Kitakyushu's kaku-uchi** — liquor-shop counters, by a tourism magazine's account.

Fares and travel times are not on this page.

## The same night out, in a different city

There is no official comparison of izakaya in Tokyo, Osaka and Fukuoka, and none of tourist-area prices against neighbourhood prices. What the official tourism sites do record:

- **The first drink varies.** Beer is the common opener; in Kyoto, many locals start with sake.
- **Fukuoka's stalls run on different rules.** The city's tourism site says seats are few and suit two or three people; order at least one drink and one dish each; most stalls charge neither otoshi nor a cover, though bar-style stalls may; most are cash only (about one in ten takes cards); most open around 18:30–19:00 and close around midnight, with no fixed holidays and closures in rain; very few take bookings; and there are no toilets.
- **Local alleys are less translated.** Tokyo's guide to yokochō says the alleys locals prefer are unlikely to have English menus.
- **"Yakitori" changes meaning.** In Higashimatsuyama and Muroran it is pork; in Imabari it is chicken skin pressed on a griddle. See the yakitori page.

## What people get wrong

- **"The otoshi is a scam."** It is a standard table charge, and the consumer centre tells visitors to think of it that way — but to ask if they are charged both an otoshi and a cover.
- **"You can order just drinks."** Tokyo's official site says it is usual for each person to order at least one dish; Fukuoka's stalls ask for a drink and a dish each.
- **"Tip for good service."** There is no tipping custom.
- **"The towel is for your face."** It is for your hands.
- **"Izakaya are open all day."** They are evening places; most close around midnight.
- **"Izakaya are an ancient institution."** One account dates them to the 1720s–30s at Edo's sake shops; it is one account, not a settled history.
