*Jūgoya*, the fifteenth night, is Japan’s autumn moon viewing, *tsukimi*. Its food is simple: dumplings, *tsukimi-dango*, offered to the full moon with taro, beside a decoration of pampas grass. The farm ministry explains the dumplings as a harvest offering, and records at least four places where they are made in a different shape, one of them for a reason that has to do with funerals rather than the moon. In the shops, meanwhile, *tsukimi* has become a seasonal name in its own right.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the [overview of food for annual events](/en/eat/seasonal/).

## Where it came from

The ministry’s *aff* magazine says jūgoya is the event of admiring the full moon on the 15th day of the eighth month in the old lunar calendar, which today falls between mid-September and early October. It says the event comes from China’s mid-autumn festival, *chūshū-setsu*, and is said to have reached Japan in the Heian period.

Eating dumplings is later. The same magazine says people are said to have begun eating dumplings at moon viewing in the Edo period.

Moon viewing is not one of the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*. The September one of those is *chōyō*, on 9 September. The ministry’s *washoku* culture material says ordinary people ate chestnut rice on that day, so it was also called the chestnut festival, *kuri-zekku*, and that it was dropped from the official festival days in the Meiji period and gradually forgotten.

## What makes it moon viewing

**Dumplings.** The *aff* magazine says the event coincides with the rice harvest, so dumplings made to look like the full moon were offered, giving thanks for the harvest and praying for a good harvest the following year.

**Taro and pampas grass.** The culture material says pampas grass, *susuki*, is put out as decoration, and moon-viewing dumplings and taro, *satoimo*, are offered. It does not give a separate meaning for the taro or the grass.

## How to eat it

The sources describe an offering first. The dumplings are set out for the moon, and the magazine speaks of eating dumplings at moon viewing from the Edo period. Neither page gives an order, a number of dumplings, or a way to arrange them, and this page does not add one.

## Not all of moon viewing is this

The *aff* magazine lists four regional versions:

- **Parts of Kanto.** A perfectly round dumpling recalls *makura-dango*, the dumplings placed by the pillow of someone who has died, so the dumplings are sometimes made slightly flattened.
- **Nagoya.** Three-coloured dumplings, shaped to look like taro.
- **Shizuoka.** *Heso-mochi*, "navel mochi", with a hollow pressed into the middle.
- **Okinawa.** *Fuchagi*, mochi coated with azuki beans.

The magazine gives a reason only for the Kanto shape. It does not explain the Nagoya colours, the Shizuoka hollow or the Okinawan beans, and this page does not supply reasons.

## Moon viewing in the shops

Away from the household offering, *tsukimi* is a name chains use for autumn. McDonald’s Japan’s news release of 26 August 2026 says its *Tsukimi* family of products went on sale nationwide, some shops excepted, from 2 September 2026 for a limited period, with end dates differing by item from late September to late October. It says the Tsukimi Burger dates from 1991 and marks 2026 as its 35th year. A company article from 2017 calls it an autumn standard and a traditional taste of the company. Neither page explains where the name came from or how the burger was launched. Prices and the full line-up are on the page on [autumn and winter seasonal food](/en/eat/shun-autumn-winter/).

Lawson’s dessert page, checked in October 2026, listed a *tsukimi* sweet-potato pudding among its puddings. The page does not explain the name.

The ministry’s link between the moon and the rice harvest does not appear in either company’s material. What the chains share with the old custom is the season and the word.

## What people get wrong

- **"Moon viewing is always in September."** The ministry places it between mid-September and early October, because it follows the old lunar calendar.
- **"Moon-viewing dumplings are always perfectly round."** In parts of Kanto they are sometimes slightly flattened, and Nagoya, Shizuoka and Okinawa make them differently again.
- **"Dumplings have always been part of it."** The ministry says the event reached Japan in the Heian period, and that eating dumplings is said to date from the Edo period.
- **"It is one of the five seasonal festivals."** The September festival among those is chōyō, on 9 September, which the ministry says was largely forgotten after the Meiji period.
