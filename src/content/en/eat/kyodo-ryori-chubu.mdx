    a: No. Miso-katsu is not in the farm ministry's list. The only mention I found is on the ministry's page for aburage-zushi, which says Toyokawa has devised inari-zushi topped with miso-katsu or eel.
  - q: Where did miso-nikomi udon come from?
    a: The sources tell it differently. The farm ministry says it was a home dish, served at restaurants in Ichinomiya by the Meiji era, and later spread with Nagoya at its centre. The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page says it came from the hōtō eaten by Takeda Shingen, passed on to the Tokugawa. This page gives both and does not choose.
  - q: Why is abalone a speciality of landlocked Yamanashi?
    a: The farm ministry gives two theories. Abalone from Suruga Bay, packed in soy sauce in barrels, took on flavour as it travelled by horse to Kōfu, or Takeda Shingen devised it as field food. The ministry's Yamanashi story adds a third account, a Numazu fish wholesaler sending abalone from the Izu islands in the late Edo period. This page does not choose.
  - q: Can I eat hoba miso at a hot spring?
    a: At Okuhida, yes. The Okuhida Onsen-gō tourism association says its five spa areas serve Hida beef, river fish and traditional food such as soba and hoba miso, and the farm ministry places hoba miso in Hida and Takayama, the city Okuhida belongs to.

This page takes five prefectures of Chūbu: Yamanashi, Nagano and Gifu, none of which touches the sea, and Shizuoka and Aichi on the coast. (Niigata, Toyama, Ishikawa and Fukui are on the [Hokuriku page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokuriku/), and Mie on the [Kinki page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/).) The official descriptions of their dishes keep returning to the same things: mountain land that would not grow rice, flour food in its place, bean miso, and fish carried inland. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Yamanashi

**Hōtō.** Flat wheat noodles simmered in miso with plenty of vegetables, across the prefecture. The farm ministry says the noodle has no salt in it, so it need not be boiled first, and that under an older name, written 餺飥, it is known to have been eaten by Heian-period nobles at ceremonies. In Hokuto, *azuki hōtō*, the noodles in sweet red beans, is made for New Year, Obon and village festivals. See [hōtō](/en/eat/udon-hoto/).

**Yoshida no udon.** From Fujiyoshida, 700 to 900 m up on the northern foot of Mt Fuji, where the ministry says cool weather and volcanic-ash soil did not suit rice. Its story is told as tradition: in the early Shōwa period, when weaving thrived, the men are said to have cooked the lunchtime udon so the women at the looms need not stop and their silk-handling hands would not roughen. Men kneaded hard, so the noodles became firm. On top go steamed cabbage and, if you like, horse meat simmered sweet and salty.

**Torimotsu-ni.** Chicken offal glazed quickly over high heat in soy sauce and sugar, in Kōfu. The ministry says it is said to have begun around 1950, when a butcher asked the second-generation owner of a soba shop founded in 1913 whether something could be done with offal that was being thrown away. Since 2008 a volunteer group of Kōfu city staff has promoted it.

**Awabi no nigai.** Simmered abalone, in landlocked Kōfu. The ministry gives two theories:

- **The journey**: Suruga Bay abalone, packed in soy sauce in barrels, improved as it travelled to Kōfu by horse.
- **Takeda Shingen**: he devised it as campaign food.

The ministry's Yamanashi story adds a third: in the late Edo period a Numazu fish wholesaler is said to have sent abalone from the Izu islands to Kai province on horseback. This page gives all three. It is used for gifts, New Year and weddings.

Raw horse is on the list too; see [basashi](/en/eat/meat-basashi/). Shingen-mochi is not in the farm ministry's list.

### Nagano

**Teuchi soba.** The ministry says buckwheat was grown because the cool highlands made rice and wheat hard to grow, and that morning mist at around 700 m protects the frost-tender plant. It says Nagano has more soba shops than any other prefecture. Local versions include Takatō soba in Ina, with grated daikon juice and grilled miso. See [Togakushi soba](/en/eat/soba-togakushi/). The ministry's Nagano story says that in 1983 oyaki, nozawana pickles and hand-made soba were selected as prefectural intangible folk cultural properties, when no other prefecture had done so for food.

**Oyaki.** Wheat or buckwheat dough wrapped round vegetables or bean paste, grilled or steamed. It is said to have begun in the Nishiyama area of Kamiminochi, where rice grew poorly and flour food was made once a day. The skin changes by place: rice-flour *anbo* in Sakae, *chanoko* in Iiyama. The ministry says it was a northern food that spread across the prefecture as its value for tourism grew, and that it is essential at Obon in the north.

**Gohei-mochi.** Half-pounded rice on a skewer, brushed with miso or soy sauce and grilled, from Kiso and Ina. The ministry gives several explanations for the name:

- its shape is like a *gohei*, the paper wand offered at shrines;
- a man called Gohei (or Gohē) mashed rice and ate it;
- an old man named Gohei, who came over the pass from Mino to Iida about 400 years ago, taught it.

This page gives them all. It is said to have existed by the mid-Edo period, and it follows the old "salt road", with Shiojiri as the dividing point. The usual sauce is walnut miso.

**Oshibori udon.** From Sakaki: udon dipped in the juice of grated hot *nezumi* daikon with Shinshū miso stirred in.

**Locusts and bee larvae.** The ministry says the Ina valley has a culture of eating insects — locusts, bee larvae, silkworms and *zazamushi* — once a scarce source of animal protein. People track a small black wasp back to its nest. Jars are sold at roadside stations, and the ministry says Suwa sells "grasshopper oyaki". Bee larvae have no entry of their own; they appear on the locust page.

Nozawana pickles are below, under Nozawa. Sanzoku-yaki and rōmen are not in the farm ministry's list.

### Gifu

**Hoba miso.** Home-made kōji miso grilled on a *hoba* magnolia leaf, from Hida and Takayama. The ministry says the leaf is antibacterial, and that among several theories it is said to have begun with Hida's foresters using the leaves as plates to grill miso in the hills. Souvenir sales began in the 1965–74 period, and today Hida beef, mushrooms and spring onion are grilled with it.

**Keichan.** Chicken and vegetables stir-fried in a special sauce, from Gujō, Gero and southern Takayama. It is said to have been born around 1950, modelled on the *jingisukan* eaten in parts of Gifu, and is also told to have begun with household hens that had stopped laying. Two explanations for the name: from *tonchan*, grilled pork offal, or from *keijan*, "chicken sauce". This page gives both.

**Hoba-zushi.** Vinegared rice wrapped in magnolia leaves, from southern Hida to Chūnō, a treat for the rest days after rice planting. At first, the ministry says, it was simply salted salmon from Hokuriku, vinegared and laid on top. In season from May to August.

**Ayu.** Sweetfish from the Kiso, Nagara and Ibi rivers, June to October. The ministry says cormorant fishing on the Nagara has 1,300 years of history. In December 2015 "the Nagara river ayu" was recognised as a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System.

**Hebo-meshi.** Rice with the larvae of the same black wasp, from Tōnō. The ministry says a book of 1850 already records it being eaten in Mino and Shinano.

**Funa miso.** Grilled crucian carp simmered with bean miso, coarse sugar and soybeans, from Seinō. The ministry calls bean miso "the red miso of Tōkai".

### Shizuoka

**Shizuoka oden.** Everything on skewers in a dark broth; see [oden](/en/eat/nabe-oden/). Its black *hanpen*, the ministry says, is mackerel, horse mackerel and sardine ground with skin and bone, which is why it is grey.

**Wasabi-zuke.** The ministry says wasabi growing began in the Keichō era (1596–1615) at Utōgi in Shizuoka city, and that Tokugawa Ieyasu forbade taking it out. In the mid-18th century the method reached Izu. Wasabi-zuke is said to have begun with sales inside Shizuoka station, opened in 1889.

**Tororo-jiru.** Grated wild yam with stock and miso over barley rice, the speciality of Mariko-juku on the Tōkaidō. The ministry says it appears in *Tōkaidōchū Hizakurige*, a Hiroshige print and a haiku by Bashō.

**Sakura ebi.** Every sakura shrimp landed in Japan comes from Suruga Bay, the ministry says. *Okiagari*, a sukiyaki-like hotpot, was what the fishermen ate with drink at their meetings after fishing.

**Nama-shirasu-don.** Raw whitebait on rice, from Maisaka to Shizuura, eaten with wasabi and soy. Kanagawa has its own; see the [Kantō page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/).

**Bokumeshi.** Eel and burdock mixed into rice, from Yoshida. The ministry says Yoshida's eel farms began in the Taishō era in paddies that floods had ruined for rice, and that eels too thick to sell went into staff meals; the name comes from *bokkui*, a thick stake. See [Kantō-style eel](/en/eat/unagi-kanto/).

Fujinomiya yakisoba is not in the farm ministry's list; see [Fujinomiya yakisoba](/en/eat/yakisoba-fujinomiya/).

### Aichi

**Miso-nikomi udon.** Unsalted udon simmered in Hatchō miso broth in an earthenware pot. The sources tell its origin differently:

- **The farm ministry**: a home dish, served at restaurants in Ichinomiya by the Meiji era, later centred on Nagoya.
- **The Cultural Agency's 100-year food page** (fiscal 2023, modern): from the hōtō Takeda Shingen ate, passed on to the Tokugawa.

This page gives both and does not choose. See [miso by region](/en/eat/nabe-miso/).

**Kishimen.** See [kishimen](/en/eat/udon-kishimen/).

**Miso oden and miso dengaku.** The ministry says Edo went on to simmer oden in soy broth, while Aichi kept dipping it in miso. Dengaku comes with greens rice (*nameshi dengaku*) in Mikawa and as wheat-gluten *fu dengaku* in Owari.

**Kashiwa no hikizuri.** Chicken sukiyaki, from Owari and Oku-Mikawa. The ministry says Nagoya Cochin chickens were bred in the early Meiji era, gives three explanations for the name, and says it is now mostly eaten on New Year's Eve.

**Aburage-zushi, or inari-zushi.** Rice in fried tofu pouches, centred on Toyokawa. The ministry says its origins are disputed. Nagoya's leaves the bottom open.

Hitsumabushi, tebasaki chicken wings, miso-katsu, *ankake* spaghetti, Taiwan ramen and *ogura* toast are not in the farm ministry's list. See [hitsumabushi](/en/eat/unagi-kansai/) and [miso-katsu](/en/eat/tonkatsu-miso-katsu/).

## What makes it Chūbu

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Yamanashi 30, Nagano 29, Gifu 30, Shizuoka 28, Aichi 30 — 147 in all. I counted both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages, and the counts matched. (Yamanashi's page sits at an address spelt "yamanasi".) I found no official explanation of why Nagano and Shizuoka have fewer. Gohei-mochi has separate entries in Nagano, Gifu and Aichi, and hebo-meshi in Gifu and Aichi.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them: Yamanashi 6, Nagano 12, Gifu 6, Shizuoka 6, Aichi 14 — 44 in all. Some are on both lists, among them hōtō, Yoshida no udon, torimotsu-ni, gohei-mochi, hoba-zushi, Shizuoka oden, miso-nikomi udon and kishimen. Others are only 100-year foods, among them rōmen, Fujinomiya yakisoba, Toyohashi *chikuwa* and Seto and Hekinan yakisoba. The two lists are chosen separately.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: Shingen-mochi, sanzoku-yaki, rōmen, Fujinomiya yakisoba, hitsumabushi, miso-katsu, ankake spaghetti, Taiwan ramen and ogura toast. Hida beef, eel and sakura shrimp have no entry of their own; they appear inside other dishes.

## What the five share

**Mountains and flour.** The ministry's Yamanashi story says about 80 per cent of the prefecture is forest, and that before Edo-period flood control paddies were few, so barley, millet and buckwheat were the staples. Nagano is 80 per cent mountain; Gifu more than 80 per cent forest. I found no official source linking the five prefectures' noodles to each other.

**Gohei-mochi.** Centred on Kiso and Ina, the ministry says, it also reached the mountains of Gifu, Toyama, Aichi and Shizuoka.

**Insects and river fish.** Insect eating runs from Shinshū to Oku-Mikawa and Gifu's Tōnō. Gifu's three rivers give ayu, crucian carp and catfish; Nagano has Saku carp and Lake Suwa's smelt.

**Miso.** Aichi's bean miso and tamari; Gifu's "red miso of Tōkai". Nagano ships more miso than any other prefecture (2019 industrial statistics, cited in the ministry's story).

**The sea, carried inland.** Besides the abalone, the Yamanashi story says salt came up from Suruga by Fuji river boats and tuna along the Nakamichi road. Gifu's says salted yellowtail from Hida went along a "yellowtail road" towards Shinshū.

I found no official source that describes Chūbu as one food culture.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Hōtō and Yoshida no udon**: many shops in Yamanashi.
- **Torimotsu-ni**: soba, hōtō and set-meal shops, and supermarket delis.
- **Oyaki**: across Nagano, including service areas and convenience stores.
- **Hoba-zushi**: farm stalls in Tōnō from mid-May to July, and restaurants.
- **Shizuoka oden**: by the skewer at cheap-sweet shops and delis.
- **Nama-shirasu-don**: canteens run by the fishing cooperatives.

**Prices.** I found no official dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** New soba from mid-October to early November. Nozawana best around New Year. Ayu from June to October. Sakura shrimp from mid-March to early June and late October to late December, with fishing closed in June–August. Raw whitebait from late March to mid-January. Wild yam from mid-October to December.

**The occasion.** Hōtō is everyday food, eaten more in winter. Yoshida no udon and abalone belong to celebrations. Oyaki is both festive and everyday. Keichan was for special days and is now eaten all year.

## Not all of Chūbu is this

**Between east and west.** The ministry's Shizuoka story calls the prefecture "a meeting point" of eastern and western food. Its Gifu story names Sekigahara and says the line between round and square New Year rice cakes is said to run through Gifu. I found nothing from these prefectures on the stock boundary. See [dashi east and west](/en/eat/dashi-east-west/).

**One rice cake, two shapes.** Gohei-mochi is round like dumplings north of the Nakasendō and flat like a straw sandal to the south.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Okuhida and hoba miso.** The Okuhida Onsen-gō tourism association's food page, covering Hirayu, Fukuji, Shin-Hirayu, Tochio and Shin-Hotaka, says Hida beef, river fish and mountain vegetables can be had there, along with traditional tastes such as soba, hoba miso and local sake. The ministry places hoba miso in Hida and Takayama, and Okuhida is in Takayama city. So you can eat hoba miso at the Okuhida springs. I did not check any shop.

**[Isawa Onsen](/en/soak/isawa/) and hōtō.** Fuefuki city's tourism portal presents a food map of the Isawa Onsen area alongside *rahō*, a ramen-meets-hōtō dish the city has made a new speciality. The ministry calls hōtō a dish of the whole prefecture. So you can eat hōtō around Isawa; rahō is a new creation, not a farm ministry dish, and no source on this page makes hōtō Isawa's own.

**Gero and keichan.** The ministry names Gero among keichan's home areas, without mentioning the spring; see [Gero](/en/soak/gero/).

**Nozawa and nozawana.** The ministry gives Nozawa Onsen village as the pickle's birthplace. Sown in September and picked after the November frost, it is ready in two or three weeks. See [Nozawa Onsen](/en/soak/nozawa/).

**Izu, without a link.** The ministry's Shizuoka story calls Izu rich in hot springs and, in the same section, names golden-eye snapper, dolphin in miso and wasabi-zuke, but ties no dish to a named spring. For Atami, see [Atami](/en/soak/atami/).

**Shirahone and onsen rice porridge.** The environment ministry's spa plan says rice porridge cooked in [Shirahone](/en/soak/shirahone/)'s water is called onsen-gayu and is often served at the inns' breakfasts, and the inn association calls it the town's speciality. It is not on the farm ministry's Nagano list.

**Togura and oshibori soba.** The farm ministry's oshibori udon page says Chikuma city (the former Togura town) has *oshibori soba*. No source ties it to the [Togura-Kamiyamada](/en/soak/togura-kamiyamada/) spa streets as such.

**Masutomi's food culture.** The environment ministry's spa plan lists [Masutomi](/en/soak/masutomi/)'s produce and food, among them hanamame (runner beans), miso, tofu and freeze-dried radish, as a food culture still handed down. None of these is on the farm ministry's Yamanashi list.

**No link found.** Official sources do not name a dish together with Yudanaka-Shibu, Shimobe or Aichi's hot springs.

**Getting there.** These figures come from different bodies and dates, so do not compare them:

- **Kōfu**: about 90 minutes from Shinjuku by JR Chūō line limited express (JNTO).
- **Matsumoto**: 2 hours 40 minutes from Shinjuku, 2 hours from Nagoya (JNTO).
- **Gifu**: about 20 minutes by train from Nagoya; the JR Takayama line runs on to Gero and Takayama (JNTO).
- **Okuhida**: by train and bus, from 3 hours 30 minutes from Nagoya (tourism association).
- **Shizuoka**: an hour or less from Tokyo to Atami or Shizuoka by Tōkaidō shinkansen (JNTO).

## Chūbu food away from its home

- **Nagano.** Ginza NAGANO, 5-6-5 Ginza, Chūō-ku, a minute's walk from Ginza station; shop 10:30–19:00, says its site. The ministry says *sunki* pickles now reach Tokyo antenna shops.
- **Gifu.** Gifu Tokyo, in Hibiya OKUROJI. The only hours I found, 11:00–20:00, were for an event in February 2026. The prefecture lists it among places selling Hida beef and keichan.
- **Shizuoka.** "Oishizu", a corner inside Nihon Hyakkaten Shokuhinkan near JR Akihabara station, 11:00–20:00, with tea and sakura shrimp, per a prefectural article of July 2024.
- **Yamanashi.** Chūō ward's list of antenna shops (7 October 2026) has none, and I could not confirm the state of the former Tokyo shop.
- **Aichi.** I found no official source for a Tokyo shop.

**What changes.** At the source these dishes have seasons and settings; the shops are open all year. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Miso-katsu is a listed Aichi dish."** Miso-katsu is not in the farm ministry's list; miso oden and miso-nikomi udon are.
- **"Hoba miso is a Gero dish."** The ministry places it in Hida and Takayama.
- **"Fujinomiya yakisoba is on Shizuoka's list."** It is not in the farm ministry's list; it is a 100-year food.
- **"Miso-nikomi udon has one origin story."** The ministry and the 100-year food page tell it differently.
