Some Japanese food is eaten on a date rather than in a season. A box of *osechi* at New Year, a sushi roll eaten facing one direction in early February, a clam soup in March, pumpkin at the winter solstice, buckwheat noodles on New Year’s Eve. The farm ministry calls these *gyōji-shoku*, food for annual events, and nearly every one of them comes with a reason attached. This page is the map of the year. The lineage pages go deeper on the events with the most to say.

## What it is

The farm ministry’s children’s food-education page defines *gyōji-shoku* as the special dishes eaten on seasonal events and celebration days, and says they carry wishes for the family’s happiness and health.

The backbone of the calendar, according to the ministry’s *washoku* culture material, is the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*:

- **Jinjitsu**, 7 January
- **Jōshi**, 3 March
- **Tango**, 5 May
- **Tanabata**, 7 July
- **Chōyō**, 9 September

The same material says these came from China between the Nara and Heian periods, together with Chinese customs and the calendar, and were merged with Japan’s own observances. It describes them as days of *shinjin kyōshoku*: festive food was made and offered to the gods, and people gathered to eat together with the gods.

Around those five sit the rest: New Year, Setsubun, the equinoxes, Obon, the autumn moon, Shichi-go-san, the winter solstice, New Year’s Eve, and Christmas.

## Why it is the way it is

Three patterns run through the explanations the official sources give.

**Wordplay.** A great many meanings rest on sound. In osechi, *kobu-maki* (rolled kelp) is linked to *yorokobu*, to be glad. At the winter solstice, foods with the sound "n" in their names, such as *nankin*, another name for pumpkin, are said to bring *un*, luck. The ministry also records that bean-throwing at Setsubun is said to come from a pun on "destroying the demon’s eye" (*ma-me*).

**Warding things off.** Red is described as driving away evil. Azuki beans are said to do so at the solstice, and the smell and smoke of grilled sardines are said to do so at Setsubun.

**One food, several stories.** The sources often give more than one meaning for the same dish, and sometimes the ministry’s own pages disagree. Sōmen is the clearest case. For Tanabata the ministry’s culture material links it to *sakubei*, a fried wheat confection eaten to avoid illness and said to be the forerunner of sōmen. For Obon an *aff* magazine article says the noodles stand for the reins of the horse the ancestors ride. Those are two different explanations for one food, and this site does not choose between them.

## The year, season by season

**New Year.** Osechi, the boxed New Year food, is set out with three *iwai-zakana* (black beans, tazukuri and herring roe), each with its own meaning. On 7 January comes *nanakusa-gayu*, rice porridge with seven spring herbs. Mochi and zōni, the New Year soup with mochi in it, are covered in the overview of rice and what goes with it.

**Setsubun, around 3 February.** Roasted soybeans thrown and eaten, sardines in parts of the country, and the sushi roll called *ehōmaki*, whose origin the ministry says is uncertain.

**Hinamatsuri, 3 March.** Scattered sushi, clam soup, three-coloured diamond mochi and *hina-arare*, which differ between Kanto and Kansai.

**The equinoxes.** At *higan*, the ministry’s culture material says, graves are visited and *botamochi* (also called *ohagi*) and dumplings are offered. I did not find an official explanation of their meaning.

**Tango, 5 May.** *Kashiwa-mochi* and *chimaki*. The ministry explains kashiwa-mochi through the oak leaf: the old leaf does not fall until the new bud appears, so it stands for life passing from parent to child to grandchild. In eastern Shimane, the ministry’s traditional-food guide says, rice-flour dumplings wrapped in bamboo leaves are eaten on the old-calendar Tango, 5 June.

**Tanabata, 7 July, and Obon, mid-August.** Sōmen in both, for different reasons, as above. For Obon the ministry describes *shōjin ryōri*, Buddhist meals without meat or fish, and a list of regional Obon foods. Sōmen itself has its own overview.

**The autumn moon.** *Jūgoya*, the full moon of the old eighth month, now falls between mid-September and early October. Moon-viewing dumplings are offered in thanks for the harvest. The ministry lists several local shapes. Chōyō on 9 September was once called the "chestnut festival" because chestnut rice was eaten, but the culture material says it was dropped from the official holidays in the Meiji era and faded.

**Shichi-go-san, 15 November.** The culture material mentions only that the day is celebrated with *chitose-ame* sweets or red rice. I could not find an official explanation of chitose-ame.

**Winter solstice, around 22 December.** Pumpkin, and pumpkin simmered with azuki beans (*itoko-ni*). The ministry pages give several reasons side by side: the "n" sound brings luck; pumpkin harvested in summer keeps, and has nutrients when vegetables are scarce in winter; and because it keeps, it was also linked to a long life. The ministry’s posters mention a bath with yuzu citrus, but I found no official explanation of why.

**Christmas.** Not one of the traditional festivals, but it has foods of its own in Japan. Kentucky Fried Chicken Japan’s company history says its first shop opened in Nagoya in November 1970 and its first Christmas campaign ran in December 1974. Its recruitment page tells the story of a store manager who dressed as Santa Claus to visit local kindergartens, and says the "Christmas means Kentucky" message grew from that. The Party Barrel went on sale in 1985, according to a company news release. Fujiya’s company timeline records a Christmas cake going on sale in December 1910; it does not say what the cake was, and it does not claim it was the first in Japan. Other stories about how chicken came to stand in for turkey I could not confirm, and have left out.

**New Year’s Eve.** *Toshikoshi soba*. The ministry’s *aff* magazine says the habit spread among ordinary people in the Edo period and gives three meanings side by side: the long thin shape for a long life, the noodles breaking easily to cut off the year’s hardships, and buckwheat’s toughness in wind and rain for good health. Soba has its own overview.

## Where to eat it

I have no shops to list. The one food whose route into shops is documented is ehōmaki. The ministry says ehōmaki was not widely known before the war, that the nori trade promoted it from around the 1970s, and that it spread nationwide after convenience stores and supermarkets took it up in the 1990s. For Japanese confectionery in general, start from the wagashi overview.

## The same name, a different place

The clearest case in the official sources is *hina-arare*, the puffed rice snack of Hinamatsuri. The ministry says it was originally made by breaking up diamond mochi, and that in Kansai it is round and salty, while in Kanto it is rice-grain shaped and sweet. The ministry does not say why. The Hinamatsuri page has more.

Other events vary inside Japan rather than between a home town and elsewhere: the shape of moon-viewing dumplings, the name and broth of New Year’s Eve soba, and the foods of Obon all change by region. I could not find an official source comparing how any of these foods is eaten where it began and how it is eaten in Tokyo or elsewhere.

## What people get wrong

- **"Each food has one meaning."** The ministry itself gives two readings of osechi’s black beans, two of Setsubun sardines, and different stories for sōmen at Tanabata and at Obon.
- **"Ehōmaki is an ancient custom."** The ministry says its origin is uncertain, lists more than one theory, and dates its national spread to the 1990s.
- **"Osechi was always New Year food."** The ministry says the word once meant the food offered at all five seasonal festivals, and only later came to mean the New Year food alone.
- **"Christmas cake started in 1910."** Fujiya’s timeline records a Christmas cake going on sale that December. It does not call it the first, and does not describe it.
