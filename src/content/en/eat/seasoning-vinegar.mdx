Vinegar sits third in *sa-shi-su-se-so*, the memory aid for the five basic Japanese seasonings, where *su* is vinegar. It is in sushi rice, in the dressing on seared bonito, in the dipping sauce for a Fukuoka chicken hotpot. It is also, by the farm ministry's account, the oldest seasoning of all. This page is about where the official sources say Japanese vinegar came from, the kinds they list, how a cheap vinegar from sake lees spread with Edo sushi, and how it is used around the country.

For seasonings in general, and how soy sauce is brewed, start from the [seasoning overview](/en/eat/seasoning/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods tells the story in four steps.

**Before Japan.** Vinegar is considered humanity's oldest seasoning. Its use began, the ministry says, because sake left over time turns sour.

**Arrival.** It is thought to have reached Japan around the time of Emperor Ōjin. The ministry gives no year and frames it as "said to", and this page keeps it that way.

**At court.** The Taihō Code of 701 set up the *Miki no Tsukasa*, an office that made sake, *amazake* and vinegar, as a government post. Vinegar and sake were made side by side from the start of the written record.

**The Edo turn.** In the later Edo period, the ministry says, a vinegar made from sake lees, *kasuzu*, was devised. It was cheaper than rice vinegar, its flavour matched well, and it spread together with the fashion for Edo-style sushi.

The ministry places vinegar with miso, soy sauce and mirin among the fermented seasonings made from grain that are familiar in Japan, and says mirin is made, like vinegar, by applying sake-brewing methods.

## What Japanese vinegar is

The ministry's consumer page defines vinegar as a sour seasoning whose main component is acetic acid, and splits it in two.

- **Brewed vinegar (*jōzōsu*).** Grain vinegar, black vinegar and fruit vinegar.
- **Synthetic vinegar (*gōseisu*).** Made in small amounts, mostly for commercial use such as pickles.

The page lists the kinds; it does not rank them or say which is used most at home. Rice vinegar, which the ministry's history compares with *kasuzu*, is a grain vinegar.

## Vinegar and Edo sushi

The two stories run together. The National Diet Library's history says fermented sushi was sold in Edo in the late 17th century, vinegared "quick sushi" (*haya-zushi*) appeared in the Hōreki era (1751–64), and hand-pressed nigiri is thought to date from before the Bunsei era (1818–31). The library's account sets vinegared sushi after the fermented kind, so in the record the sourness first came from fermentation and only later from vinegar.

The farm ministry's Tokyo page lists the work of Edo-style sushi as curing (*shimeru*), simmering, steaming, marinating in soy, pressing in kelp and washing in vinegar (*su-arai*), and names red vinegar (*akazu*) among its marks. The sources this page uses do not say whether red vinegar and sake-lees vinegar are the same thing, so they are kept apart here. The [Edo-style sushi page](/en/eat/sushi-edomae/) describes red vinegar as made from rice lees; that is its wording, not something the farm ministry's vinegar entry states.

Sugar plays a part too. The farm ministry's magazine *aff* says sugar holds water and keeps vinegared rice from going hard. For the dish itself, see the [sushi overview](/en/eat/sushi/); for fish cured in vinegar, see [kobujime and shimesaba](/en/eat/sashimi-shime/).

## Not all of Japan's vinegar is this

Vinegar turns up in regional food mostly as part of a dressing. Three examples from official pages:

- **Kōchi, bonito tataki.** The farm ministry says the dressing changes across the prefecture: central Kōchi uses *nihaizu*, two-part vinegar of soy sauce and brewed vinegar; the west mainly soy mixed with mirin and the like; the east soy cut with yuzu juice; the mountain areas *sanbaizu*, three-part vinegar. See [mackerel, bonito and tuna](/en/eat/seafood-saba-katsuo-maguro/).
- **Fukuoka, mizutaki.** The ministry's Fukuoka page says the chicken hotpot can be eaten with a dressing of rice vinegar, soy sauce, *daidai* citrus vinegar and mirin, with condiments as you like. See [mizutaki](/en/eat/chicken-mizutaki/).
- **Kagoshima, black vinegar.** "Kagoshima's pot-brewed black vinegar" (*tsubozukuri kurozu*) is one of the Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, certified in fiscal 2021 (Reiwa 3) in the traditional category. The agency's list names it; the page used here gives no method or history, so none is given.

Many local sushi rest on vinegared rice as well; see [persimmon-leaf sushi](/en/eat/sushi-kakinoha/) and the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Vinegar and raw fish

Vinegar is part of curing fish, and it is easy to assume it also makes raw fish safe. The health ministry says the opposite for anisakis, a parasite of fish: the vinegar, salting, soy sauce and wasabi used in ordinary cooking do not kill the larvae. Freezing at -20 °C for 24 hours or more, or heating to 70 °C (or 60 °C for a minute), does. The [sashimi overview](/en/eat/sashimi/) has the rest.

## What people get wrong

- **"Japanese vinegar is one product."** The ministry lists grain, black and fruit vinegar as brewed kinds, plus synthetic vinegar.
- **"Edo sushi used expensive vinegar."** The ministry says the sake-lees vinegar that spread with it was cheaper than rice vinegar.
- **"Sushi was always vinegared."** The older fermented sushi took its sourness from fermentation; vinegared quick sushi came in the 1750s.
- **"Vinegar kills parasites in raw fish."** The health ministry says it does not kill anisakis.
