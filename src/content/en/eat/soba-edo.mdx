Soba did not start in Edo, but Edo is where it acquired manners. The cold plate on a bamboo tray, the small cup of black sauce, the rule about dipping only half, the cooking water at the end, the three family names that still sort the city's shops — all of that is the capital's work, and most of it dates from the hundred years before and after 1800. The same city then produced, in the 1960s, the opposite institution: a counter where you stand and eat a hot bowl in four minutes for the price of a coffee. Both are Tokyo soba.

## Where it came from

The noodle itself is first recorded in Nagano in 1574; by the eighteenth century it was a city food in Edo, sold from stalls and shops. The houses that still define it date from the end of that century onward. Sarashina Horii's founding family puts its origin at 1789: a cloth merchant from Shinshū who turned to noodles, set up near his lord's Edo mansion in Azabu, and hung out a sign reading "Shinshū Sarashina soba, Nunoya Tahei" — "Sarashina" being a play on the Shinshū soba district of Sarashina with one character borrowed, by permission, from the lord's name. Its signature was and is the white noodle of first-milled flour. The Sunaba name is older than Edo: it comes from the sand yard of Hideyoshi's Osaka castle site, where a soba shop prospered among the builders in the 1580s; the Tokyo house, Muromachi Sunaba, dates from 1869 and claims to have invented serving tempura with cold soba. Kanda Yabu, the third name, was founded in 1880, rebuilt after a fire and reopened in 2014.

The standing counter is the postwar chapter. Dan Michio opened Soba Kiyo, the forerunner of Fuji Soba, in 1966, founded Daitan Food in 1972 to concentrate on standing soba, and ran 24-hour counters in Shibuya, Shinjuku, Ikebukuro and Nishi-Ogikubo. Fuji Soba has 104 shops as of autumn 2026 and switches them all to new-crop Tochigi buckwheat each October.

## What makes it Edo soba

**The ratio.** *Ni-hachi*: 80% buckwheat, 20% wheat, which gives a noodle that holds together and still tastes of buckwheat. The food-industry press is firm that the name is the ratio and not the Edo price of sixteen mon, whatever the guidebooks say.

**The sauce.** *Kaeshi* — soy sauce aged with sugar and mirin — cut with bonito stock into a dark, salty, concentrated dip. It is strong because it is meant to meet only part of the noodle.

**The plates.** *Mori* and *zaru* are both cold soba on a tray. The national noodle association's officer says that until recently the zaru came with a richer sauce made from better bonito; now the difference is usually nori on the zaru and sometimes portion size. The old houses keep their own rules — Kanda Matsuya serves its mori without wasabi or nori — and Sunaba's *tenzaru* and *tenmori* put a plate of tempura beside it.

**The manners.** The association's instruction, written for children: dip the noodle about halfway, so that you taste the soba itself; draw it in with a sound. Then *soba-yu*, the water the noodles cooked in, arrives in a lacquer pot to be poured into the leftover sauce and drunk.

## Why Edo

A city of single men, labourers and officials who needed cheap, quick food, in the east of Japan where buckwheat grew on cold uplands within a day of the city; a soy-sauce industry on the Chiba coast producing the dark *koikuchi* that makes the sauce black; and a bonito trade that supplied the stock. The temple town of Jindaiji, now within Tokyo in Chōfu, records its own version: farmers paid the temple in buckwheat flour, the priests made it into noodles, and the shogun Iemitsu is said to have eaten it on a hawking trip. The association there dates the local tradition to the early nineteenth century, citing the Edo-period gazetteer.

Prices at the houses, from their own menus:

- **Sarashina Horii**, Azabu-Jūban (founded 1789). All prices tax included: mori ¥1,000, sarashina (the white noodle) ¥1,100, thick-cut ¥1,600, kake ¥1,000, tempura soba ¥2,310 with one prawn and ¥3,600 with two, kakiage tenmori ¥2,400, duck seiro ¥2,400.
- **Kanda Yabu Soba** (founded 1880). Seiro ¥1,320, kake ¥1,320, tempura soba ¥2,860, duck seiro ¥3,190; the menu does not state whether tax is included.
- **Muromachi Sunaba** (1869) and **Kanda Matsuya** (1884) do not publish prices in a form I could verify.
- **Standing counters.** Fuji Soba kake ¥450, Yudetarō ¥430, Komoro Soba ¥390 (tax not stated); with kakiage ¥550–620.

The statistical plate — "Japanese soba, eating out, one plate", central Tokyo — was ¥788 in August 2026. Tokyo households rank 22nd of 52 cities for noodles eaten out, at ¥8,166 a year.

## How to eat it

1. Order the cold plate — *mori* or *seiro* — on a first visit. Hot soba is for winter and for the standing counter.
2. Put a little wasabi and spring onion in the cup, or on the noodle, not both at once.
3. Lift a few strands, dip the bottom half, slurp. Do not stir the plate into the cup.
4. When the plate is empty the *soba-yu* arrives. Pour it into the cup and drink.
5. At a standing counter: buy a ticket, hand it over, stand, eat, leave. Four minutes is normal.

## Edo soba away from Edo

Tokyo's soba is the one that got exported as "soba", so what changes is less the dish than what people elsewhere assume about it. Three things are worth knowing. The buckwheat is mostly not Japanese: the country imports about 88,000 tonnes a year against 38,000 grown at home, three-quarters of the imports from China, so a Tokyo plate is usually Chinese buckwheat cut by a Japanese hand. The ratio is not fixed: the old houses' *ni-hachi* is 80:20, and the standing chains do not publish theirs. And the manners are Tokyo's: Izumo pours the sauce over the noodle, Togakushi serves it in bundles, and nobody outside the capital will mind how deep you dip.

## Getting there

Sarashina Horii is a few minutes from Azabu-Jūban station (Namboku and Ōedo lines); Kanda Yabu is near Awajichō and Ogawamachi stations; Muromachi Sunaba near Mitsukoshimae. The standing counters are at the stations themselves.

## What people get wrong

- **"Zaru is the proper one."** Zaru has nori. That is now most of the difference.
- **"Dunk it all."** Half. It is the one rule.
- **"The sauce is a soup."** It is a dip, and then — with soba-yu — a drink.
- **"Ni-hachi is a price."** It is a ratio.
- **"Tokyo buckwheat."** Mostly Chinese, by the ministry's figures.
