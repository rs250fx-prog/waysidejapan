Visitors to Japan rank beef the most satisfying thing they eat here — ahead of sushi by a factor of two in the tourism agency's survey — and almost none of them could say what the word on the sign means. "Wagyu" is a legal category, "A5" is a trading grade, "Kobe" is a certification with a bronze statue, and none of the three is a promise about how dinner will taste. This page is the glossary, the price map, and the way in to the brands and the two great ways of eating it, sukiyaki and yakiniku.

## What it is

**Wagyu is a breed list.** Four Japanese breeds — Japanese Black (*Kuroge washu*), Japanese Brown, Japanese Shorthorn, Japanese Polled — and crosses between them, born and raised in Japan. Japanese Black is more than 90% of the total, and it is the breed with the genetic tendency to marble, which is the whole point. Beef from Holstein or Holstein–wagyu crosses raised in Japan is *kokusan-gyū*, domestic beef — honest meat, not wagyu. And beef from wagyu genetics exported to Australia and America in the 1970s–90s is "WAGYU" abroad; JETRO's position is that it differs in raising and quality, and it cannot be re-imported and sold as wagyu in Japan. Japan has about 2.47 million beef cattle on 31,800 farms, both numbers falling; it exported ¥73 billion of beef in 2025, mostly to the US, Taiwan and Hong Kong; and its beef self-sufficiency is 40% by weight and 6% once imported feed is counted.

**A5 is two numbers for traders.** The Japan Meat Grading Association grades every carcass twice. The letter is yield: A means 72% or more of the carcass becomes saleable meat, B 69–72%, C less. The number is quality, 1 to 5, and it is the *lowest* of four marks — marbling, meat colour, firmness and texture, fat colour and quality. Marbling itself is scored 1 to 12 (the Beef Marbling Standard); a 5 needs BMS 8 or above, a 4 needs 5–7, a 3 needs 3–4. The association describes the system as "a yardstick for carcass trade" so that producers and buyers can deal fairly. It does not describe it as a measure of flavour, and this site has not found a public body that does. A5 means a lot of fat, evenly spread, in a carcass that butchers well. Whether you want that much fat is your business.

**Every animal has a number.** Under the traceability law each beef animal carries a ten-digit ID from ear tag to shop counter, and restaurants serving yakiniku, shabu-shabu, sukiyaki or steak are required to display it. Anyone can look it up on the livestock centre's site and see the birth date, breed, sex and farms. If a shop will not show you the number, that tells you something.

## Why it is the way it is

**Japan ate beef late, and from the top down.** The brands' own histories start with foreigners: Kobe's name comes from the port opened in 1868 and the Englishmen who first bought Tajima cattle there; Yonezawa's from an English teacher who took a cow home to Yokohama in 1875; Ōmi's from the Hikone domain, the one place allowed to produce beef in the Edo period, which sent miso-cured beef to the shogun as medicine from 1687. The Meiji emperor's publicised beef meal in January 1872 made it respectable, and the Tokyo beef-hotpot shops went from one to 550 in a decade. A food introduced as a prestige import stayed a prestige food, and the grading system, the brands and the prices all follow from that.

**The brands are rules, and the rules differ.** Kobe is the strictest: Tajima cattle only, Hyōgo-born and raised, 28–60 months, grade A or B 4-and-up with BMS 6-and-up, a weight band, about 7,000 head a year, under 0.2% of Japan's beef, sold only through registered shops that display a bronze cow. Matsusaka is defined by place and sex — virgin heifers fattened longest in a defined area, with no grade requirement for the basic name. Ōmi requires A4/B4. Yonezawa requires grade 3. Several are now registered Geographical Indications. The ministry's own GI documents call Ōmi and Yonezawa "often counted among the three great wagyu" — a phrase with no official definition, which the ministry records as a thing people say.

**The menu names are not the cuts.** In 2010 the Consumer Affairs Agency asked the yakiniku trade to stop selling other cuts as "rōsu". The industry's defence is worth knowing: yakiniku is a postwar dish whose first menus had only *rōsu* and *karubi*, so the words settled as menu categories rather than anatomy, and *karubi* is simply Korean for rib. The trade is now stricter, but a yakiniku menu is still a menu, not a butcher's chart.

## What it costs

The spread is the widest in Japanese eating, because the same animal is sold five ways:

- **The brand steak.** At Mōriya in Kobe (founded 1885), lunch runs from ¥8,020 for 120 g of round to ¥21,780 for 130 g of A5 sirloin; dinner courses from ¥9,090. Asakusa's Matsunami is ¥6,600 for a wagyu sirloin lunch and ¥8,800 for its cheapest wagyu dinner course, plus 10% service.
- **The sukiyaki house.** Ningyōchō Imahan's sukiyaki set is ¥13,530 for the top grade plus 12% service; the 1868 Yokohama beef-hotpot house charges ¥8,200. The chains do the same dish for ¥1,430–3,190.
- **The buffet.** Gyūkaku's all-you-can-eat courses are ¥3,058–3,718 and Yakiniku King's ¥3,278–3,828; Jojoen's lunch set is ¥3,200. This is how most Japanese people eat beef out.
- **The pack.** A certified Ōmi shop sells 500 g of sukiyaki beef for ¥4,320 to ¥10,800 depending on grade; Matsusaka's oldest house sells 300 g of its sukiyaki cut for ¥7,050.
- **The number on the ticket.** Fukuoka's Jojoen charges ¥8,000 for a weekday course with service included; a Kobe registered restaurant in Tokyo will charge Tokyo rent on top of Kobe certification. There are 147 registered Kobe beef outlets in Tokyo against 408 in Hyōgo.

## Where to eat it

- **Kobe** — the registered houses around Sannomiya; look for the bronze cow.
- **Matsusaka** — Wadakin, the house that made the name, in a town an hour from Nagoya.
- **Ōmi** — Hikone and Ōmi-Hachiman, on the Biwako line from Kyoto.
- **Sukiyaki** — Ningyōchō and Asakusa in Tokyo, Teramachi in Kyoto, Yokohama for the 1868 original.
- **Yakiniku** — Tsuruhashi in Osaka for the postwar original; Shin-Ōkubo in Tokyo; any suburb for the buffet chains.

## What people get wrong

- **"Wagyu means Japanese beef."** It means four breeds. Domestic beef from other breeds is sold under a different word.
- **"A5 is the best."** A5 is the fattiest and the best-yielding, by a system built for traders. The association does not claim it measures taste.
- **"Kobe is a kind of beef."** It is a certification on Tajima cattle that pass a grade, sold only through registered shops. About 7,000 animals a year.
- **"Rōsu is loin."** On a yakiniku menu it is a menu name, by the trade's own account to the regulator.
- **"The brand guarantees the restaurant."** It guarantees the animal. The cooking, the cut and the rent are the restaurant's, and the ID number on the wall is the only part you can check.
