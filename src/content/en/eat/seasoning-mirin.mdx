Mirin is the sweet one on the Japanese seasoning shelf. It is not in *sa-shi-su-se-so*, the memory aid for the five basic seasonings, yet it is in the sauce on grilled eel, the dipping sauce for cold noodles and a long list of local dishes. It is also, in law, an alcoholic drink. This page is about how the official sources say mirin is made, the two stories of where it came from, what it does in cooking, and where visitors will meet it.

For seasonings in general, and how soy sauce is brewed, start from the [seasoning overview](/en/eat/seasoning/).

## Where it came from

The farm ministry's guide to traditional foods gives two origin stories side by side. This site does not choose between them.

- **A Japanese sweet drink.** Mirin began with *nerizake*, a sweet sake of the early Muromachi period.
- **A Chinese honey liquor.** Mirin came from *mitsurin*, a honey liquor of Ming China.

On what followed, the ministry gives a single account. In the Edo period a technique for making a sugar-rich mirin with shōchū was established, and mirin came to be used as a seasoning as well. The ministry gives no year for either step.

## How mirin is made

The ministry says mirin, like vinegar, is made by applying the methods of sake brewing. The ingredients are rice koji, steamed glutinous rice, and shōchū or another distilled spirit.

1. Steamed rice and rice koji are put into the shōchū.
2. The mash is left at 20–30 °C for 40 to 60 days, while it turns sweet (saccharification) and matures.
3. It is pressed and filtered.
4. It is stored for several months.

## What it does in a pan

The ministry lists five jobs:

- **Sweetness.** It adds a sweet taste.
- **Soaking in.** It helps flavour soak into the food.
- **Shine.** It gives a glaze and gloss (*teri*, *tsuya*).
- **Holding shape.** It keeps simmered food from falling apart.
- **Smell.** It masks unwanted smells.

These are the ministry's words for what mirin does in cooking; they are not claims about health.

## Mirin is an alcoholic drink

The National Tax Agency's table of liquor types under the Liquor Tax Act sorts drinks into four groups. Mirin is in the "mixed" group (*konseishu*), with synthetic sake, sweet fruit wine, liqueurs, powdered alcohol and miscellaneous liquor. The legal definition of mirin is not set out here; the [drinks overview](/en/eat/drinks/) explains the four groups.

That matters to some visitors. The Japan Tourism Agency's Muslim guidebook says many Muslims do not take alcohol, mirin included, and that whether to avoid the small amounts of alcohol in soy sauce and similar seasonings is debated and differs from person to person. For a no-alcohol meal, its practical volume says, leave out cooking sake and mirin as well as sake and beer. It notes, as examples, restaurants that use sugar, honey or apple juice in place of mirin. JNTO's convenience-store guide for Muslim visitors likewise suggests checking rice-ball labels for mirin; see [onigiri](/en/eat/conbini-onigiri/).

## Mirin and the eel sauce

One dish where the sources trace mirin's arrival is grilled eel. They differ in the steps.

- **The farm ministry's Tokyo page.** Eel was first seasoned with salt, then with soy sauce as soy spread, and later with a sauce of soy, sugar and mirin.
- **Kikkoman's food-culture journal.** Sake was first added to soy, then mirin, which improved the flavour, aroma and gloss.
- **Kibun, a food company.** As mirin production grew in Kantō, around Nagareyama in Chiba, a sauce combining mirin and soy sauce spread.

The three are given side by side; the company account is the only one that names a place. For the dish, see [Kantō-style eel](/en/eat/unagi-kanto/).

## Not all of Japan's mirin is this

Mirin turns up across regional food, usually in a sauce. Examples from official pages:

- **Nara, sōmen.** The Miwa sōmen union gives sauce ratios: for cold sōmen, dashi 2, light soy ½, mirin ½; for hot *nyūmen*, dashi 5, light soy ½, mirin ⅓. See [Miwa sōmen](/en/eat/somen-miwa/).
- **Ehime, tai-meshi.** In southern Ehime, the farm ministry says, raw sea bream is marinated in soy sauce, mirin, egg, sesame and stock and spooned over hot rice. See [Setouchi local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/).
- **Ōita, ryūkyū.** Fresh fish dressed in soy sauce, sake, mirin, sesame and ginger. See [Kyūshū local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/).
- **Kōchi, bonito tataki.** The ministry says western Kōchi dresses it mainly with soy mixed with mirin and the like, where central Kōchi uses two-part vinegar. See [mackerel, bonito and tuna](/en/eat/seafood-saba-katsuo-maguro/).
- **Dried fish.** The ministry's guide to dried foods lists *mirin-boshi* among seasoned dried fish. See [himono](/en/eat/seafood-himono/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Mirin has one known origin."** The farm ministry gives two, a Muromachi sweet drink and a Ming honey liquor.
- **"Mirin is just sweetened seasoning, not alcohol."** The tax agency's table lists it among the mixed liquors.
- **"Mirin is one of the five basic seasonings."** *Sa-shi-su-se-so* is sugar, salt, vinegar, soy sauce and miso.
- **"Eel sauce was always made with mirin."** The ministry and Kikkoman both describe mirin as a later addition.
