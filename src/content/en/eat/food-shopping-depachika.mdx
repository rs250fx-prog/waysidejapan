The *depachika* — from *depāto*, department store, and *chika*, basement — is the food floor of a Japanese department store. To a visitor it looks like a market for dinner. The sales figures say it is just as much a shop for gifts: on the national numbers for August 2026, department stores sold more sweets than prepared dishes. This page is about where the food floor came from, what is known about how it works, and what the record does not say.

For ready-made food in Japan in general, start from the buying-food overview.

## Where it came from

The clearest early record is Hankyu’s, in Osaka, and it begins with a railway company. On the company’s own history pages:

- **1920** — Hankyu Railway opened a directly run restaurant, the Umeda Hankyu Shokudō, on the second floor of the Hankyu Building, promising cheap, clean and good food, with no tipping.
- **1925** — On 1 June it opened the Hankyu Market on the building’s second and third floors. The company says customers crowded in from the day it opened; it was the forerunner of the department store.
- **1929** — On 15 April the Hankyu Department Store opened, which the company describes as the world’s first terminal department store directly connected to a railway station.
- **1936** — On 21 March the store reopened fully rebuilt, 56,200 square metres, which the company called the largest department store in the East. Its restaurant was enlarged into the biggest in Japan, and by July an average of about 45,000 people a day came. In April that year a separate company, Sannomiya Shokuryōhin-ten (Sannomiya Food Shop), was set up in the basement of the Hankyu Kobe Kaikan, and it became the forerunner of the Kobe Hankyu market. The Kobe branch opened on 11 April.

What these pages do not say is when the Umeda store’s food hall became a basement floor, or when the word *depachika* came into use. I saw dates for both only in a search summary of a page I could not open, so they are not given here. I also could not find a history page from the Japan Department Stores Association.

What can be said is that the food floor and the railway grew up together. JR West’s 2026 research report on ekiben notes that as department stores opened at stations, the *depachika* appeared in the station itself and became one of the station bento’s competitors.

## What makes it a depachika

**A gift shop as much as a grocer.** The Japan Department Stores Association’s August 2026 figures (without consumption tax) put food at 25.2% of department store sales. Sweets were 8.7%, prepared dishes (*sōzai*) 6.2%, other food 6.2% and fresh food 4.0%, the last falling for the 29th month in a row. The association’s explanation: food held up on demand for small gifts over Obon and the summer holidays, and for gifts taken home to family, while everyday buying was weak because of rising prices. Why gifts are given the way they are in Japan, I could not find set out in an official source.

Compare a supermarket, where in 2025 prepared food of every kind (bento, onigiri, sushi and in-store bakery included) was 11.3% of sales, and fresh produce, fish and meat together were about a third.

**Range.** JNTO’s 2011 article describes food floors at major stores such as Isetan, Odakyu, Seibu and Takashimaya, with the biggest carrying about 30,000 items: sushi, tempura, tonkatsu, *wagashi*, eel and okonomiyaki through to fresh food, wagyu, imported cheese, wine and sweets. Some counters cook in small kitchens on the floor. That article is from 2011, so the numbers may have changed.

**Sold by weight, under the measurement law.** Some deli food is priced by weight. The trade ministry’s guidance on the Measurement Act counts weighing goods to put a price on them, or to sell vegetables at "so many yen per so many grams", as measurement for a transaction, and scales used for transactions must have passed official inspection. That guidance is written mainly for users of automatic scales, so I would not stretch it into a description of every counter. I could not confirm a current per-100 g price from a store’s own site, so none is given.

**Samples.** Both of JNTO’s guides say some counters offer free samples.

**Events.** JNTO’s 2011 article mentions *bussanten*, fairs of regional specialities held in the store. A related department store event, the ekiben fair, is on the ekiben page.

## How to shop it

JNTO’s current guide suggests:

- avoiding the lunch and dinner rush;
- looking out for samples;
- choosing a ready-made bento to take away;
- Daimaru’s sweets as gifts or souvenirs, and a bottle of sake to take home.

On closing time, JNTO’s budget-travel article says a visit to the basement food floor around closing is an affordable way to try it. The same article says food shops may discount perishable food in their last two or three hours, and that this does not always happen. I could not find an official explanation from department stores of whether, when or how much they discount.

Prices are not given on this page. I could not confirm current tax-included prices for deli items from the stores’ own sites.

## Not all of Japan’s ready-made food is this

- **Supermarkets**, where food is the main business; prepared food there was 11.3% of sales in 2025 on the industry survey.
- **Station shops and ekiben** grew up separately and now compete with the food floor inside the same stations.
- **Convenience stores** have their own overview page.

## Depachika away from home

JNTO’s 2011 article contrasts the two big cities: Tokyo’s food floors relatively calm (busy at weekends), Osaka’s more like a market, with staff calling out to shoppers. It is a 2011 description of atmosphere, not a measurement.

The food floor has also moved closer to the traveller. JNTO’s current guide notes that food floors are often connected to stations, and JR West’s report describes them opening into stations. In the other direction, the department store is where many regional foods come to the city: the ekiben fair began in a department store, Osaka Takashimaya, in 1953.

## What people get wrong

- **"Depachika is mainly dinner."** Sweets outsold prepared dishes in department stores in August 2026, and the association links food sales to gifts.
- **"Hankyu opened the first depachika in 1936."** Hankyu’s records for 1936 show the Umeda store’s full reopening with a much larger restaurant, and a food shop set up in a Kobe basement. When the Umeda food hall went underground is not on the pages I read.
- **"Everything is half price at closing."** JNTO says closing time is an affordable time to visit, and that food-shop discounts do not always happen. There is no official rule.
- **"Every counter sells by weight."** Some do; the law covers the weighing when they do. It does not say every counter must.
