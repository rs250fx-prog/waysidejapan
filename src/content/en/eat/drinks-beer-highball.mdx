Most visitors’ first drink in Japan is a beer, and many of the cans in a convenience store are not beer in law. The difference is a matter of tax, and on 1 October 2026 the tax changed. This page explains the three names on the beer shelf, where the big brewers and the small ones came from, and how the whisky highball — a drink that was in decline in the 2000s — came back, by its maker’s account, through izakaya rather than distilleries.

For drinks in Japan in general — the tax categories, the drinking age, customs — start from the drinks overview.

## Where it came from

**The big brewers each date themselves.** The dates below are each company’s own account.

- **Kirin.** Kirin’s history museum says William Copeland opened the Spring Valley Brewery in Yamate, Yokohama, in 1870 — the longest-lasting of the early Meiji breweries on the Yamate bluff, and the first in Japan to make beer continuously as an industry. Kirin Holdings’ English history says Kirin Brewery was founded in 1907, taking over the business of the Japan Brewery Company, founded in 1885. Whether the 1870 brewery leads directly to the 1907 company, neither page says, so I do not join them.
- **Asahi.** Asahi traces itself to the Osaka Beer Company, founded in November 1889 by Torii Komakichi, Toyama Shūzō and others. "Asahi Beer", made at its factory in Suita village, Osaka, went on sale from May 1892.
- **Sapporo.** Sapporo says it began with the Kaitakushi brewery, set up in Sapporo, Hokkaido, in 1876, and marks 2026 as its 150th year.
- **Suntory.** Suntory’s business pages date its beer to 1963; its timeline lists its first beer brewery, at Musashino. It launched an all-malt beer in 1986 and, in 1994, Hop’s Nama, which it says created a new market for happoshu.

**How many "big" brewers?** The tax agency’s statistics speak of five major beer makers. The phrase "big four" does not appear in the official material I read, so this page says "the big brewers" and leaves the number alone.

**Craft beer began with a number.** Until April 1994 a beer licence required brewing at least 2,000 kilolitres a year, which in practice meant only the big companies. The 1994 amendment cut the minimum to 60 kilolitres; both the tax agency and the Japan Craft Beer Association (JBA) record the change.

## What makes it beer

**Beer.** In the Liquor Tax Act, beer is made by fermenting malt, hops and water, under 20% alcohol. If other permitted ingredients such as other grains are used, malt must be at least 50%, and fruit and certain flavourings may be no more than 5% of the weight of the malt.

**Happoshu.** A sparkling drink, under 20%, that uses malt or barley as part of its ingredients. The JBA says the first were Suntory’s Hop’s in 1994 and then Sapporo’s Draft-y.

**"New genre".** An industry name, not a legal term. The tax agency’s leaflet on the 2026 change lists it as "happoshu ② (the so-called new genre)".

**The tax change.** The three used to pay different rates; the government said in 2017 that the gaps were affecting product development and sales. In steps from October 2020, beer-type drinks reached one rate in October 2026: ¥155,000 per kilolitre for beer (with or without sparkle) and happoshu, which the ministry gives as ¥54.25 per 350 ml. "Other sparkling drinks" under 11%, including chūhai, rose to ¥100,000 per kilolitre. In short, beer’s tax went down and happoshu’s and the new genre’s went up.

What that did to the price of a can, I have not checked against the makers’ own announcements, so I give none.

**The highball.** Whisky and soda. Suntory’s rules for its revived *kaku* highball, from its own staff article: fill the mug with ice, pour chilled soda gently, one part whisky to four of soda — kept to 7–8% alcohol. Where the highball itself began, I could not confirm from a primary source.

**Japanese whisky.** The tax law defines whisky by what it is made of — sprouted grain and water, saccharified, fermented and distilled — and says nothing of country or ageing. The Japan Spirits and Liqueurs Makers Association filled that gap with a voluntary standard, set on 12 February 2021 and in force from 1 April 2021. To be labelled Japanese whisky:

- malt (always), other grains, and water drawn in Japan only;
- mashing, fermentation and distillation at a distillery in Japan, distilled to under 95%;
- at least three years in wooden casks of 700 litres or less, in Japan;
- bottled in Japan at 40% or more; caramel allowed for colour.

Whisky that does not qualify may not use "Japan whisky" or similar words, translations, or "-type"/"-style", nor Japanese personal or place names, the flag or era names, unless it makes clear it does not qualify. Existing labels had until 31 March 2024. The association’s chairman wrote in 2021 that whiskies made only from foreign spirit were being sold abroad as Japanese whisky, confusing customers. A logo followed on 27 March 2025. It is the association’s rule, not a law, and there is no Japanese whisky GI on the tax agency’s list.

## The breweries, and how many there are

The tax agency counts "local beer" production sites, excluding the five big brewers and trial licences, at each 31 March:

- 1995: 6 · 1997: 103 · 2000: 264 (the first peak)
- 2009: 206 · 2018: 184
- 2019: 395 · 2020: 400 · 2021: 405 · 2022: 412 · 2023: 423 · 2024: 428 · **2025: 430**

At 31 March 2025 there were 394 makers. The jump in 2019 is not a sudden boom: the tax agency explains that the 2017 reform widened the definition of beer, and anyone holding a happoshu licence at 31 March 2018 was given a beer licence. A count for March 2026 was not in the material I read.

**What "craft" means.** There is no legal definition. The JBA’s own, from May 2018, has three parts: independent of the big mass-producing brewers that existed before April 1994; small batches, 20 kilolitres of wort or less per brew, so the brewer can watch over it; and individual beer made by traditional methods or with local ingredients, rooted in its area.

## How to order it

**The highball.** Suntory’s own design was a highball you drink like beer: a *kaku jokki* (mug), full of ice, one to four. If you want it served that way, that is the reference.

**Beer, happoshu or new genre.** They are different items in law. I could not find an official source on how izakaya menus or convenience-store shelves tell you which is which, so if it matters to you, ask.

Prices are not given here; the izakaya overview covers what drinks cost on a night out.

## Not all of it is this

Beer has no home town: each big brewer dates itself to its own city, and the small breweries came after 1994 across the country. And whisky that misses the 2021 standard is still whisky in tax law; it just may not call itself Japanese whisky.

## Beer and the highball away from the brewery

**The highball spread from the izakaya out.** Suntory’s staff article says the whisky market peaked in 1983 and by 2007 had fallen to a sixth of that by volume. In 2008 it started a project to revive the highball with its Kakubin whisky, aimed at bars and restaurants. It says the project helped the whisky market grow 17% in 2009, the year after it began; that the number of places serving its highball went from about 15,000 at the end of 2008 to 60,000 in 2009; and that it launched a canned highball that year. Its staff call 2009 the first year of the revival. The key, by its account, was izakaya and restaurants: the beer-style mug and the serving rules. These are the company’s figures; I found no official statistics to check them.

**At the source, you book.** Suntory says its Yamazaki Distillery in Shimamoto, Osaka prefecture, began in 1923 and is Japan’s oldest malt whisky distillery; it handles everything from raw materials to bottling, and is open 10:00–16:45 (last entry 16:30). Suntory’s tours need reservations except for walk-in courses, some by lottery and some first-come. Entry is free but some tours charge. Tours with tasting cannot be combined on the same day. Guiding is in Japanese only, with a free audio-guide app at Yamazaki. Its Q&A says under-20s from junior-high graduation may join at factories that do not serve alcohol; whether they may join at Yamazaki, which does, I could not confirm, nor the fee.

**Asahi’s Suita museum** is by reservation and has charged since 14 January 2023, with separate prices for those 20 and over and for younger visitors from primary-school age; the page does not say whether they include tax, so I give none. Drivers (on a motorbike, the passenger too), cyclists and anyone under 20 get soft drinks, not beer. English is by audio-guide app.

**Craft breweries** — I did not read any official material on brewery taprooms or tours, so I say nothing about them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Every can in the beer aisle is beer."** Some are happoshu or new genre, which are different items in law.
- **"The tax change made beer cheaper."** It cut beer’s tax. Prices are set by makers and shops.
- **"Japan has hundreds of new breweries since 2018."** Part of that jump was a licence change.
- **"Japanese whisky is protected by law."** It is protected by a makers’ association’s voluntary standard.
- **"The highball is the distillery’s drink."** By Suntory’s own account, it came back through izakaya.
