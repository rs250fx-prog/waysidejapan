*Senbei-jiru* is a hotpot from Hachinohe, on the Pacific side of Aomori, with crackers broken into it. The crackers are not rice crackers. They are *Nanbu senbei*, made of wheat flour, and the reason they exist, and the reason they ended up in soup, is the weather. This page sets out what the farm ministry, the Cultural Agency and the B-1 Grand Prix organisers say, and where their accounts differ.

For B-grade local food in general, start from the [B-grade overview](/en/eat/bkyu/). Senbei-jiru also appears on the [Tohoku local dishes page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-tohoku/).

## Where it came from

**The cracker.** The farm ministry describes Nanbu senbei as a traditional food of the old Nanbu domain, which covered south-eastern Aomori and northern Iwate. Wheat flour is mixed with salt and water and baked round in an iron mould. The area had frequent cold-weather crop failures, and rice was hard to grow; the crackers were a valuable keeping food. Putting them in soup gave senbei-jiru.

**The first pot.** The ministry says there are several theories of the origin, and gives one as an example: before the war, crackers were simmered in a soup made from the trimmings of *ugui*, a dace caught in the river. It does not choose this theory over others, and neither does this page.

**The war, and a new cracker.** During the war, the ministry says, iron pans were given up for the war effort and farm households stopped baking crackers. After the war, makers developed and sold a cracker made for soup, one that stays chewy and does not fall apart when simmered.

## How old is it?

Two government sources give two answers, and they are about different things.

- **The dish.** The Cultural Agency, which listed it as a 100-Year Food in fiscal 2022 (Reiwa 4, traditional category), says it has been eaten in the Hachinohe area for more than 200 years. The farm ministry’s children’s page says it has been eaten at home for about 200 years.
- **The name.** The farm ministry’s main entry says the name *senbei-jiru* took hold only in the Heisei era, when Hachinohe tourism groups promoted it as a speciality for visitors.

So a home dish with a long history acquired its current name, and its fame, in living memory. This page gives both.

## What goes in it

The farm ministry describes a chicken-stock hotpot seasoned with soy sauce, with vegetables and *ito-konnyaku* (thin konjac noodles), into which the crackers are broken and simmered. It calls chicken stock and soy sauce the most common style. As a port town would, Hachinohe also has versions with cod or grilled mackerel, and there is a version with horse meat in the style of *sakura-nabe*.

## The contest it started

The B-1 Grand Prix, the best-known contest for local food, began with this dish. The organisers’ history says that in autumn 2004 members of the Hachinohe Senbei-jiru Research Institute talked over the idea of a national event at a yakitori shop in Hachinohe. The farm ministry describes the institute as a citizens’ group formed in 2003, and calls it the parent of the B-1 Grand Prix.

- **The first contest** was held in Hachinohe in February 2006: ten town-promotion groups from Hokkaido to Kyushu, and 17,000 visitors over two days.
- **The institute’s record**, on the organisers’ list: silver at the second, third and fourth contests, bronze at the fifth and sixth, and gold at the seventh, in 2012.

The organisers stress that the awards go to the town-promotion group, not to the dish, and that the event is not meant as a food sale.

## Where to eat it

The farm ministry says about 200 restaurants in Hachinohe serve senbei-jiru. Aomori’s official tourism site says the Tohoku Shinkansen runs direct from Tokyo Station to Hachinohe Station.

## Not the only flour dish in Hachinohe

Senbei-jiru is one of several flour foods from Nanbu country. The farm ministry’s entry on *hittsumi* — dough pinched off by hand into soup — places it in the same rice-poor farmland, and says that in Hachinohe it is eaten at home about as often as senbei-jiru. One is the dish visitors are directed to; the other, by the ministry’s account, is just as much a part of home cooking.

## Senbei-jiru away from Hachinohe

No source checked for this page compares senbei-jiru served outside Hachinohe with senbei-jiru at home. Nanbu senbei itself travels: Iwate prefecture says its antenna shops, including Iwate Ginga Plaza in Ginza, stock foods such as soba and Nanbu senbei.

## What people get wrong

- **“Senbei-jiru is made with rice crackers.”** Nanbu senbei is wheat flour, salt and water, from an area where rice was hard to grow.
- **“Senbei-jiru is an old name.”** The dish is old by the Cultural Agency’s account; the name took hold in Heisei, by the farm ministry’s.
- **“Any cracker will do.”** After the war, makers developed a cracker meant for soup that does not fall apart.
- **“It won the first B-1 Grand Prix.”** The first contest was held in Hachinohe; the institute’s gold came at the seventh, in 2012.
