Tango, on 5 May, is the festival listed in English as Children’s Day, and it has two foods. *Kashiwa-mochi* is a mochi named after the *kashiwa* oak, whose leaf carries its meaning. *Chimaki* is a dumpling wrapped in leaves; in Shimane, rice flour in bamboo-grass leaves. The farm ministry gives a meaning for each, and they are not the same kind of meaning: one is about families, the other about keeping evil away. The festival itself began as something else entirely, a time of ritual abstinence at rice-planting.

For food on the rest of Japan’s calendar, start from the [overview of food for annual events](/en/eat/seasonal/).

## Where it came from

Tango is one of the five seasonal festivals, the *gosekku*, which the ministry’s *washoku* culture material says came from China between the Nara and Heian periods and were merged with Japan’s own observances.

The same material explains the name and the history in a few steps:

- *Tango* means the first day of the horse in the month.
- The fifth month was treated as a month of abstinence, and in China there was an old custom of driving out evil with sweet flag (*shōbu*) and mugwort.
- It came to Japan in the Nara period, joined a Japanese custom of abstinence at the time of rice-planting, and became a court banquet, the *tango no sechie*.
- It became popular among ordinary people in the Edo period.

The ministry’s children’s food-education page groups it with the other *gyōji-shoku*, the special dishes eaten on seasonal days, which it says carry wishes for the family’s happiness and health.

## What makes it Tango

**Kashiwa-mochi.** Two of the ministry’s pages give the same reason in slightly different words:

- The children’s page says the *kashiwa* oak does not drop its old leaves until new buds have come out, and that the mochi is eaten wishing for life to pass on from parent to child, and on to grandchildren.
- The culture material says that because the parent leaves do not fall until the new buds appear, kashiwa-mochi is a food that promises succession through the generations.

**Chimaki.** The culture material says chimaki comes from a Chinese story and is said to ward off evil. It does not tell the story, and this page does not supply one.

The national confectioners’ association, explaining how Japanese sweets carry the seasons, puts kashiwa-mochi among the sweets made only in their own season, alongside sakura-mochi, kusa-mochi and chestnut sweets. For how those sweets fit together, see [wagashi](/en/eat/wagashi/).

## How to eat it

The ministry’s pages give meanings, not instructions. They do not say whether the leaf of a kashiwa-mochi is eaten, how chimaki is unwrapped, or which of the two a household should choose. Official sources consulted for this page do not set out an order or a manner of eating either food.

## Not all of Tango is this

**Shimane wraps its chimaki a month later.** The ministry’s traditional-food guide says that in eastern Shimane, chimaki is eaten at the old-calendar Tango, on 5 June. It is rice flour wrapped in the leaves of *kumazasa*, a broad-leaved bamboo grass, and is also called *sasamaki*. The page says:

- chimaki is said to have come to Japan from China as an offering for Tango in the Northern and Southern dynasties, between the 3rd and 6th centuries;
- in Shimane it was made to pray for boys’ growth, and also carried the meaning of a celebration after rice-planting;
- it is called *chimaki* in the east of the prefecture and *maki* or *kayamaki* on the Oki islands.

The link to rice-planting matches what the culture material says of the festival as a whole: an older Japanese custom tied to planting, which the imported festival joined.

**Tokushima’s potato in place of ohagi.** The ministry’s page on Tokushima dishes describes *shusse-imo*, from Kaiyō and the south of the prefecture: steamed potato wrapped in smooth bean paste and sliced into rounds. It says it was devised when rice was short and potatoes were eaten instead, that it was made in place of *ohagi*, and that because *shusse* means getting on in the world, it was offered at the birth of a boy and at Tango. More of the island’s food is in the [Shikoku local-food guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-shikoku/).

## Tango away from home

There is no home town to go back to here. The two foods are told as national, and the clearest variations the official sources record are inside one prefecture: a different date, a different leaf and a different name in Shimane, and a different name again on Oki. Official sources do not compare how kashiwa-mochi or chimaki is sold or eaten in Tokyo, Osaka or elsewhere.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tango began as a festival for children."** The ministry’s culture material describes a month of abstinence, warding off evil with sweet flag and mugwort, a rice-planting custom and a court banquet, before it became popular in the Edo period. Prayers for boys’ growth appear in its Shimane account of chimaki.
- **"The two foods mean the same thing."** Kashiwa-mochi is given a meaning about generations; chimaki is said to ward off evil.
- **"It is always 5 May."** Eastern Shimane keeps chimaki for the old-calendar Tango, 5 June.
- **"Chimaki has one name."** The ministry records *chimaki*, *sasamaki*, *maki* and *kayamaki* within Shimane alone.
