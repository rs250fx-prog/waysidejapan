*Champon* is a bowl of noodles stewed in their soup with a large amount of meat, seafood and vegetables, and it belongs to Nagasaki. The farm ministry lists it among Nagasaki prefecture's local dishes, next to its soupless sibling *sara udon*. It has a named restaurant, a named cook and a year — though even here, the sources say "is held to". This page is the map: where it came from, why it is cooked the way it is, what the name may mean, and what changes outside Nagasaki.

## What it is

The farm ministry's local-food database describes champon as Chinese noodles stewed in a rich soup with meat, vegetables and seafood. The usual ingredients it lists are pork, *hanpen* (a fish cake), cabbage, spring onion, bean sprouts, squid and prawns, with clams in summer and oysters in winter, and a raw egg if you like.

Its root, the ministry says, is a Fujian dish written 湯肉絲麺: a noodle soup with a light broth, pork, shiitake, bamboo shoots and spring onion. The Nagasaki version made the soup richer, the toppings more plentiful, and gave it a firm noodle of its own.

## Why it is the way it is

**It was built to feed students.** The farm ministry says that in 1899 (Meiji 32), Chin Heijun (陳平順), owner of the Chinese restaurant Shikairou, made it for Chinese students — something cheap and nourishing — by frying vegetables and scraps of meat, adding Chinese noodles and stewing them in a rich soup. The ministry presents this as what is held to be the beginning.

Shikairou's own history fills in the man. Chin Heijun came to Nagasaki from Fuzhou in Fujian in 1892, aged 19. In 1899 he opened Shikairou, a Chinese restaurant and inn, at Hirobaba near the entrance to the old Chinese quarter. Seeing how the students ate, he devised a dish that was cheap, nourishing and plentiful. At first it was called *Shina udon*, and only later champon. His daughter urged him to register a trademark; he declined, the restaurant says, on the grounds that it was enough for many people to eat it. The restaurant describes its soup as chicken and pork bones, with produce of Nagasaki's hills and sea.

**One wok.** The ministry describes the method: fry the toppings, then add the noodles and stew them in the soup. It can be made in a single Chinese wok. That is the mechanical difference from its relatives. Champon stews its noodle; thick-noodle sara udon fries the noodle until no soup is left; Tokyo's *tsukemen* serves noodle and soup apart and dips. And yakisoba, in the account of the sauce maker Otafuku, began as a copy of Chinese fried noodles with Worcestershire sauce. Each takes a Chinese wheat noodle and changes what happens between noodle and liquid. That grouping is this site's, not any source's.

**It spread fast in Nagasaki.** Shikairou's own timeline, which the restaurant compiled, says a newspaper in 1905 described champon as a favourite of the students; that a 1907 prefectural publication counted more than ten champon shops in the city; and that a 1914 guide to Nagasaki presented *Shina udon* as a local specialty.

## The name

There are three explanations, and this site does not choose.

1. **A Chinese word for a simple meal.** The farm ministry gives *shanpon* (喰飯), meaning a simple meal, changed in pronunciation.
2. **A Portuguese word for mixing.** The ministry also gives a Portuguese word meaning "to mix", changed in pronunciation. It does not name the word.
3. **A Fujian greeting.** Shikairou says the name comes from the greeting the Chinese of Nagasaki exchanged in the Fujian dialect at the time, *shapon* or *seppon*, meaning "to eat a meal".

- **Two champon in one prefecture.** The farm ministry names Nagasaki prefecture's two main champon as Nagasaki champon, based mainly on pork bones and rich, and Obama champon, based mainly on chicken bones and light. I have no further source on Obama's, so it is listed but not written.
- **Sara udon is the same restaurant's dish.** Both the ministry and Shikairou say Chin Heijun made it as a soupless champon. It now comes with thick or thin noodles, and the two sources describe the thin one differently.

## Where to eat it

- **Nagasaki, at Shikairou.** The restaurant that is held to have created it opens for a lunch session and an evening session. On 2026-10-10 its official page said the restaurant does not take general reservations; private rooms are booked only for set courses of four people or more; no booking is accepted through websites, apps or booking agents; and closing days are announced month by month on the official site. The page gives no prices or waiting times.
- **Elsewhere in Nagasaki.** The ministry says champon is eaten every day in Nagasaki.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## The same name, a different place

- **It has left home.** The farm ministry says champon is eaten every day in Nagasaki and can also be eaten at shops in other prefectures. I found no source that compares the two, and this page does not.
- **Sauce on the table, in Kyushu.** The ministry's page for children says that in Kyushu champon is sometimes eaten with sauce poured on it, and that shops often have sauce on the table. I found nothing on whether shops outside Kyushu do the same.
- **The type changes.** Within Nagasaki prefecture there are already two champon, rich and light. What a shop elsewhere serves under the name, I could not find a source for.
- **The origin restaurant has its own rules.** Shikairou's no-reservation policy is the most concrete difference between eating champon at its source and anywhere else.

## What people get wrong

- **"Champon is ramen."** It is stewed: the noodles are cooked in the soup with the toppings, in one wok. The farm ministry treats it as a Nagasaki local dish in its own right.
- **"It is a Chinese dish."** Its root is a Fujian noodle soup, but the ministry says it was changed in Nagasaki — richer soup, more toppings, a noodle of its own.
- **"The name is Portuguese."** That is one of three explanations, two of them Chinese. Nobody has settled it.
- **"There is one champon."** Nagasaki prefecture alone has two main ones, and the restaurant that made champon also made sara udon.
- **"You can book Shikairou online."** As of 2026-10-10 it said it takes no bookings through websites, apps or agents.
