Niigata sake is commonly sold with a two-word phrase, *tanrei karakuchi* — clean and dry. The official description of Niigata sake, written by the national tax agency when it gave the prefecture a protected name in 2022, uses the first word and not the second. That gap is a good way into what Niigata sake is, and what the label can and cannot promise.

For what sake is and how to read a label, start from the sake overview.

## Where it came from

The tax agency's GI document tells the story through rice, water and people.

**Rice country.** The Echigo and Takada plains are grain-growing country, and in the Edo period Niigata's ports were calls for the *kitamae-bune*, the coastal trading ships that carried rice and sake to the rest of Japan.

**Brewers who went west to learn.** From the mid-Edo period, men from Echigo (old Niigata) went away to work in the farming off-season, learned their trade under the brewers of the west — Tanba, Tajima and elsewhere — and became a guild of master brewers, the Echigo *tōji*, who worked in breweries across the country. The Niigata brewers' association adds that by the early Meiji era they were the most numerous in Japan.

**An institution behind it.** The same document names the Niigata Prefectural Brewing Experiment Station, founded in 1930, as the only independent prefectural research institute for sake in Japan, and the Niigata Sake School run by the brewers' association as the first of its kind.

## What makes it Niigata sake

**The official description.** The GI text says Niigata sake is generally *tanrei*, and defines the word: sake with a clean taste and few off-flavours is called tanrei. It describes a light sweetness from the rice mingling with the other savoury elements. The Niigata brewers' association uses similar words — fine-grained, with few off-flavours. Neither says the sake is dry. The phrase *tanrei karakuchi* is common in Japanese, but I could not find it in any official text, so the accurate thing to say is that Niigata's official word is tanrei.

**The water.** Plentiful and generally soft, in the GI document. The brewers' association explains why that matters: in soft water fermentation proceeds slowly, producing a fine-grained sake.

**The snow.** Niigata has heavy snow, but even in the coldest season temperatures do not drop to extremes, and the difference between day and night is small. The GI document says the steady cold brought by the snow suits the kōji mould and the yeast. The association adds that winter snow cleans the air and makes it harder for unwanted bacteria to get in.

**The rice.** Gohyakumangoku, a sake rice bred in Niigata from the varieties Kikusui and Shin 200-gō, and Koshitanrei, a prefectural variety crossed from Yamada Nishiki and Gohyakumangoku. The national research institute lists Gohyakumangoku as grown mainly in Niigata and the Hokuriku region.

**Polishing, in the association's words.** The whiter the rice, the higher the cost, and the better the sake can be; daiginjō is polished by more than half; ordinary sake has no requirement and is generally about 73–75%. That is the association's explanation of its own trade, not a rule that more polished is always better.

## The protected name

**Niigata** became a geographical indication for sake on 7 February 2022. To use it: Japanese rice only; water drawn within Niigata prefecture only; brewed, stored and bottled in the prefecture; added alcohol no more than 50% of the weight of the rice. It is managed by the Niigata prefectural brewers' cooperative.

By comparison, Nada Gogō in Hyōgo caps added alcohol at 25% and requires rice of inspection grade 3 or better. Each GI writes its own rules.

**Ponshukan.** Tasting rooms inside Niigata, Nagaoka and Echigo-Yuzawa stations, with machines pouring the representative sakes of every brewery in the prefecture. You pay at the desk and receive a borrowed cup and five tasting coins, enough for up to five pours. From 1 July 2026 that costs ¥1,000 including tax; it had been ¥500 for 31 years. The machines hold 129 sakes at Echigo-Yuzawa and 111 at Niigata and Nagaoka. Over 20s only, and children may not come in. From the same date there is a concierge and food pairing, the food also paid in coins.

**The prefecture's sake festival.** The Niigata brewers' association has announced Niigata Sake no Jin 2027. I have not confirmed the dates, venue or price, so they are not given here.

## How Niigata drinks it

The brewers' association's own Q&A has two pieces of advice worth passing on:

- **Cold sake is fine.** There is no medical basis for the idea that it is bad for you — but because it is easy to drink, it is easy to drink too much.
- **Drink water alongside.** *Yawaragi-mizu*, chaser water, slows absorption and lets you keep track of how drunk you are. The national brewers' association suggests roughly as much water as sake.

## Niigata sake away from Niigata

**The bottle is the same bottle.** Sake under the Niigata GI must be brewed, stored and bottled in Niigata with Niigata water, so a Niigata bottle on a Tokyo menu is not a copy.

**A region name always means the whole bottle.** Under the national labelling standard, a region may be named on a sake label only if all of the sake was brewed there; a blend from different regions cannot carry a place name.

**The breadth stays in Niigata.** What Ponshukan does — over a hundred sakes from one prefecture's breweries, poured one cup at a time — a Tokyo bar does not usually attempt. The national brewers' association's information center in Toranomon, Tokyo, does something different: about 50 drinks from across Japan, from ¥100 a glass.

## What people get wrong

- **"Niigata sake is dry."** The official word is *tanrei*, clean and with few off-flavours, and the official text mentions a gentle sweetness.
- **"Snow makes it cold, so it's brewed colder."** The point in the official text is steadiness: heavy snow, but no extremes and little difference between day and night.
- **"Niigata is just one style."** The GI says "generally" tanrei. Breweries differ.
- **"Cold sake is bad for you."** Niigata's brewers say there is no medical basis for that. Drinking too much of it is the risk.
- **"Ponshukan is ¥500."** It was, for 31 years. Since July 2026 it is ¥1,000 for five coins.
