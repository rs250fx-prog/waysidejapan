This page covers the two Shikoku prefectures that face the Pacific and the Kii channel rather than the Inland Sea: Tokushima and Kochi. Kagawa and Ehime are on the [Setouchi page](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/), with the other Inland Sea prefectures. In the official descriptions of Tokushima and Kochi, mountains keep coming up — buckwheat where rice would not grow, sushi topped with mountain vegetables — alongside bonito from the Kuroshio current and the banquets Kochi calls *okyaku*. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Tokushima

**Iya soba.** From the Iya district of Miyoshi city. The farm ministry says buckwheat growing "is said" to have begun with the Heike, who fled into Iya after losing the Genpei war. Rice grew poorly, and buckwheat, which grows easily and quickly, became the staple. It is made from local buckwheat flour alone, with no binder, so the noodles break easily and are thick and short; it is also called *sobakiri*. It was served at celebrations and to guests, and it is always on the menu at events in Ōboke and Iya.

**Soba-gome zōsui.** *Soba-gome* is buckwheat grain boiled in salt water, husked and dried, then simmered with vegetables, meat and stock into a rice-less porridge. The ministry says the Heike are said to have made it at New Year. It is sold in supermarkets across the prefecture, and freeze-dried.

**Iya no dekomawashi.** Potato (the local *gōshu-imo*), buckwheat dumpling, firm *iwa-dōfu* tofu and konnyaku on a skewer, turned over the hearth and grilled with miso. The name is said to come from the way it resembles the *deku* puppets of Awa puppet theatre turning their heads. Few homes now have a hearth, so the prefecture's "Tokushima local dishes" material gives a method for the oven or grill.

**Handa sōmen.** Thin wheat noodles from Handa in Tsurugi town, slightly thick for sōmen and firm. The ministry says there are several accounts of how it began, and that boatmen in the mid-Edo period are said to have brought sōmen to Handa by way of Miwa in Nara, Awaji and Naruto. It is properly made in the cold months, November to March. It is a different producing area from [Miwa sōmen](/en/eat/somen-miwa/).

**Shusse-imo.** From Kaiyō and the south of the prefecture. Devised when rice was short and potatoes were eaten instead: steamed potato wrapped in smooth bean paste and sliced into rounds. It was made in place of *ohagi*, and because *shusse* means getting on in the world, it was offered at the birth of a boy and on Boys' Day.

Tokushima ramen, Awa-odori chicken and Naruto wakame are not in the farm ministry's list.

### Kochi

**Katsuo no tataki.** Seared bonito, eaten across the prefecture. The ministry's dish page says it "is said" to have been fishermen's food on board that spread to everyone, and also that it developed as a way to eat bonito that had lost its freshness at sea before there was any way to keep it. *Tataki* is said to come from slapping (*tataku*) salt or sauce in. The ministry's Kochi story gives three accounts of the origin side by side:

- **On board**: fishermen's food at sea.
- **Two lords**: Chōsokabe Motochika ate bonito half-grilled, and Yamauchi Kazutoyo later banned eating it raw.
- **For foreigners**: it was served to Westerners in the Meiji era in place of steak.

This page gives all three. The 100-year food page says bonito became Kochi's prefectural fish in 1988 (Shōwa 63) and that Kochi eats the most per household in Japan. Autumn's returning bonito, fatty, is called best for tataki, and the first bonito of early summer is popular too. Searing over straw is held to be best, though homes mostly use gas. Some areas make tataki of moray eel or vegetables such as aubergine and pumpkin. For the catch, the method and the dressing, see [mackerel, bonito and tuna](/en/eat/seafood-saba-katsuo-maguro/).

**Sawachi.** Not a dish but a way of serving, the ministry says: sashimi, katsuo tataki, sushi, simmered and fried food on platters 36 to 39 cm across. It is the food of *okyaku*, Tosa dialect for a banquet with guests, and it belongs to big days, not everyday meals — weddings, funerals, births, sixtieth birthdays, seasonal festivals, new houses. The ministry traces it to the domain era, when large platters came out at the end of samurai banquets, and says it spread across the prefecture as okyaku food after the Meiji era. The number of platters shows the size of the okyaku. From the 1955–64 period (the Shōwa 30s) caterers multiplied and the platters grew bigger and showier; lately they take in Western dishes such as roast beef and fried prawns.

**Guru-ni.** Daikon, carrot, taro and other roots simmered together. *Guru* is said to mean "friends" or "everyone" in Tosa dialect. It once used six ingredients, for the six characters of *Namu Amida Butsu*; in Tosa city, nine ingredients make it *itoko-ni*. A winter side dish, reheated again and again to let the flavour settle, and served in school lunches.

**Inaka-zushi.** Sushi topped with the mountains: myōga, *ryūkyū* (*hasu-imo* stems), konnyaku, shiitake and the square *shihōchiku* bamboo shoot. The ministry says it began when kelp and nori were hard to come by. It is part of a sawachi, sold at the Sunday market, farm stalls and supermarkets, and cheaper and more everyday than fish sushi. It was chosen in 1986 among Japan's "100 hometown rice balls", and a public-private group to promote Tosa sushi was formed in 2018.

**Kobu-zushi.** Rolled in white kelp in the centre of the prefecture (*ita-kobu-zushi*) and in black kelp in the west (*kuro-kobu-zushi*). The ministry thinks the split came because white kelp was expensive and hard to get.

*Imo-kenpi*, fried sweet-potato sticks, is not in the farm ministry's list.

## What makes it Tokushima and Kochi

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Tokushima 30, Kochi 30 — 60 in all. I counted both the dish tiles and the links to dish pages, and the counts matched. Among them:

- **Tokushima**: soba-gome zōsui, *bōze* sugata-zushi, dekomawashi, Handa sōmen, Iya soba, shusse-imo, Awa *uirō*, *hangoroshi*, *iri-meshi*, bara-zushi with kintoki beans, white-miso zōni, tai-meshi, *ayu* porridge, grilled *amego*.
- **Kochi**: katsuo tataki, guru-ni, sawachi, *tsugani-jiru*, kobu-zushi, *kokera-zushi*, inaka-zushi, mackerel sugata-zushi, *kibinago*, steamed sea bream.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them: Tokushima 8, Kochi 12 — 20 in all.

- **Tokushima**: hangoroshi (fiscal 2025, future), Gosho *tarai* udon (2024, future), Mugi's beach-festival lunch box (2024, modern), Mugi pressed sushi (2022, traditional), Awa uirō (2021, traditional), *kakimaze* with Kitō yuzu (2021, future), Shima sōmen (2021, future), Naruto wakame dishes (2021, traditional).
- **Kochi**: ayu sushi pressed with yuzu vinegar (2025, traditional), kokera-zushi (2023, traditional), Tosa *sōda-bushi* (2023, traditional, special award), Kōnan chive yakisoba (2022, future), *hakugyokutō* (2022, traditional), katsuo tataki and sawachi (both 2021, traditional, special award), Chūnichi soba (2021, future), aubergine tataki (2021, future), *hera-zushi* (2021, modern), yuzu dishes (2021, traditional), *yunosu* dishes (2021, future).

Hangoroshi, Awa uirō, Shima sōmen, katsuo tataki, sawachi and kokera-zushi are on both lists. The two lists are chosen separately.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: Tokushima ramen, Awa-odori chicken, Naruto wakame and imo-kenpi.

## What shaped it

**Tokushima.** The ministry's Tokushima story says the prefecture is surrounded by three seas — the Inland Sea, the Kii channel and the Pacific — with the Naruto strait, counted among the world's three great tidal currents, and the Yoshino and Naka rivers; about 80 per cent of it is mountain. Along the Yoshino, sugar cane, brought in the time of the shogun Tokugawa Ienari, became Awa *wasanbon* sugar, which goes into Awa uirō; Handa sōmen comes from the same valley. In Iya, rice grew poorly and buckwheat became the staple. From the days of the ancient *ritsuryō* state Tokushima was Shikoku's gateway, with close ties to Kyoto and Osaka, and the ministry says that is why dishes for guests and dishes that keep grew up. It also names the *yusan-bako*, a three-tier lunch box handed down from the Edo period.

**Kochi.** The ministry's Kochi story says 84 per cent of the prefecture is forest, with the Shikoku mountains to the north and the Pacific to the south, where the Kuroshio brings fish. The okyaku culture is shared across the prefecture, and sawachi is indispensable to it. The ministry says that, cut off from neighbouring prefectures by the mountains, Kochi's food developed on its own. It names ayu from the Shimanto and Niyodo rivers, and tsugani-jiru.

**Together.** I found no official source that describes Tokushima and Kochi, or all of Shikoku, as one food culture. The Yoshino river and the Kuroshio each belong to one prefecture's story.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Iya soba**: several restaurants, hotels, canteens and souvenir shops in Iya.
- **Handa sōmen**: from the noodle makers.
- **Katsuo tataki**: izakaya, farm stalls and fishmongers.
- **Sawachi**: from caterers; restaurants in the prefecture serve small sawachi for a few people.
- **Inaka-zushi**: the Sunday market, farm stalls and supermarkets.

**Prices.** I found no official dish price clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none.

## How it is eaten, and when

**When.** Handa sōmen is made from November to March. Bonito for tataki is best in autumn, with the first bonito of early summer also popular. Guru-ni is a winter dish. Shusse-imo is for Boys' Day.

**The occasion.** Sawachi is for okyaku, not for every day. Inaka-zushi and guru-ni are everyday food. Iya soba was for celebrations and guests.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Iya and Iya soba.** Miyoshi city's tourism site, on its English page for Hotel Iya Onsen, says the hotel's restaurant serves Iya soba. The farm ministry places Iya soba in the Iya district of Miyoshi, but does not mention the springs, and the city's feature on the Ōboke and Iya springs is about the baths and the scenery, not soba. So you can eat Iya soba at an Iya onsen hotel; I do not call it the springs' dish. See [Shikoku's onsen](/en/soak/onsen-shikoku/).

**Kochi, without a link.** Kochi's tourism site lists its hot springs without any food, and the ministry's tataki and sawachi pages do not mention hot springs. I found no link.

**Dōgo is in Ehime.** For Shikoku's best-known spring, see [Dōgo Onsen](/en/soak/dogo/) and the Setouchi page.

**Getting there.** Checked 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Tokushima** (Tokushima tourism association): about 1 hour 15 minutes from Tokyo by air (the page does not name the airport); overnight highway bus about 9 hours 50 minutes to 10 hours.
- **Ōboke and Iya** (Miyoshi city tourism): about 2 hours 10 minutes from Tokushima airport to JR Ōboke station by JR, about 1 hour 30 minutes from Kōchi Ryōma airport.
- **Kochi** (Kochi tourism): Haneda to Kōchi Ryōma airport about 1 hour 30 minutes, ten flights a day. By rail, *Nozomi* to Okayama about 3 hours 15 minutes, then the limited express *Nanpū* to Kochi about 2 hours 35 minutes. The site notes that flights and times can change with the season, weather and traffic.

## Tokushima and Kochi food away from home

- **Tokushima.** TurnTable, 10-3 Shinsen-chō, Shibuya-ku, run by a private operator; the prefecture's Tokyo office lists "running and coordinating the antenna shop" among its duties. Its site describes an 88-seat restaurant, a 15-room hostel and a ground-floor market, with the restaurant open 11:30–15:00 and 18:00–23:00 and closed on Sundays. The site still carries old notices, and I could not confirm how it operates now; check before you go.
- **Kochi.** Marugoto Kōchi, opened by the prefecture in Ginza on 21 August 2010, at Orb Premier, 1-3-13 Ginza, Chūō-ku. Food on the ground floor (*Tosa-ichi*), sake, crafts and tourist information in the basement (*Tosa-gura*), and the restaurant TOSA DINING Okyaku upstairs. The shop is open 10:30–19:00, says its site, and it is run by the Kochi Prefecture Local Products Promotion Corporation, a general incorporated foundation.
- **Easy to carry home.** Handa sōmen and Iya soba by mail order, soba-gome in freeze-dried packs.

**What changes.** At the source, sawachi belongs to a banquet with guests and Handa sōmen to the cold months; the shops are open all year. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sawachi is one dish."** It is a way of serving many dishes on large platters for okyaku.
- **"Katsuo tataki has one origin story."** The ministry gives three, and does not choose.
- **"Shikoku is one cuisine."** I found no official source that treats it as one; Kagawa and Ehime are on the Setouchi page.
- **"Tokushima ramen is a listed local dish."** Tokushima ramen is not in the farm ministry's list.
- **"Iya soba is like other soba."** It is buckwheat alone, with no binder, thick, short and easily broken.
