Tajima is the name under the name. Kobe beef is famous; Tajima beef is what it is made of — a single prefecture's closed bloodline of Japanese Black, bred only from Hyōgo's own sires for generations, raised in the mountains of northern Hyōgo, and so prized as breeding stock that the ministry's own file calls it the foundation of wagyu improvement across Japan. If you understand Tajima you understand why Kobe beef is scarce.

## Where it came from

The cattle of Tajima, the old province in Hyōgo's north, were small, hardy work animals with an unusual tendency to marble. The Kobe council's history credits the opening of Yokohama in 1859 and Kobe in 1868 with turning them into food, when foreign residents began buying them for beef. Hyōgo then did something the other prefectures did not: it kept the line closed. The GI file for Kobe beef records that Hyōgo-bred Tajima avoids crossing with wagyu from other prefectures to maintain the bloodline, and that Tajima is used as *moto-ushi* — seed stock — for Japanese Black across the country. Tajima beef became Japan's second registered Geographical Indication, with Kobe beef the third, in December 2015; at the 2012 national wagyu show in Nagasaki it took the prize for fat quality.

## What makes it Tajima beef

**The bloodline.** Japanese Black descended only from sires owned by Hyōgo prefecture, generation after generation.

**The place.** Born, raised and slaughtered in Hyōgo, by registered producers, at prefectural meat centres.

**The age and grade.** Heifers and steers of 28 to 60 months, grading A or B with quality 2 or above. This is the floor; Kobe beef is the top of the same herd — quality 4 or 5, marbling 6 and above, within a weight band.

**The numbers.** About 7,500 animals a year are certified as Tajima beef, about 7,000 of them as Kobe beef. The two or three hundred that qualify as Tajima but not Kobe are sold as Tajima — the same animal, a grade lower, and much less famous.

## Why Tajima

Mountains, isolation and a decision. The northern Hyōgo valleys bred small cattle for farm work and kept them separate from the lowland herds; when beef became a market, the prefecture had a distinctive animal and chose to protect it rather than dilute it. The reward is that Hyōgo calves are the most sought-after in Japan — Matsusaka's "Tokusan" tier is defined as Hyōgo calves fattened in Mie for 900 days — and the cost is that the herd is small.

Tajima beef that is not Kobe beef is sold mainly in Hyōgo, around Toyooka, Kinosaki Onsen and the Tajima valleys, at prices below the Kobe-certified cut from the same herd; no official price list exists and none is given here. For the certified top tier, the Kobe page has a Kobe house's menu. The sensible order in Tajima country is Tajima beef: it is the Kobe animal without the Kobe seal, and the local shops know it.

## How to eat it

Steak, sukiyaki or shabu-shabu, as with any wagyu; in the hot-spring town of Kinosaki it is often a *teppan* course at the inn. Ask which grade the animal reached — the ID number tells the shop, and a 3 is still a Tajima animal.

## Tajima beef away from Hyōgo

It leaves the prefecture mostly as calves. Hyōgo's Tajima calves are bought by fattening farmers in Mie, Shiga and elsewhere, and finish their lives under other brands; the beef sold as Tajima is the small share raised at home. Outside Hyōgo the word rarely appears on a menu, and when it does, the test is the same as for Kobe: the ID number, and the registration.

## Getting there

Toyooka and Kinosaki Onsen are on the JR Sanin line, about two and a half hours from Kyoto or Osaka by limited express. The onsen town is the practical place to eat the beef and see the country it comes from.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tajima is a region, Kobe is a beef."** Tajima is both — a region and the breed line — and Kobe is its top grade.
- **"Kobe cattle."** There is no such animal. Every Kobe beef carcass was a Tajima steer or heifer.
- **"Tajima is second-rate Kobe."** It is the same herd. The seal is a grade, not a different cow.
