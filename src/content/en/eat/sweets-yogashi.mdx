*Yōgashi* (洋菓子) means Western-style sweets: cakes, puddings, cream puffs. The ones that look most ordinary in a Japanese cake-shop window — a triangle of sponge, cream and strawberry; a pudding surrounded by fruit — are, according to the companies that sell them, Japanese inventions or Japanese rebuilds. This page sets out those accounts as accounts, adds the one part of the story that has a public source behind it (the strawberry), and says plainly what is missing.

For sweets in Japan beyond wagashi in general, start from the sweets overview.

## Where it came from

**Fujiya and the shortcake.** Fujiya’s company timeline says Fujii Rin’emon opened a Western confectionery shop in Motomachi, Yokohama, in November 1910, began selling a Christmas cake in December of the same year, and began selling shortcake in 1922 (Taishō 11) at 8 sen a piece.

Fujiya then tells the shortcake’s origin two ways, and I give both:

- **Its FAQ** says the founder devised it himself in the Taishō era, combining soft sponge with cream and other things. It says the cake called shortcake in Japan is not mainstream overseas, so it can be called a Japan-born Western sweet. On the English word *short*, it says there are several theories, including one connecting it to shortening, and it does not pick one.
- **Its centenary page** says the founder went to America to study confectionery, met shortcake there, and on his return adapted it to Japanese taste. It calls the 1922 cake a forerunner of Japanese shortcake.

The two are not the same story, and I could not find a trade body or other independent source to settle either. Other companies’ claims to have created the cake I saw only in search summaries, so they are not here. Why the cake is topped with strawberries, Fujiya’s centenary page does not say.

**Sembikiya and the fruit parlour.** Sembikiya Sōhonten, the Tokyo fruit seller, says it began in 1834 as a street stall set up by Ōshima Benzō, opened a "fruit restaurant" in 1868 where people could enjoy Western-style meals and desserts without ceremony, and in 1929 opened a fruit parlour on the second floor of a new Western-style building. It lists strawberry milk, fruit punch, ice-cream soda, shortcake and fruit sandwiches among the restaurant’s attractions, unusual at the time.

That puts "shortcake" on a menu well before Fujiya’s 1922. Whether it was the same cake, the sources cannot say, and this site does not choose. Nor could I find support for calling Sembikiya’s the first fruit sandwich in Japan.

**Hotel New Grand and the pudding à la mode.** The Yokohama hotel says that during the seven years after the war when it was requisitioned by the occupation forces, its pastry chef created a dessert to please the wives of American officers. He combined pudding with canned fruit from America and ice cream; it was too large for a normal dessert plate, so it went on a special dish the hotel calls a *colton dish* (コルトンディッシュ), and its stylish look gave it the name *pudding à la mode*. The hotel says it still uses the same dish. It gives no year, so all that can be said is: during the occupation. That is the hotel’s account.

**The parfait and the coffee-house pudding.** I could not find an official account of how either took hold in Japan. Sembikiya’s history page has the word "fruit parfait" in a photo caption and nothing more.

## What makes it Japanese yōgashi

**The strawberry, and when it arrives.** Here there is a public source. ALIC’s vegetable report of December 2022 says that until the 1960s strawberries were in season around May and June. With greenhouse growing, autumn-planted plants became the norm, as they suit the year-end demand season. At Tokyo’s central wholesale market, Tochigi strawberries begin to arrive in November; Fukuoka, Ibaraki, Shizuoka and Saga join in December and arrivals rise sharply; the peak is March, and they keep coming until May. From June, a few come from Miyagi, and until October there are very few.

Put that beside Fujiya’s Christmas cake of 1910 and you have the shape of the story. I have not found a source saying the cake caused the change, so I leave the two facts side by side.

**How many strawberries.** The farm ministry’s magazine *aff* says Japan has about 300 strawberry varieties, with breeding going on in many places — for example Sky Berry (Tochigi, registered 2014), Tenshi no Mi (Saga, 2014), Shinku no Misuzu (Chiba, 2015) and Yotsuboshi (Mie, Kagawa and Chiba, 2017).

## The shops, and what they charge

I could not find official tax-included prices for a shortcake, a pudding à la mode or a fruit parfait. For reference on what drives the fruit’s cost: ALIC reports that domestic fresh strawberries at Tokyo’s central wholesale market averaged ¥1,757 per kilogram in 2021, ranging from ¥929 to ¥2,663. Prices tend to fall as shipments grow from January, bottom out in April–May, and rise from July to October. Those are wholesale prices, not shop prices. How variety or brand affects price, I could not find in an official source.

## How to order it

- **In a cake shop**, shortcake is sold by the piece, as Fujiya’s 1922 price of 8 sen a piece suggests.
- **At Hotel New Grand**, the pudding à la mode is the hotel’s own dessert, by its account, served in the same type of dish it describes.
- **At a fruit parlour**, I could not find an official menu or price to point to.

## Not all of Japan’s Western sweets are this

The three companies here are in Yokohama and Tokyo. For convenience-store cakes and puddings, see the convenience-store sweets page; for shaved ice and coffee houses, see the tea pages.

## Yōgashi away from Yokohama and Nihonbashi

I could not find an official source comparing these sweets in Yokohama or Nihonbashi with anywhere else. What does change from month to month is which prefecture’s strawberries reach Tokyo’s market, as ALIC sets out above. The pudding à la mode is the hotel’s dessert by its own account; what a café elsewhere serves under the same name, no source here says.

## What people get wrong

- **"Shortcake in Japan is just the Western cake."** Fujiya says Japan’s version is a Japan-born sweet. That is the company’s claim, without an independent check.
- **"There is one shortcake origin story."** Fujiya alone tells two, and Sembikiya lists shortcake on a menu earlier.
- **"Strawberries are naturally a winter fruit in Japan."** They were a May–June fruit until the 1960s.
- **"Pudding à la mode has a known birth year."** The hotel says only that it was during the occupation.
