Kansai eel skips the step that defines Tokyo's. The eel is opened along the belly, skewered, and grilled over charcoal while being brushed with tare — no steaming. Tokyo's tourism site describes the result as fragrant, crisp-skinned eel. In Osaka the eel is even grilled with the head on. This page covers that method, the explanations for the belly cut, and Nagoya's *hitsumabushi*, the best-known eel dish of the region between.

For eel in general — prices, grades, the day of the ox — start from the unagi overview.

## Where it came from

The Kikkoman food culture research centre's journal says *kabayaki* was born in Kyoto around the Genroku era, and that Edo first cooked it the same way before adding steaming. Kyoto's tourism site says the belly cut was devised first in Kansai, and that while Kantō steams, Kansai normally finishes by grilling alone.

**A second story for the belly cut.** The farm ministry's entry on Shimane's eel tofu records a different route. In 1756 eels were suddenly abundant in the Nakaumi lagoon; merchants of Matsue began selling them in Osaka, carrying them along the Izumo road — the "eel road". The belly-cut culture, it says, is *said* to have reached Osaka with that trade, and eel shops named Izumoya multiplied there. That is a separate explanation from Edo's back-cut stories, and this site does not choose between them.

**Mamushi.** Kyoto's tourism site notes that calling kabayaki *mamushi* is Kansai usage. The farm ministry's magazine says the word is said to come from *ma-mushi*, "steamed between": grilled eel tucked between layers of rice so it steams.

## What makes it Kansai style

**The farm ministry's method:** open along the belly, remove bone and guts, skewer, and grill over charcoal while brushing with tare, without steaming.

**Head on, in Osaka.** The ministry's Osaka entry says the eel is opened from the belly with the head still attached, grilled with tare, and the head cut off at the end. The heads, called *hansuke*, are simmered with tofu as *hansuke-dōfu*. Two stories for the name: a basket of heads cost 50 sen, half of one yen (*ensuke*), or a man called Hansuke sold them.

**One shop's account.** An Osaka eel shop and live-eel wholesaler founded in 1867 explains Osaka *jiyaki* as belly-opened and grilled without steaming; it grills over *binchōtan* charcoal, turning many times, and rests the skewered eel in the fridge for four to five hours first. It buys from Lake Hamana, Mikawa Isshiki, Kōchi, Kagoshima, Shizuoka's Yoshida, Tokushima and Miyazaki. I could not find an official definition of the word *jiyaki*.

**Not a rule.** Tokyo's tourism site notes that some specialists now use both methods, and Nara's Unagi Edogawa opens along the back and steams. "Kansai means belly cut, no steaming" is the typical pattern, not a law.

## Nagoya's hitsumabushi

Atsuta Hōraiken was founded in 1873 at Miya-juku, and says it has topped up its tare since then and that today's way of eating hitsumabushi was settled in the Meiji era. Its story of the name: it had an unbreakable wooden tub (*hitsu*) made and served eel and rice in it; customers ate the eel first, so the shop chopped the eel and mixed (*mabushi*) it into the rice.

**The four steps**, as the shop gives them:

1. The first bowl as it is.
2. The second with the condiments.
3. The third as *ochazuke*, with broth poured over.
4. The last however you liked best.

The shop says it grills over *binchōtan* charcoal; whether it steams, its pages do not say.

## Not all of Japan's eel is this

- **Kantō** opens along the back, grills plain, steams and grills again, for soft eel. See the Kantō page.
- **Yanagawa** steams eel on rice in a basket (*seiro-mushi*).

## Eating it in Kansai and Nagoya, and getting there

Osaka for *jiyaki*; Nagoya's Atsuta for *hitsumabushi*; Hamamatsu, between the two, for both, as the city says. Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Kansai eel away from home

- **The region spends.** In the 2015–17 household survey, the top spenders on shop-bought kabayaki after Hamamatsu were Kyoto (¥4,224), Ōtsu (¥4,182), Nara (¥3,713) and Osaka (¥3,608), all above central Tokyo (¥3,352) and Nagoya (¥3,344).
- **The eel is national.** An Osaka *jiyaki* shop buys from at least seven prefectures. The style is local; the eel is not.
- **The method crosses.** Kansai has steaming shops and Tokyo has specialists using both methods, so read the shop, not the city.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kansai eel is just unsteamed Kantō eel."** It is opened differently, along the belly, and in Osaka grilled with the head on.
- **"The belly cut is a merchant thing, the back cut a samurai thing."** One explanation among several; another traces Osaka's belly cut to eel brought from Izumo in the 1750s.
- **"Hitsumabushi is just an eel bowl."** It is eaten in stages, and the shop that tells its origin story says so.
- **"Every Kansai shop grills without steaming."** Some do both; at least one steams.
