Two places sell a sweet or a cup and a seat to sit with it, and both have a history that is easy to get wrong. The shaved-ice shop of Nagatoro is famous for natural ice — which runs out. The Japanese coffee house, the *kissaten*, looks like a fixture of every street — and has fallen to well under half its 1981 number. This page covers both: where they came from, how the ice is made, where coffee houses are thickest on the ground, and what you pay.

For tea itself — the kinds, the regions and the prices — start from the tea overview.

## Where it came from

**The shaved-ice house.** Asami Reizo, a long-established shaved-ice shop in the Nagatoro area of Saitama, gives its founding as Meiji 23 (1890) in a heading on its official blog. I found no second source for that date. It has two shops: the main shop at Kanasaki in Minano town, Chichibu district, and the Hodosan-michi shop in Nagatoro town.

The wider history of shaved ice in Japan — the old court writings, the ice houses, the first ice factories — I could not confirm from a primary source, so it is not on this page.

**The first coffee house.** The National Diet Library dates Japan's first kissaten to 1888: the Kahiichakan in Ueno, Tokyo, opened by Tei Eikei, who had studied in the United States. It kept newspapers and magazines, playing cards, go, shōgi and croquet, and had a dressing room and a writing room. It served coffee and black tea, and is said to have served bread, sweets and something like à la carte dishes. Where the Rokumeikan was a meeting place for the upper class, the library says, the Kahiichakan aimed to be a social salon for ordinary people and students. Even its name was read more than one way.

**The "pure" coffee house.** The library's account continues. From the end of Meiji into Taishō, more and more places called themselves *kafē*, and the number grew again around the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923. "Kafē" came to mean waitresses who entertained guests and served alcohol; in 1929 the Tokyo police issued rules to control them. In the 1930s and early 1940s, the word *jun-kissa*, "pure coffee house", came into use for places that wanted to set themselves apart as wholesome, without that kind of service. The library's examples include the Brasileiro in Umeda, Osaka (1930), which served no alcohol, and the Kyūperu in Ginza (1932). Tokyo city counted 55 coffee houses just after the earthquake and 671 in 1928, though the library notes that the statistics did not clearly separate coffee houses, restaurants, eating houses and kafē.

## What makes it kakigori, and what makes it a kissaten

### The ice

**Natural ice is cut in winter.** The Nagatoro tourism association described the cutting in January 2014: water from a stream is led into a hollow in the hills and frozen by the cold. That day the ice was about 14 cm thick, thinner than usual. The ice company cut it into blocks and carried it to an ice store. The site is private land and cannot be visited. Asami Reizo's blog recorded a cutting on 13 January 2025.

**Natural ice runs out.** The same blog warned in October 2024 that, depending on stock, it would serve pure ice instead, and a 2016 tourism association leaflet noted that the year's natural ice was finished and pure ice was being served. Natural ice is not guaranteed all year.

**Pure ice and machine ice.** A health ministry paper from 2016, reporting a visit to an ice business, records the business's own explanation. *Junpyō*, the pure ice sold by ice shops, is made from tap water that has been filtered and cleaned, then frozen slowly while air stirs the water — on average 3 mm an hour. The crystals are large, it holds no impurities and it melts slowly. Ice from an automatic ice machine freezes at about 50 mm an hour; its crystals are fine, it traps air and impurities and it looks white. The business says pure ice borrows the principle of ice formed slowly in nature.

Note what that comparison is between: pure ice and machine ice. I found no official or industry source comparing natural ice itself — how long it takes to freeze, its crystals, how it feels when shaved — with pure ice, so this site does not say one is better.

**A shaved ice in Uji.** Not all kakigori is about the ice. In Uji, the Itohkyuemon tea room serves *Uji kintoki*, shaved ice with matcha, from late April to mid-September.

### The coffee house

**Fewer every decade since 1981.** The All Japan Coffee Association publishes the government's count of coffee-house establishments, defined as places that mainly serve coffee, tea, soft drinks and simple meals to be eaten on the spot:

| Year | Coffee houses | 
|---|---|
| 1966 | 27,026 |
| 1975 | 92,137 |
| 1981 | 154,630 (peak) |
| 1991 | 126,260 |
| 2001 | 88,933 |
| 2012 | 70,454 |
| 2016 | 67,198 |
| 2021 | 58,669 |

People working in them fell from 575,768 in 1981 to 307,693 in 2021.

**Thickest in Kochi, Gifu and Wakayama.** The association's prefectural figures (2021 census, 2022 population) give one coffee house for every 2,146 people nationally. The fewest people per shop are in Kochi (831), Gifu (834) and Wakayama (981). Aichi has 6,171 shops, one for every 1,220 people; Osaka 6,758 (1,302); Kyoto 1,940 (1,295); Tokyo 6,121 (2,254). The association suggests that where there are more shops per person, residents probably use coffee houses more.

**Morning service and the Napolitan.** Two things often linked with the kissaten have their own stories. Morning service — toast and an egg with a coffee — is covered on the separate page about Nagoya's morning. The chain Komeda Coffee says that from opening until 11:00, ordering a drink brings a free choice of toast or a roll with a boiled egg, egg spread or sweet bean paste, and that this differs by shop. The *Napolitan* spaghetti is credited by the Hotel New Grand to its second head chef, Irie Shigetada: during the postwar occupation (1945–52) American soldiers ate boiled spaghetti with salt, pepper and ketchup, and after the occupation ended the hotel developed a version with garlic and onion fried until brown, fresh and canned tomatoes and tomato paste, bay leaf and olive oil. That is the hotel's own account. How it relates to the coffee-house Napolitan, I could not find.

## How to order it

**Nagatoro.** Check the shop's blog for the day: both shops have closed on Thursdays since 2024, and the blog announced Thursday closing over Golden Week 2026 as well, while summer and Golden Week bring extra opening days. If you want natural ice, the blog has announced the switch to pure ice before.

**Uji.** Uji kintoki is seasonal; outside late April to mid-September it is not on the menu.

**A kissaten.** At Komeda the free morning toast comes only with a drink and only until 11:00. At Starbucks, say whether you are eating in, because the tax differs.

## Not all of this is the same thing

- **Tea-house shaved ice**, like Uji kintoki at Itohkyuemon in Uji, is a seasonal item on a tea room's menu, sold alone or as a set with tea.
- **Chain coffee is priced in different ways.** Doutor posts one price to eat in or take away; Starbucks posts the eat-in price; Komeda sets prices shop by shop.

## Kakigori and kissaten away from home

**Nagatoro in summer and in winter.** The tourism association's leaflet says a two-hour wait is normal at the Hodosan-michi shop in summer, while in winter you can eat without waiting, and that the ice melts more slowly in the cold. Even at the source, the kind of ice changes with the season and the stock.

**Coffee houses by region.** Gifu has one coffee house for every 834 people, Aichi one for every 1,220, Tokyo one for every 2,254. On the household survey's spending on coffee houses (two-or-more-person households), Nagoya ranks first and central Tokyo second. A city can have fewer shops per person and still spend heavily.

**What I could not compare.** I found no official, tax-included prices for the same shaved ice in Nagatoro and Tokyo, or waiting times in Tokyo, so this page does not say which is cheaper or better.

## What people get wrong

- **"Nagatoro kakigori is always natural ice."** The shop switches to pure ice when the natural ice runs out.
- **"Natural ice is proven to be better."** The official comparison I found is between pure ice and machine ice. Nothing I found compares natural ice with pure ice.
- **"Kissaten are everywhere and always have been."** Their number peaked in 1981 at 154,630 and was 58,669 in 2021.
- **"Jun-kissa is just an old-fashioned word for café."** In the National Diet Library's account it came into use in the 1930s for places setting themselves apart from kafē with hostesses and alcohol.
- **"Morning service is a Komeda thing."** Komeda offers one and says it differs by shop; the custom's own history is on the Nagoya morning page.
