Kombu and bonito get most of the attention, but much of western Japan grew up on a cheaper stock: small dried fish. In Kagawa it is *iriko*, dried anchovy, and it is the reason Sanuki udon broth tastes the way it does at many shops. In northern Kyushu it is *ago*, flying fish, used for Gotō's udon and Hakata's New Year soup. This page covers both.

For dashi in general, start from the dashi overview.

## Where it came from

The National Diet Library's essay says there are hardly any clear early records of dried sardines as stock. From about the Kyōhō era (1716–36), it says, katsuobushi was expensive enough to be kept for New Year and celebrations, and everyday cooking, especially in western Japan, used dried sardines and cheaper fish flakes instead. Osaka's tourism bureau makes the same point for its own city: cheap dried anchovies from Osaka Bay and the Inland Sea supported Osaka's dashi culture.

## Iriko: Kagawa

**What it is.** *Iriko* is Kagawa's name for *niboshi*: Japanese anchovy, boiled and dried. The farm ministry says anchovy fishing thrives in the Inland Sea, and that the area around Ibuki island, off western Kagawa, is a leading producer. One business catches and processes the fish, and the catch reaches Ibuki and is boiled within just 30 minutes. In Kagawa, it says, iriko stock is essential to Japanese cooking, used in Sanuki udon and in home cooking alike, and in school lunches. Kagawa's tourism association calls Ibuki iriko the finest of the dried sardines used for udon stock, and a registered regional brand.

**Why Sanuki.** A Kagawa prefecture recipe on the farm ministry's site says that good iriko caught in the Sanuki area is thought to be one reason udon-making developed there. It also gives other reasons: much of Sanuki was tenant farmland with little rain, poor for rice, so wheat — and udon — was eaten, and the land suited good wheat. It does not say how the iriko shaped the noodles, so neither do I.

**A recipe.** The same recipe makes 1 litre of stock from 1,300 ml water, 25 g iriko and 15 g katsuobushi, with 60 ml light soy: simmer the iriko just below the boil for 20 minutes and lift it out, then add the bonito for about 10 minutes and strain. Iriko and bonito, together.

**At the udon shop.** Kagawa's tourism association says kake udon broth is mainly iriko-flavoured or bonito-flavoured, and that each shop has its own character. A soy-sauce company's account says Sanuki udon broth uses a blended light soy.

## Ago: northern Kyushu

**What it is.** Flying fish, called *ago* in northern Kyushu.

**Gotō.** The farm ministry's entry on Nagasaki's *jigoku-daki* says the standard way to eat Gotō udon is to boil the dried noodles in an iron pot and eat them straight from it with *ago* stock from flying fish caught off Gotō. (Its recipe uses granulated ago stock.) The noodles' origin has two stories: technique brought by Japanese envoy ships to Tang China, which called at Gotō, or, as some now suggest, noodles from Zhejiang.

**Hakata.** Hakata's New Year *zōni* uses grilled ago, kombu and bonito: gut and split the grilled flying fish, soak with kombu in water, remove the kombu just before the boil, add bonito and simmer about a minute. The ministry's recipe, from Nakamura Gakuen University, uses three grilled ago, 7 g of bonito and 10 cm of kombu to a litre of water. The farm ministry's *washoku* booklet notes that zōni stocks around Japan vary — flying fish, kombu, chicken — all seasoned with soy.

So *ago* stock is often a blend, too.

## Away from home

- **The style is the place.** What Kagawa and northern Kyushu offer is their stock — iriko, ago — with their soy. I could not find an official comparison of Sanuki-style udon broth in Tokyo with Kagawa's, so I make none.
- **Freshness, in one case, is documented.** For iriko, the farm ministry says the anchovy is boiled within 30 minutes of reaching Ibuki. That is a fact about how iriko is made at its source, not a claim that broth elsewhere is worse.
- **Blends are normal.** The Kagawa recipe uses iriko with bonito; Hakata uses ago with kombu and bonito.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sanuki udon is always iriko."** Shops use iriko or bonito, and recipes combine them.
- **"Dried sardines are a poor substitute."** From about the 1720s, by the National Diet Library's account, they were western Japan's everyday stock.
- **"Ago dashi is only flying fish."** Hakata's zōni recipe adds kombu and bonito.
