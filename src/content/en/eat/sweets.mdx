Japan’s traditional confectionery — *wagashi*, with its classes, seasons and bean pastes — has its own overview on this site. So do tea, the shaved ice and coffee houses that go with it, and the convenience store. This page is about the rest: the sweets a visitor actually walks past. A bowl at an old-fashioned sweet shop. A strawberry shortcake in a cake-shop window. A crepe rolled in paper on a Harajuku street. A roast sweet potato. A pudding from a convenience-store chiller that was not there last week.

They are a loose family, and I have not tried to make them a tidy one. What they share is that most of their stories are told by the companies that sell them, and that is how this page reports them.

## What it is

Five groups, each with a lineage page:

- **The sweet shop** (*amamidokoro*, 甘味処). Bowls of bean soup, bracken-starch *warabi-mochi*, *anmitsu*. A Ginza shop, Ginza Wakamatsu, is credited by the Chūō ward tourism association with inventing anmitsu in 1930; it closed at the end of 2023.
- **Western-style cakes made in Japan** (*yōgashi*). The strawberry shortcake, the pudding *à la mode*, the fruit parlour. Fujiya, Hotel New Grand and Sembikiya each tell an origin story about one of them.
- **The crepe.** Marion Crepes says its founder brought the crepe back from France and sold it rolled in paper to be eaten walking, in Shibuya in 1976 and on Harajuku’s Takeshita Street from 1977.
- **The roast sweet potato** (*yaki-imo*). The farm ministry says roast potatoes became popular in late Edo, and Kawagoe’s potatoes made their name feeding them.
- **Convenience-store sweets.** Roll cakes, cream puffs and puddings from the three big chains, priced to the yen and replaced on a schedule.

Two sweets you might expect here live elsewhere: shaved ice (*kakigori*) is on the tea and kissaten page, and *taiyaki* and *imagawayaki* are with wagashi.

## Why it is the way it is

**The same name can mean a different bowl.** The clearest case is *zenzai*. The Toraya confectioners’ archive, in a 2014 exhibition on east and west, explains that in the Kyoto–Osaka area zenzai generally means a sweet soup with whole adzuki beans in it, while in Tokyo it means mochi or steamed millet with thick bean paste poured over and no soup. What Kyoto and Osaka call zenzai, Tokyo calls *ogura-jiruko* or *inaka-jiruko*. Order by name and you get whichever one the city means.

**Western sweets were rebuilt for Japan, by the companies’ own accounts.** Fujiya says the strawberry shortcake Japan knows is not mainstream abroad and calls it a Japan-born Western sweet; it dates its own first shortcake to 1922. Hotel New Grand in Yokohama says its pudding *à la mode* was invented during the postwar occupation to please American officers’ wives, piled with canned fruit and ice cream on a special dish. Marion says Japan’s crepe went from a plated French dish to a paper-wrapped snack. None of these is confirmed by an independent source I could find, so read each as the company telling its story.

**The fruit moved to meet the cake.** A report by the Agriculture and Livestock Industries Corporation (ALIC) says strawberries were in season around May and June until the 1960s. Greenhouse growing then made autumn-planted plants the norm, which suit the year-end demand season. At Tokyo’s central wholesale market, Tochigi strawberries start arriving in November, other prefectures join in December, and arrivals peak in March. Fujiya dates its first Christmas cake to December 1910. I put the two facts side by side; I have not found a source that says one caused the other.

**Convenience-store sweets are built to rotate.** Each chain publishes prices to the yen, launches in a fixed rhythm, and in Seven-Eleven’s case limits many items to a handful of prefectures. The convenience-store lineage page sets out the October 2026 releases.

## Where to eat it

- **Tokyo** — Harajuku’s Takeshita Street for Marion’s crepes; Nihonbashi for Sembikiya, which dates itself to 1834; Ginza, though the shop credited with anmitsu has closed.
- **Yokohama** — Hotel New Grand, which still serves its pudding à la mode in the same type of dish, it says. Fujiya opened its first shop in Motomachi in 1910.
- **Kyoto** — Bunnosuke Chaya in Higashiyama, a sweet tea house known for warabi-mochi and amazake, introduced by the city’s official tourism site.
- **Kawagoe** — the town whose potatoes made Edo’s roast-potato trade, though the city says it grows far fewer now.
- **Any convenience store** — but which sweets you find depends on the prefecture.

## The same name, a different place

I looked for official sources comparing these sweets at home and away — Kyoto warabi-mochi against Tokyo’s, Yokohama’s pudding against a Tokyo café’s, Harajuku crepes against a regional mall’s — and found none. What can be said is narrower:

- **The word changes, not the quality.** Zenzai is one thing in Kyoto and Osaka and another in Tokyo, by the Toraya archive’s account.
- **Some ingredients are tied to a place.** The farm ministry says Yoshino’s winter cold and water are behind its *kuzu* starch, and that warabi-mochi in Nara is sold mainly at the prefecture’s confectioners.
- **The source town can lose the crop.** Kawagoe was a major sweet-potato area until the 1950s–60s, but the city says it now grows few, pushed out by leafy vegetables.
- **Convenience stores split by prefecture.** Seven-Eleven sold a Shizuoka-tea pudding only in Yamanashi and Shizuoka, and an Izumo zenzai pudding only in Tottori, Shimane, Hiroshima and Yamaguchi. Lawson lists different prices for some items in Okinawa.

## What people get wrong

- **"Zenzai means one thing."** It means a bean soup in Kyoto and Osaka and soupless paste over mochi in Tokyo.
- **"Japanese shortcake is a copy of a Western cake."** Fujiya, which sells it, says the Japanese version is a Japan-born sweet. That is its claim; an independent check was not available.
- **"Strawberries are a winter fruit."** In Japan they were a May–June fruit until the 1960s, says ALIC. Greenhouses moved the season.
- **"Convenience-store sweets are the same everywhere."** Prices, and sometimes the items themselves, differ by region.
- **"The anmitsu shop is still in Ginza."** Ginza Wakamatsu closed at the end of 2023.
