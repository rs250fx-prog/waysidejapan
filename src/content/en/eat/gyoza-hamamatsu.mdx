Hamamatsu gyoza are the ones you can recognise from across the room: a ring of browned dumplings filling the plate like the spokes of a wheel, with a heap of boiled bean sprouts in the hub. It is one of the few Japanese gyoza styles with a look of its own, and the story behind it is a practical one — a station-front stall that needed to fry a lot of dumplings at once.

This page is about where that plate came from, why the sprouts are there, and what the gyoza ranking Hamamatsu now leads is actually counting. For gyoza in Japan generally, start from the gyoza overview.

## Where it came from

The story belongs to one shop, and it is told as that shop's story.

The tourism bureau for Hamamatsu and Lake Hamana introduces Ishimatsu Gyoza this way: it began in 1953 as a stall in front of Hamamatsu station. As customers multiplied, the founder arranged the dumplings in a circle following the edge of the frying pan, so that more could be cooked efficiently at once. The ring was likened to a cart wheel, and the style came to be called *kuruma-mori*, "wheel serving". The space in the middle looked bare, so he filled it with boiled bean sprouts.

Two cautions. First, this is the shop's history as the bureau relays it; I have not found other Hamamatsu shops' accounts, or an independent record. Second, Ishimatsu's own page on how to fry its gyoza says only to spread sesame oil around the pan in a circle — it does not describe the ring arrangement. The ring is how the plate is served at the shop, not an instruction for cooking at home.

The city's other claim to gyoza fame is much more recent, and it is statistical (see below).

## What makes it Hamamatsu gyoza

**The ring.** Shizuoka prefecture's statistics office describes Hamamatsu gyoza as fried in a circle with bean sprouts in the middle. At Ishimatsu the wheel comes in 20 or 25 pieces.

**The bean sprouts.** Ishimatsu's own explanation is not decorative. Eating gyoza one after another leaves the mouth oily, and the sprouts reset it. It says the sprouts that work are thick and full of water.

**The sauce.** Ishimatsu makes its own slightly sweet vinegar-soy, which it says suits both the gyoza and the sprouts, and leaves chilli oil to the customer.

**What it is not.** No source used for this page says every Hamamatsu shop serves the ring, and a local definition of "Hamamatsu gyoza" that circulates in Japanese could not be traced to a primary source. Read the ring as the town's best-known style, not its law.

## Why Hamamatsu

The round plate made Hamamatsu gyoza recognisable. The household survey made it famous.

The government's household survey records what households of two or more people spend each year on many foods, by prefectural capital and major city. The *gyōza* line, as Hamamatsu city explains it, covers fresh or pan-fried gyoza from supermarkets and similar shops plus takeaway from gyoza specialists. It does not cover frozen gyoza or takeaway from Chinese restaurants, and the Shizuoka statistics office adds that eating out is not counted either — so the number does not show how many gyoza anyone eats.

Utsunomiya led that line for 15 years up to 2010. In 2011 Hamamatsu was first for the first time, at ¥4,314 against Utsunomiya's ¥3,737 and a national average of ¥2,181. The statistics office points out that on the three-year average for 2009–11, Utsunomiya was still ahead (¥4,686).

Since then, in the decade Hamamatsu city has tabulated, Hamamatsu has been first in 2015 (¥4,646), 2016 (¥4,819), 2018 (¥3,501), 2020 (¥3,765), 2023 (¥4,041), 2024 (¥4,066) and 2025 (¥4,046). Utsunomiya took 2017 and 2019; Miyazaki took 2021 and 2022. On the survey's 2023–25 average, Hamamatsu leads at ¥4,051, ahead of Miyazaki (¥3,478) and Utsunomiya (¥3,192), with the national figure at ¥1,984.

The definition matters when reading the ranking: takeaway from gyoza specialists counts, restaurant meals and frozen packs do not.

## How to order it

**Order the wheel.** At Ishimatsu the plate is 20 or 25 pieces with sprouts.

**Eat the sprouts between the gyoza.** That is what the shop says they are for.

**Use the house sauce first.** It is a sweetish vinegar-soy made by the shop; chilli oil is there if you want it.

## Not all of Japan's gyoza is this

- **Utsunomiya** has no single style. Its shops are known for serving pan-fried, deep-fried and boiled gyoza, often at the same price — six for ¥380 at Utsunomiya Minmin's Miyamirai shop — and its festival in November sells plates of three to five for ¥200. See the Utsunomiya page.
- **Kobe** dips pan-fried gyoza in a miso sauce, a local dish the farm ministry traces to a diner opened after the war by a man back from Manchuria.
- **Fukuoka** eats small one-bite gyoza in the Nakasu drinking district; the city's tourism site features a shop there opened in 1949 by a founder back from the Chinese mainland, filled with onion, mince and garlic chives.

## Hamamatsu gyoza away from Hamamatsu

**The look may not travel.** The ring with sprouts is a Hamamatsu shop style. How often you will see it served that way in Tokyo or Osaka, I could not find out, so I would not expect it.

**The plate is sized differently.** In Tokyo the national chains sell gyoza in threes and sixes — Hidakaya three for ¥160 and six for ¥300, Gyoza no Ohsho's Kanto menu six for ¥363. The wheel of 20 or 25 is a Hamamatsu idea of a plate.

**The ranking is about home.** Hamamatsu households spent ¥4,051 a year on shop-bought gyoza on the 2023–25 average; households in central Tokyo spent ¥2,110. That says Hamamatsu families bring home more gyoza. It says nothing about whose restaurants are better, and this site does not rank them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Hamamatsu eats the most gyoza."** It spends the most on shop-bought gyoza in a household survey that excludes frozen gyoza and eating out. Nobody counts the dumplings.
- **"Hamamatsu has always been number one."** It first came top in 2011. Utsunomiya led for 15 years before that and has come top twice since; Miyazaki has too.
- **"The bean sprouts are a garnish."** By the shop's account they are there to clear your palate between dumplings, and started as a way to fill the middle of the ring.
- **"Every Hamamatsu shop serves a ring."** It is the best-known style, told as one shop's invention. I could not confirm that all shops follow it.
