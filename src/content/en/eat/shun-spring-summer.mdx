Spring in Japanese food starts with something bitter and something dug out of the ground: mountain greens and bamboo shoots. Summer turns to gourds and river fish, and to food that looks cool. This page goes through the half of the year from spring to summer using the farm ministry's own lists, follows the bamboo-shoot harvest north from Kyushu, and ends with the one spring product whose price a company states: a cherry-blossom drink.

For what *shun* means and how chains sell the season, start from the shun overview.

## Where it came from

The idea that each ingredient has a peak, and that you can enjoy it at its first appearance (*hashiri*), its peak (*shun*) and its last days (*nagori*), comes from the farm ministry's description of Japanese cooking on its washoku learning pages. Spring and summer are where the *hashiri* idea is easiest to see: the ministry says many vegetables at the *hashiri* stage are tender and good raw.

## What is in season

The ministry has at least four lists. They overlap, and they do not match, so they are given separately.

**Spring.**

- **The children's food-education page:** rape blossoms (*nanohana*), strawberries, short-neck clams (*asari*) and bamboo shoots.
- **The washoku page** calls spring the season of sprouting: bamboo shoots, butterbur buds (*fukinotō*), rape blossoms, and *sakura-dai*, sea bream in the cherry-blossom season.
- **The recipe page** says spring is the season when bitter vegetables such as wild mountain greens (*sansai*) taste good.
- **An English-language calendar** the ministry prepared for restaurants serving overseas visitors lists, for spring, asari clams, sakura shrimp, green laver, Spanish mackerel, whitebait, rockfish, yamame trout, red sea bream, strawberries, iyokan and hassaku oranges, and among vegetables Japanese parsley, fava beans, snow peas, bamboo shoots, rape blossom buds, chives, the flower of Japanese pepper, Japanese honewort and mugwort. Its note on *sansai* says they are edible plants that grow naturally in the fields or mountains. The Japanese text of the calendar could not be read, so its month ranges are not given here.

**Summer.**

- **The children's page:** cucumbers, tomatoes, horse mackerel (*aji*) and watermelon.
- **The washoku page** calls summer the season of the sun. Cucumber, pumpkin, winter melon and other gourds are described as foods that cool the body and calm summer's heat. Tomatoes (for vitamin C) are in season too. The fish it names are eel, sweetfish (*ayu*) and pike conger (*hamo*). In Japan's humid summer, it says, colourful dishes that make you feel cool are served.
- **The recipe page** gives edamame as a summer ingredient, in a cold soup.
- **The English-language calendar** lists conger eel, sweetfish, horse mackerel, abalone, char, sea urchin, turban shell, pike conger, the first bonito of the season, firefly squid, sea squirt, cherries, watermelon, peaches, figs, okra, sweet green peppers, green soybeans, water shield, corn and green Japanese peppercorns, with a note that it is customary for Japanese to eat eel on *doyō no ushi no hi*.

Eel and that midsummer day have their own overview on this site, as do shaved ice, sōmen and chilled ramen; this page does not repeat them.

## What makes it spring: bamboo shoots

The ministry's *aff* magazine (March 2021) gives the clearest picture of a spring ingredient moving through the country.

- **Mōsōchiku** usually starts in Kyushu around March, and the harvest moves north until around May. In the south of Kagoshima prefecture, early-dug shoots are shipped from around December.
- **Madake** comes in May and June, **hachiku** in May, and **nemagari-dake** (*chishima-zasa*) around June.
- **Shihōchiku**, grown mainly in Kōchi, comes in October to early November — a bamboo shoot outside spring altogether.

Production is mostly in western Japan, in prefectures such as Fukuoka and Kagoshima. The article gives national production in 2019 as 22,284.9 tonnes, citing the Forestry Agency.

**Preparing them.** The same article describes a home method: on the day of purchase, boil the shoots with rice bran for about 30 minutes, then leave them to cool in the water for four to five hours. That is one household method the magazine gives, not a rule.

## And the warning that comes with sansai

Bitter mountain greens are a spring pleasure, and the ministry pairs them with a warning. It publishes a leaflet because, it says, many cases of food poisoning are reported from mistaking poisonous plants for vegetables or wild greens, and elderly people are involved in many of them. Its examples of lookalikes include:

- butterbur and its buds (*fuki*, *fukinotō*) against *hashiridokoro* (Japanese belladonna) and *fukujusō* (Amur adonis);
- *urui* (hosta shoots) against false hellebores and colchicum;
- garlic chives against daffodils.

## The shops, and what they charge

Cherry blossom is the spring flavour the official sources show being sold. Starbucks Japan's press release of 12 February 2026 announced three seasonal-limited drinks from 18 February 2026. Prices are tax included, takeaway / eat-in, as posted in the release; the two figures follow Japan's consumption tax rates of 8% for takeaway and 10% for eating in, as of 2026-10-10.

- *Sakura saku yo* white peach Frappuccino, Tall: ¥687 / ¥700
- *Sakura saku yo* latte: ¥618 / ¥630
- *Sakura saku yo* white peach soda: ¥579 / ¥590

The release gives a start date but no end date, warns that items may run out temporarily or end early, and says the range was planned both for people who like the taste of sakura and for people who want the feeling of sakura.

What I could not find: the history of eating cherry blossoms, how salted blossoms are made, and an official company source for strawberry as a limited flavour. Sakura-mochi and hanami dumplings belong to the wagashi overview.

## How to eat it

The official sources describe ways of thinking about the season more than rules for eating it. From the ministry's pages:

- **Eat the first of the season early.** The *hashiri* stage is described as getting ahead of the season, and many vegetables are tender enough to eat raw.
- **Expect bitterness in spring.** The recipe page singles out bitter vegetables and mountain greens as what is good in spring.
- **Expect summer food to look cool.** The washoku page describes colourful, cool-looking dishes and gourds that cool the body.

## Not all of Japan's spring is this

The same ingredient comes into season at different times in different places. Bamboo shoots are the clearest case: March in Kyushu, moving north to May, December in southern Kagoshima, autumn for one kind in Kōchi. Buckwheat has a summer crop as well as an autumn one; a farm ministry page on Nagano soba calls summer's new soba *natsu-shin*. The autumn and winter page has more on soba.

## Spring away from where it grows

In Tokyo, produce arrives through the central wholesale market. The market's children's page (figures from 2008) says vegetables and fruit come in at night and go to auction from about 7 a.m., and that growers use greenhouses and other methods to ship when supply is low and prices are high. The ministry's children's page says many foods sold all year regardless of season are grown in heated greenhouses or imported.

The market publishes a month-by-month "relay" of where 13 vegetables come from, but in animations and PDFs I could not read, and bamboo shoots are not one of the 13. So I cannot say when Kyushu's bamboo shoots reach Tokyo, or what they cost there. How Tokyo supermarkets handle strawberries in winter, and other out-of-season produce, I could not find in any official source.

## What people get wrong

- **"Bamboo shoots are a single-season, single-place food."** The harvest moves north from Kyushu over months, starts in December in southern Kagoshima, and one kind comes in autumn.
- **"Wild greens are safe if they look like the picture."** The ministry warns about lookalikes and reports many poisoning cases.
- **"Summer food is about eel."** Eel is on the ministry's list, but so are gourds, tomatoes, sweetfish, pike conger, edamame and much else.
- **"Sakura flavour is an old spring dish."** What the official sources show is a company's seasonal-limited drink from February 2026. The history of eating the blossoms I could not confirm.
