faq:
  - q: Are mentaiko and Hakata ramen on the farm ministry's list of Fukuoka dishes?
    a: No. Fukuoka's 30 entries include mizutaki, game-ni, Hakata zōni and Yanagawa nabe, but neither mentaiko nor Hakata ramen. The ministry's list is chosen region by region, so this says only what Fukuoka put on it.
  - q: Where did mizutaki begin?
    a: The farm ministry gives two accounts. Its dish page says it spread from Nagasaki, where foreign ships called, and grew out of a Chinese-style chicken simmer blended with Western soups. Its Fukuoka story names Hayashida Heizaburō, from Nagasaki, who learned Western cooking in Hong Kong and opened a mizutaki restaurant in Fukuoka in 1905. This page gives both.
  - q: Why are hiyajiru and chicken nanban missing from Miyazaki's 100-year foods?
    a: Because the two lists are chosen separately. Both dishes are in the farm ministry's Miyazaki list. Miyazaki's three 100-year foods, as I counted them, are chestnut ohagi, natōfu and the fish culture of Hososhima in Hyūga.
  - q: Can I eat raw chicken safely in Kagoshima?
    a: The farm ministry says Kagoshima and Miyazaki set their own prefectural standards for processing chicken to be eaten raw, and only meat that meets them goes on sale for that use. Because hygiene is hard to manage, it is eaten more in restaurants than at home.

Seven Kyushu prefectures: Fukuoka, Saga, Nagasaki, Kumamoto, Ōita, Miyazaki and Kagoshima. Their official dish descriptions keep returning to chicken, sweet potato, flour dumplings and contact abroad, but each tells its own story. This page goes prefecture by prefecture and notes where the sources disagree.

For how Japan records local dishes, and what the farm ministry's list is, start from the [local-dishes overview](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori/).

## Where it came from

### Fukuoka

**Mizutaki.** Chicken simmered on the bone into a broth, with seasonal vegetables, eaten with ponzu. The farm ministry gives two accounts of its start:

- **The dish page**: it first spread when foreign ships called at Nagasaki and later reached Fukuoka. A Chinese-style chicken simmered in light salt caught on in Nagasaki and merged with Western soups.
- **The ministry's Fukuoka story**: Hayashida Heizaburō, from Nagasaki, used Western cooking he had learned in Hong Kong to open a mizutaki restaurant in Fukuoka city in 1905.

This page gives both. Mizutaki is always eaten at the *naorai*, the ritual meal, of the Hakata Gion Yamakasa festival, and the ministry says *Hakata jidori*, a chicken brand the prefecture developed in 1999, suits it best. For the hotpot itself, see [mizutaki](/en/eat/chicken-mizutaki/).

**Game-ni.** Chicken and vegetables simmered together, made for New Year, festivals, weddings and the Yamakasa celebrations. Two theories for the name, both from the ministry:

- From the Hakata dialect *gamekurikomu*, to gather things together.
- From soldiers sent to Korea in the Bunroku campaign, who stewed *dobugame*, soft-shelled turtle, with whatever they had.

This page does not choose. The ministry sets it apart from the *chikuzen-ni* known across Japan: chikuzen-ni can use boneless chicken, while game-ni sometimes uses chicken on the bone. On Shikanoshima in Fukuoka city, the number of ingredients is always kept odd.

### Saga

**Mutsugoro kabayaki.** The Ariake Sea has a tidal range of about six metres, and the ministry says the mudskipper (*mutsugorō*) lives in Japan only there and in part of the Yatsushiro Sea. It is skewered alive, grilled over charcoal, then simmered in sugar, soy sauce and sake. The season is May to August, and the ministry says it is generally eaten only then.

**Suko-zushi.** A boxed sushi from the Suko district of Shiroishi. The story is that more than 500 years ago the local lord worked to improve the rice, and the grateful people presented him with sushi. It should be topped with grilled mudskipper, but since that is now hard to get, prawns or gizzard shad sometimes stand in.

### Nagasaki

**Gu-zōni (Shimabara gu-zōni).** A New Year soup crowded with ingredients, made in an earthenware pot. It is *said* to have begun in 1637, when Amakusa Shirō and his followers held out in the Shimabara Rebellion and cooked their rice cakes with whatever the hills and sea gave them. The ministry says it was chosen in 2007 for the "100 local dishes of farming, mountain and fishing villages".

**Ōmura-zushi.** A pressed sushi from Ōmura. It is said to date from the Muromachi period, when the lord Ōmura Sumikore returned to reclaim his domain and the people made sushi to celebrate. The generous sugar is said to show hospitality. Since 2000 a lower-sugar "new Ōmura-zushi" has been promoted.

**Hikado.** A Nagasaki stew handed down since the early 1600s, said to come from a stew eaten by Portuguese missionaries. The name is said to come from the Portuguese for "finely chopped" (*picado*). Vegetables and fish or chicken are diced and simmered, then thickened with grated sweet potato. *Ryōtei* also serve it as one course of *shippoku*.

**Champon and sara udon** are both on the Nagasaki list. See [champon](/en/eat/champon/) and [sara udon](/en/eat/champon-sara-udon/).

### Kumamoto

**Karashi renkon.** Lotus root stuffed with miso and mustard, battered and fried. The farm ministry's story: in 1632 (Kan'ei 9) a priest sought nourishing food for Hosokawa Tadatoshi, the sickly first Hosokawa lord of Higo, who refused lotus root as grown in mud until it came stuffed and fried. Its cross-section looked like the family crest, so it was kept within the house until the Meiji Restoration. The ministry's Kumamoto story differs in detail: a Zen priest recommended it, and the cook who devised it was given a sword and a surname. The ministry's dish page says it is still made only in Kumamoto; I have not checked that against any other source.

**Basashi.** Raw horse meat. Kumamoto produces the most horse meat in Japan. The ministry gives several origins, one being that Katō Kiyomasa's army ran out of food in Korea and ate its warhorses. It also says it spread after the war, when people in Aso, a warhorse-breeding area, ate it in the food shortage, and reached restaurants in the 1955–64 period. For the dish, see [basashi](/en/eat/meat-basashi/).

**Dagojiru.** Soup with flour dumplings (*dago* in Kumamoto dialect), eaten between farm work. The ministry says it is eaten across Kyushu, but that many areas of Kumamoto use sweet potato in the dumplings. See [dagojiru and dangojiru](/en/eat/udon-dagojiru/).

### Ōita

**Toriten.** Chicken tempura. The ministry says it is thought to have begun in the early Shōwa era at Tōyōken in Beppu, the prefecture's first restaurant, where boneless thigh was fried tempura-style because bone-in chicken seemed hard for women to eat. It also gives another story, that when chicken was expensive, a thick batter made it go further. This page gives both. A 2019 national survey of prefectural capitals and major cities put Ōita city first for chicken eaten per household.

**Ryūkyū.** Fresh local fish dressed in soy sauce, sake, mirin, sesame and ginger, once fishermen's own food and a way to keep fish. Two theories for the name: Ōita fishermen learned it from Okinawan (Ryūkyū) fishermen, or it grew out of *rikyū-ae*, a name for sesame dressing. This page does not choose.

**Atama ryōri.** "Head cooking" from Taketa, far inland. Sea fish were precious in the Edo period, so the gills, jaws and innards of large fish such as *ara* and *kue* were blanched and eaten, nothing wasted.

### Miyazaki

**Hiyajiru.** Fish, sesame and miso ground and toasted in a mortar, thinned with stock, chilled, topped with cucumber and poured over hot barley rice. The sources differ on where it began:

- **The dish page**: farmers in the hot summer put raw miso on barley rice and poured water over it.
- **The ministry's Miyazaki story**: it is said to be a rice-with-soup dish spread around Japan by monks in the Kamakura period.

This page gives both.

**Chicken nanban.** Known as a Nobeoka dish. The dish page says it began as staff food at a Western-style restaurant in the 1955–64 period; the Miyazaki story says two cooks at a well-known Western restaurant in the mid-Shōwa era, and that the original used breast meat. Tartar sauce made it popular. It later spread to school lunches and homes. *Nanban*, the ministry says, is a word from the Portuguese of the Warring States era and refers to the technique of marinating in sweet vinegar with chilli.

### Kagoshima

**Keihan.** From Amami, where Kasari is said to be its home. Shredded chicken, shredded omelette, shiitake and pickled papaya go on rice, and chicken stock is poured over. It is said to have been made to entertain officials from mainland Satsuma when Amami was under the domain. The ministry says pouring the stock over is a Shōwa-era change. Nagasaki has a different dish with the same written name; they are separate entries.

**Tsukeage.** Fish paste mixed with tofu and *akumochi-zake*, a local sake, then fried and sweetened with sugar. Elsewhere it is often called *satsuma-age*. Two theories:

- **Shimazu Nariakira** took the idea from Kishū hanpen and kamaboko and fried it to keep in the hot, humid climate.
- **Chiki-agi**, an Okinawan dish, whose name was worn down to *tsukeage*.

This page does not choose. The Cultural Agency's entry says Ichikikushikino is also called the birthplace of tsukeage.

**Tonkotsu.** Not the ramen (for that, see [Kagoshima ramen](/en/eat/ramen-kagoshima/)). Pork ribs on the bone, sizzled with sweet-potato shōchū and simmered with miso and brown sugar, begun as outdoor cooking for Satsuma samurai.

## What makes it Kyushu

**The lists.** The farm ministry's database has, as I counted them: Fukuoka 30, Saga 25, Nagasaki 30, Kumamoto 29, Ōita 30, Miyazaki 30, Kagoshima 30 — 204 in all. Among them:

- **Fukuoka**: game-ni, Hakata zōni, mizutaki, *okyūto*, *kashiwa-meshi*, *goma-saba*, Yanagawa nabe.
- **Saga**: *funanko-gui*, Suko-zushi, game-ni, mutsugoro kabayaki, *warasubo* miso soup.
- **Nagasaki**: gu-zōni, Ōmura-zushi, sara udon, champon, hikado, Nagasaki tempura, *kankoro-mochi*, Nagasaki milkshake.
- **Kumamoto**: karashi renkon, dagojiru, *ikinari dango*, basashi, *taipien*.
- **Ōita**: ryūkyū, dangojiru, atama ryōri, toriten, *yaseuma*, *kihan* (yellow rice).
- **Miyazaki**: chicken nanban, hiyajiru, *natōfu*, *kappo-dori*, miso korobakashi, *obi-ten*.
- **Kagoshima**: keihan, satsuma-jiru, tsukeage, tonkotsu, *akumaki*, *karukan*, *shirokuma*, torisashi.

**The other list.** The Cultural Agency's 100-year foods, as I counted them on each prefecture's page: Fukuoka 12, Saga 5, Nagasaki 8, Kumamoto 4, Ōita 5, Miyazaki 3, Kagoshima 9. The two lists overlap in places — karashi renkon, ryūkyū, atama ryōri, Suko-zushi, tsukeage and akumaki are on both — and not in others. Miyazaki's hiyajiru and chicken nanban are on the farm ministry's list but not among its 100-year foods.

**What recurs.** Game-ni is on both the Fukuoka and Saga lists. *Gane* (Kumamoto's *gane-age*) is on the Kumamoto, Miyazaki and Kagoshima lists. Dumpling soups turn up in Fukuoka, Saga, Kumamoto and Ōita.

**What is missing.** These are not in the farm ministry's list: mentaiko and Hakata ramen (Fukuoka); Yobuko squid, Saga beef and Sicilian rice (Saga); shippoku and castella (Nagasaki); *akaushi* beef (Kumamoto); *seki-aji* (Ōita); charcoal-grilled jidori (Miyazaki); and black pork as an item of its own (Kagoshima). That is all this says; shippoku, for one, appears inside the hikado page. Saga's Sicilian rice is a 100-year food in the "future" category. For Hakata ramen, see [Hakata ramen](/en/eat/ramen-hakata/).

## Why Kyushu

I found no public source that describes the food of Kyushu as a whole. What follows sets the prefectures' own descriptions side by side.

**Volcanic soil and sweet potato.** The ministry's Kumamoto story calls it the "land of fire", with the active volcano Aso. Its Kagoshima story says the Kirishima volcanic belt runs through the prefecture and most of it is covered by *shirasu*, volcanic deposits, on which sweet potato grows well. Sweet potato stands in for rice in Kumamoto's *koppa-mochi*, Nagasaki's kankoro-mochi and *rokubei*, Kagoshima's sweet-potato rice and Miyazaki's *nerikuri*. Each prefecture gives its own reason.

**Flour.** Ōita's dangojiru page says the prefecture has many plateaus unsuited to rice, so a culture of flour food took root. Its dumplings are stretched into thin strips and put in a soup such as barley miso.

**Chicken.** Mizutaki in Fukuoka, toriten in Ōita, chicken nanban and jidori in Miyazaki, satsuma-jiru, keihan and torisashi in Kagoshima. The ministry's Kagoshima satsuma-jiru page says Satsuma samurai held cockfights and cooked the loser with vegetables on the spot. Keeping chickens at home and killing one for guests or celebrations comes up in the Ōita, Miyazaki and Kagoshima descriptions alike.

**Contact abroad.** Nagasaki mixed Portuguese, Dutch and Chinese influence; Kagoshima traded with Okinawa. Hence hikado, and the "nanban" in chicken nanban.

**Shōchū.** Iki's barley shōchū, Kuma's rice shōchū, and sweet-potato shōchū in Kagoshima's tonkotsu. The Kagoshima story says the warm climate did not suit sake brewing, so *akumochi-zake* was made instead. For the drink, see [shōchū](/en/eat/drinks-shochu/).

**Sweetness.** Individual entries mention sugar: Kagoshima's tsukeage and barley miso, Ōmura-zushi, sweet soy sauce with basashi, and Miyazaki's obi-ten. I found no public source that says this of Kyushu as a whole, so I do not say it.

## Where to eat it, and what it costs

**The places**, as the farm ministry describes them:

- **Mizutaki**: there are many specialist restaurants.
- **Gu-zōni and Ōmura-zushi**: home dishes for New Year and celebrations, now served all year in some restaurants.
- **Suko-zushi**: roadside stations, farm stands and supermarkets.
- **Karashi renkon and basashi**: specialist shops and supermarkets.
- **Ryūkyū**: as *ryūkyū-don* in restaurants at sightseeing spots.
- **Atama ryōri**: some restaurants still make it if you book.
- **Keihan**: specialist restaurants, mainly in Amami but also on the mainland.
- **Tsukeage**: supermarkets and roadside stations.

**Prices.** I found no official price for a dish at the source clearly stated as including tax, so this page gives none there. The only tax-inclusive price I found is in Tokyo (see below).

## How it is eaten

- **Toriten**: with vinegared soy sauce and mustard, ponzu, or *kabosu* citrus.
- **Basashi**: with sweet soy sauce, ginger and garlic. The ministry says horse fat melts at a low temperature, which is why people eat it promptly.
- **Hiyajiru**: cold, over hot barley rice; the ministry says it is known nationally against summer fatigue.

**When.** Mudskipper from May to August. Gu-zōni at New Year. Game-ni at New Year, festivals and weddings, and in Saga at *okunchi* festivals. Suko-zushi at festivals, for guests, and when married daughters come home. Satsuma-jiru often in winter. Ōita's dangojiru from early autumn into winter.

## Not all of Kyushu is this

**The same name, different dishes.** Keihan in Kagoshima and the dish written the same way in Nagasaki are separate entries. Game-ni is on both the Fukuoka and Saga lists; Saga's page traces it to Hideyoshi's army, quartered in Hakata, stewing soft-shelled turtle with vegetables.

**Dago or dango.** The ministry's Ōita page says the soup is *dago-jiru* in Fukuoka and Kumamoto and *dango-jiru* in Ōita. Kumamoto often uses sweet potato in the dumplings; Ōita stretches them into strips.

**Two champon.** The ministry calls pork-bone Nagasaki champon and chicken-bone Obama champon the prefecture's two main styles.

**Rules, not habits.** Basashi is always frozen before distribution, against parasites. Raw chicken goes on sale only if it meets Kagoshima's or Miyazaki's prefectural standard. The ministry describes these as systems, and so does this page.

## Eating it at the source, and getting there

**Ureshino Onsen and hot-spring tofu.** Ureshino Onsen yudōfu is a Cultural Agency 100-year food, certified in fiscal 2024 (Reiwa 6) in the traditional category. It is not in the farm ministry's Saga list. The agency's page says Ureshino was a post town popular with travellers in the Edo period, and that tofu simmered in the spring water melts into a soft texture. The haiku poet Taneda Santōka, staying in Ureshino, wrote in his diary that the broth "turns like milk".

**Obama and champon.** The farm ministry's champon page says the Obama-machi Onsen ryokan association in Unzen city uses Obama champon in events to promote the area.

**Beppu and toriten.** The ministry says toriten is thought to have begun at Tōyōken in Beppu. It says nothing about the hot springs, and I do not claim a link. For the baths, see [Beppu](/en/soak/beppu/).

**No link found.** Saga's story names Takeo and Ureshino as its leading spa towns but does not tie them to dishes. I found no official source tying a dish to [Yufuin](/en/soak/yufuin/) or [Kurokawa](/en/soak/kurokawa/). Kagoshima's story describes Kirishima's hot springs and, next, the sweet-potato dishes of the area, without connecting them. For the region's springs, see [onsen in Kyushu](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/).

**Getting there.** Prefectural tourism sites, with no update date given; check times before you travel.

- **Fukuoka**: about 5 minutes by subway from Fukuoka airport to Hakata station; at shortest about 33 minutes by bus from Kitakyushu airport to Kokura. The prefecture's site gives about 1 hour 45 minutes from Kagoshima by shinkansen (its map information is as of February 2023).
- **Kagoshima**: Kagoshima's site gives Hakata to Kagoshima-Chūō as about 1 hour 16 minutes at fastest — a different figure from Fukuoka's, perhaps on a different basis. Kumamoto to Kagoshima-Chūō about 53 minutes. About 1 hour 45 minutes to 2 hours from Haneda to Kagoshima airport, then about 38 minutes by bus to Kagoshima-Chūō. From the airport, Kirishima Onsen Maruo is about 30 minutes.
- **Nagasaki**: about 1 hour 20 minutes from Hakata by train, or about 2 hours 30 minutes by bus.
- **Kumamoto**: about 40 minutes from Hakata; about 50 minutes by airport limousine bus from Aso Kumamoto airport to the city centre.
- **Beppu**: about 50–55 minutes by bus from Ōita airport; about 2 hours from Hakata by limited express.
- **Miyazaki and Saga**: I found no official journey times, so I give none.

## Kyushu food away from Kyushu

- **Ōita.** Zarai Ōita, the prefecture's official shop on 3F of Hulic Square Tokyo in Yūrakuchō. Dinner is served on weekdays. Courses change monthly and cost ¥12,650 to ¥27,650, tax included, plus a 10% service charge, booked three days ahead (checked 11 October 2026). They include ryūkyū and kihan, and the shop says seasonal ingredients are sent from Ōita on the morning of the day.
- **Nagasaki.** Nihonbashi Nagasaki-kan has a shop, a tourist desk, a small eating area serving champon and castella, and an event space.
- **Kagoshima.** Kagoshima Yūrakukan in Yūrakuchō, set up by the prefecture, has a sweet-potato shop, a restaurant and a brand shop.
- **Miyazaki.** Shinjuku Miyazaki-kan KONNE sells products on 1F and has a restaurant on 2F.
- **Fukuoka.** The prefecture's tourism site lists The Hakata in the Tokyo Kōtsū Kaikan, centred on tonkotsu ramen and mentaiko, and Kōjimachi Nadaman Fukuoka Bettei, with a Hakata jidori mizutaki course.
- **Kumamoto.** I could confirm only that the prefecture's Tokyo office is on 3F of Ginza Kumamoto-kan.
- **Saga.** No permanent Tokyo shop. In a reply dated 18 September 2025, the prefecture said the cost of a Tokyo shop is large, and selling widely through department stores, retailers and hotels works better. It gave a Saga fair at the Haneda Excel Hotel Tokyu, with Saga beef and Yobuko squid, as an example.

**What changes.** At the source, ryūkyū is fishermen's and home food; in Tokyo it is one course of a meal from ¥12,650. No official source compares taste, price or portion, and I make none.

## What people get wrong

- **"Mentaiko and Hakata ramen are Fukuoka's official local dishes."** Neither is in the farm ministry's list.
- **"Castella and shippoku are on Nagasaki's list."** Neither is in the farm ministry's list; shippoku appears only as the setting for hikado.
- **"Chicken nanban is a 100-year food."** It is on the farm ministry's list, not among Miyazaki's 100-year foods.
- **"Ureshino's hot-spring tofu is a farm ministry dish."** It is a Cultural Agency 100-year food and is not in the farm ministry's Saga list.
- **"Kyushu is one cuisine."** The sources speak prefecture by prefecture; even dumpling soup changes at the border.
