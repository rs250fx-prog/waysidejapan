*Kome no Akita wa sake no kuni* — "Akita of rice is a land of sake" — is a catchphrase the prefecture quotes on its own website. Its explanation is a list: good rice and water from a rich natural setting, a cold and snowy climate suited to brewing, and brewing skill both traditional and advanced. What sets Akita's official record apart is the rice and the yeast. The prefecture and its brewers bred their own sake rice to stand beside Yamada Nishiki, and a yeast first isolated in Akita in 1930 is still sold to brewers across Japan.

For what sake is and how to read a label, start from the [sake overview](/en/eat/sake/).

## How Akita describes itself

The Akita brewers' cooperative calls Akita a "kingdom of fine sake" that stands out in both production and consumption, a land of long, cold, snowy winters where people have always drunk a lot. It gives no figures or years for that, and I have not checked the consumption side against national statistics.

What the national tax statistics do show, for fiscal 2023: Akita produced 12,321 kilolitres of sake, about 3.8% of Japan's total — a production figure, not shipments — from 38 licensed production sites. The cooperative's website has pages for 33 member breweries, and 27 breweries took part in its 2026 sake tasting event in Akita. These are different ways of counting, so none of them is "the number of breweries in Akita".

## The rice

**What sake rice is.** Akita's food research centre describes sake rice as rice with qualities suited to brewing: larger grains than table rice, with a white, starchy core, the *shinpaku*, near the centre. The cooperative adds that premium sake such as ginjō uses large, soft grains with that core — Miyama Nishiki, Akita Sake Komachi, Yamada Nishiki — and that table rice such as Akita Komachi and Menkoina is used in brewing too.

**A prefecture breeds its own.** In 1988 Akita prefecture — its agricultural experiment station and what was then its brewing experiment station — and the brewers' association began a joint programme under the slogan "the best sake rice grown in Akita, for rice-country Akita". The goal was a variety that could be grown anywhere in the prefecture and match Yamada Nishiki, which the research centre calls the finest sake rice in Japan. The centre lists Gin no Sei in 1993, Aki no Sei in 2000, Misato Nishiki in 2002 and Akita Sake Komachi in 2004. The same document describes the programme as running for 15 years, from 1988 to 2002, and mixes registration years and development years in its tables, so treat the years as the centre's own.

**Akita Sake Komachi.** The agricultural experiment station bred it to suit Akita's weather, to grow like Miyama Nishiki and brew like Yamada Nishiki. Against Miyama Nishiki, it ripens at about the same time, stands up better to lodging, has better grain quality, heavier grains and less crude protein; its brewing qualities are equal to Yamada Nishiki's, and the station saw it as promising for ginjō. The food research centre adds that its grains are as large as Yamada Nishiki's, with as little protein, and that it dissolves more easily; the sake has few off-flavours, a refined sweetness and savouriness, and a light finish. As of 2021 it was the most widely planted sake rice in Akita, used by many breweries mainly for junmai and ginjō.

**The others.** *Miyama Nishiki* was registered in 1978, bred in Nagano by irradiating the variety Takane Nishiki. *Misato Nishiki*, a cross of Yamada Nishiki and Miyama Nishiki, was the first sake rice in the Tōhoku region with Yamada Nishiki as a parent. *Gin no Sei*, the prefecture's first variety, has a small core and is often used for unpasteurised sake. In the 2020 crop inspection figures quoted by the centre, Akita Sake Komachi came to 38,217 bales, Miyama Nishiki 26,483, Misato Nishiki 4,267 and Gin no Sei 3,283. I have not found more recent figures.

**The newest.** *Ichihozumi* (Koshitanrei × Akita Sake Komachi) and *Hyakuden* (an Akita sake line × Misato Nishiki). In the 2021 brewing year eight companies in the prefecture used Ichihozumi and fourteen used Hyakuden. The prefecture's 2023 sake guidebook also introduces its own yeasts, AKITA Yukiguni (UT-1 and UT-2).

## The yeast

The cooperative says a yeast isolated at the Aramasa brewery in Akita city in 1930 was adopted as *Kyōkai No. 6*, and that it is the oldest of the association yeasts still on sale. A joint project of the prefecture and the cooperative produced the Akita flower yeast, AK-1, in 1990; it was adopted as Kyōkai No. 1501 in 1996. In 1991 Akita launched a shared brand, *Akita Shun Ginjō*, under a prefectural certification scheme for regional sake.

## Water, cold and people

**Water.** The cooperative explains that brewing needs 20 to 30 times as much water as rice by weight, for washing, for the mash and for the final dilution, so the quality of each matters. It says that, compared with Nada's hard water, Akita's water is generally soft, giving a mellow, fine-grained sake. That is the cooperative's description; it gives no measurements.

**Cold.** In the cooperative's account, brewing starts around November and peaks from December to February. Snow cleans the air, breweries buried in snow keep a steady temperature inside, and the mash ferments slowly at low temperatures, which it says makes a fine-grained sake. It calls this *Akita-ryū kan-zukuri*, Akita-style cold brewing.

**People.** The food research centre gives the reasons for Akita sake's quality as the cold, snowy climate, rice country, clean water, and the skill of the *Sannai tōji*, the master brewers of Sannai. I could not open a public source on their history or numbers. On the word *tōji* itself, the cooperative says there are several theories, and that the strongest is that brewing was once the work of the woman of the house, called *toji*. It does not set out the others.

## The protected name

There is none. Akita is not on the national tax agency's list of geographical indications for sake. A region name on a label still means that all of the sake was brewed in that region, but there are no GI rules on Akita water or bottling. For how a GI works, see [Niigata sake](/en/eat/sake-niigata/).

## Akita food and Akita sake

I could not find an official pairing. The farm ministry's Akita recipes use sake as an ingredient — kiritanpo hotpot ("add sake or salt to taste"), the main pickling of hatahata sushi, shottsuru hotpot, and a cod soup seasoned simply with miso and sake — but none says which sake to drink with them, and the prefecture's pages I read do not either. For the dishes themselves, see [Akita's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-akita/).

**Brewery tours.** The prefecture's sake guidebook (updated January 2023) lists 36 breweries: 11 say they offer no tours, 6 that tours are suspended or not offered, and 19 something else, of which 11 say booking is required. Each brewery takes applications its own way. The guidebook does not mention English. It is 2023 information, so check with the brewery.

**Open brewery days.** Most are in February, the cold-brewing season, with some in April or October. The cooperative's 47th open-brewery campaign gives 7–8 February 2026 as one example, and some breweries cap visitors at 100 a day by lottery.

**Getting there.** Check fares and journey times with the operator before you travel.

## Akita sake away from Akita

**The season stays at home.** Most open brewery days fall in February, while the sake is being made. A Tokyo bar cannot offer the brewery itself.

**The cooperative brings it to Tokyo.** Its *Akita no Sake wo Tanoshimu Kai in Tokyo* was held on 4 and 5 April 2026 at Ebisu Garden Place, with 28 sakes, by ticket. The ticket price was not stated as including tax.

**The Tokyo shop.** Akita Bisaikan in Takanawa sells Akita sake along with local foods; see [Akita's local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-akita/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Akita has the most breweries."** Fiscal 2023 tax statistics give Akita 38 licensed sites; Niigata 100, Hyōgo 88 and Hiroshima 56.
- **"Akita sake is made with Yamada Nishiki."** Some is. The prefecture bred Akita Sake Komachi to match it and grow in Akita, and that is now the most planted sake rice there.
- **"Akita sake is a GI."** It is not.
- **"Kiritanpo goes with warm sake."** Neither the farm ministry nor the prefecture says so, and I found no official source on a warm-sake custom in Akita.
