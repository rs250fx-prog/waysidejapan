Takoyaki and Akashi-yaki look like the same food: small round balls of batter with a piece of octopus inside, from two cities on the same stretch of coast. They are not the same. Takoyaki is finished with thick sauce, green laver and bonito flakes, and eaten off a boat-shaped tray with a toothpick. Akashi-yaki — which Akashi calls *tamagoyaki*, "egg-grill" — is mostly egg, soft enough to collapse, and dipped in warm dashi. Akashi also tells a story about how takoyaki got its octopus. Osaka does not tell it.

For the batter family as a whole, start from the konamono overview.

## Where takoyaki came from

**The official line.** The farm ministry says takoyaki is said to derive from *choboyaki*, a snack sold at sweet shops and festival stalls from the end of Meiji into Taishō, filled with konjac, dried shrimp or pickled radish in a soy-seasoned batter. When radio broadcasting began at the end of Taishō, *rajio-yaki* ("radio grill") appeared. In the early Shōwa period, stalls in Osaka began using octopus as the filling and selling it as takoyaki. Osaka's tourism bureau describes choboyaki as cooked on copper or iron plates with half-round hollows, with pickled ginger, konjac and spring onion in a flour-and-water batter, and says takoyaki took the idea, made the batter with dashi, added octopus and became something adults would eat. Takoyaki shops spread through the city only from the Shōwa 30s (1955–64): "the history is surprisingly short", in the bureau's words. It also notes the large octopus catches of Osaka Bay as a background.

**The shop's claim.** Aizuya, founded in Osaka in 1933, says on its own site that it created takoyaki two years later. Its founder was Endō Tomekichi. Its earlier rajio-yaki used konjac and beef instead of octopus, and the shop still serves both its original takoyaki — without sauce — and rajio-yaki. Neither the farm ministry nor the tourism bureau names Aizuya or any other shop; they say there are several theories.

**The sauce came later.** The farm ministry says it was the arrival of thick sauce after the war that fixed today's form: sauce, green laver and bonito flakes, in a boat-shaped tray with a toothpick. The tourism bureau says the sauce-mayonnaise-laver-bonito finish came from okonomiyaki.

## Where Akashi-yaki came from

**The name.** In Akashi it is *tamagoyaki*. The farm ministry says "Akashi-yaki" spread after city staff used the name to promote it around 1988.

**Two stories, at different levels.** The farm ministry, the city and the tourism association suggest it began with the egg yolks left over from making *Akashi-dama*, an artificial coral produced in Akashi from the end of Edo into Taishō, said to have been devised by an Edo tortoiseshell craftsman, Edoya Iwakichi — the yolks mixed with flour and local octopus and grilled. As a business, the city's records say it is said to have begun in 1919, when Mukai Seitarō of Taruya-machi sold it from a stall; eggs were a luxury, and pieces were sold a few at a time. The tourism association's page does not mention 1919. The city says there are several theories.

**How the dashi arrived.** A 2026 paper in the journal of the Japan Society of Cookery Science, read here in abstract, traces the change: Taishō-era recipes mixed the batter with kelp dashi and served no dipping broth; in the Shōwa era the egg increased, and from 1949 serving it with dashi became general. Today shops serve it with dashi, sometimes sauce, and differ in condiments, count and filling.

## The Akashi story about takoyaki

Akashi's tourism association relays a tradition: in early Shōwa Osaka people ate *rajio-yaki*, a flour batter with konjac and beef tendon. A customer at one shop remarked that in Akashi there was octopus inside; the shop tried it, it was a hit, and it spread across Japan as takoyaki. The city's records add that tradesmen came from Osaka to see how Akashi made it. Both are worded as things that are "said" or "handed down", and neither names a shop.

The Osaka sources used for this page — the farm ministry's takoyaki entry, Osaka's tourism bureau and Aizuya — do not mention the story. So: it is Akashi's account, not shared by Osaka's, and this site leaves it there.

## What makes each one

| | Takoyaki | Akashi-yaki (tamagoyaki) |
|---|---|---|
| Batter | Flour, dashi, egg | Flour, *jinko* (refined wheat starch), much more egg, dashi |
| Filling | Boiled octopus, tempura bits | Octopus |
| Pan | Takoyaki pan, about 220°C | Copper pan |
| Finish | Thick sauce brushed on, green laver, bonito flakes | None; dipped in dashi |
| Served on | A boat-shaped tray, with a toothpick | A wooden board (*age-ita*) |

The farm ministry's takoyaki method: heat the pan to about 220°C, pour in the batter, add octopus and tempura bits, and when the edges turn white after a minute or two, cut between the hollows and turn each ball 90 degrees at a time into a sphere, swapping positions so they cook evenly. *Jinko* is why Akashi-yaki stays soft: the ministry says it does not harden when heated.

## How to eat them

**Takoyaki.** In Osaka, the tourism bureau says, you buy it at a stall and eat it on the street, turned by a stallholder with a pick and served in a bamboo boat.

**Akashi-yaki.** Dip each piece in the dashi. One portion at Kimuraya is 20.

## Eating it at home, and getting there

- **Osaka.** Stalls and shops across the city; Aizuya serves its sauceless original.
- **Akashi.** About 70 tamagoyaki shops, by the city's and the tourism association's count, many around the Uonotana shopping street.

Fares and travel times are not on this page yet.

## Away from home

**The name changes.** Outside Akashi it is "Akashi-yaki", a name the city itself promoted from around 1988. In Akashi, ask for *tamagoyaki*.

**The chain price is a tax structure.** Gindaco's eat-in and takeaway prices differ by a few yen. I could not get comparable tax-included prices from the Osaka or Akashi originals, so I cannot say how a chain plate compares with one at the source.

**Takoyaki at home is an Osaka thing.** The tourism bureau repeats, as something "said, like an urban legend", that every Osaka household has a takoyaki pan.

## What people get wrong

- **"Akashi-yaki is takoyaki with dashi."** It is a different batter — much more egg, plus wheat starch — cooked in copper.
- **"Takoyaki is an old Osaka tradition."** Octopus arrived in the early Shōwa period, the sauce after the war, and the shops spread from the mid-1950s.
- **"Aizuya invented takoyaki."** That is the shop's claim. Official sources name no inventor.
- **"Takoyaki came from Akashi."** That is Akashi's story. Osaka's sources do not tell it.
- **"Takoyaki always has sauce."** Aizuya's original does not.
