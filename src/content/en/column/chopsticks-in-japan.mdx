The farm ministry makes a striking claim: many countries eat with chopsticks, but only Japan eats with chopsticks *alone*. Much of what visitors are told about Japanese table manners follows from that — why you lift your bowl, why soup is drunk from it, and why certain chopstick moves are frowned on. This column sets out what the official sources say, in their own terms. They describe some moves as "considered bad form". They do not claim a single correct way.

## Only chopsticks

The ministry says most of South-east Asia uses a spoon or ladle alongside chopsticks; its *washoku* guidebook adds that in Japan chopsticks and bowls are personal and no spoon is used.

**Why the spoon disappeared.** The ministry says spoons dropped out after the Nara period, and one reason was the bowl: a lacquered wooden bowl does not conduct heat, so you can hold it full of hot soup and drink straight from it. Personal bowls and chopsticks came with that change.

**When chopsticks arrived.** The sources describe different moments, and this column does not pick one:

- **First use.** The farm ministry's magazine estimates they came into use between the Yayoi and Asuka periods (3rd–7th centuries). Ogura Tomoko of the Japan Chopstick Culture Association, interviewed there, says early chopsticks were probably sacred implements rather than eating tools — a single bent bamboo piece like tweezers, perhaps to avoid touching offerings to the gods.
- **Spread.** The same magazine says that in the 7th century Japanese envoys to Sui China were entertained with chopsticks, and when a Sui embassy visited Japan the Japanese side ate with chopsticks in its manner, which is thought to have helped them spread.
- **Common use.** The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties notes many chopsticks found at the Heijō palace site and says their use is thought to have flourished in the Nara period.

## Kinds of chopsticks

- **By use.** *Shokubashi* for eating, usually personal and sized to the user; *toribashi* for serving from shared dishes; *saibashi* for cooking.
- **By shape.** Square, square with rounded edges, five- to eight-sided, shaved; in cedar, cypress, bamboo and more.
- **The ministry's eight examples.** Lacquered (everyday, personal); *genroku* (the common disposable kind, with chamfered head and a central groove); *kuromoji* (from the spicebush); *rikyū* (tapered at both ends, for *kaiseki*); green bamboo serving chopsticks; *tensoge* (mid- to high-grade disposables); willow (for celebrations); and *chōroku*, a plain disposable pair whose name is linked to a length of six *sun*, about 18 cm.

**Disposable chopsticks** (*waribashi*), the ministry's magazine says, began in the Meiji era as a way to use offcuts of cedar from barrel-making, and spread with restaurants. Today they are made from thinnings and offcuts, and are being reconsidered for environmental and economic reasons — while cheap imports have left the domestic industry in decline.

## The five moves called bad form

The farm ministry lists five uses of chopsticks considered bad form, known together as *kirai-bashi*:

- **Utsuri-bashi** — starting to take one dish, then moving to another.
- **Mayoi-bashi** — hovering over the dishes, undecided.
- **Sashi-bashi** (指し箸) — pointing at people or things with chopsticks.
- **Watashi-bashi** — resting chopsticks across a bowl during the meal.
- **Yose-bashi** — pulling a distant dish towards you with chopsticks.

**Two more, from the tourism bodies.** JNTO calls sticking chopsticks upright in rice and passing food from chopsticks to chopsticks the "biggest no-nos", and says chopsticks are not for skewering food. Tokyo's tourism site lists *hashi-watashi* (passing food), *sashi-bashi* (刺し箸, skewering) and *mayoi-bashi*.

**Watch the names.** The same sounds name different things. The ministry's *watashi-bashi* is resting chopsticks across a bowl; JNTO's concern is passing food between chopsticks, which Tokyo's site calls *hashi-watashi*. The ministry's *sashi-bashi* means pointing (指); skewering (刺) appears only in the tourism sources.

## Why

The official sources give three kinds of explanation, and only these:

1. **How Japanese meals are eaten.** The farm ministry lists the five as bad form against the background that a Japanese meal is properly eaten by always taking rice between mouthfuls of side dish or soup. It does not explain each move.
2. **Funerals.** A JNTO blog post says passing food between chopsticks recalls a ritual at funerals, and that chopsticks stuck upright in rice resemble rice offered at a memorial tablet. Tokyo's tourism site gives the funeral association too.
3. **Appearance.** Tokyo's tourism site adds that these moves simply do not look nice.

The ministry's magazine introduces them as moves that are "embarrassing in front of others". I found no survey of how many people observe them, and no official account of when each name arose.

## Practical points

- **Holding them.** The ministry's advice: hold about two-thirds of the way from the tips, like a pencil; keep the lower chopstick still and move only the upper. It says adults can still learn.
- **Resting them.** Use the chopstick rest during the meal and return them to it at the end, Tokyo's site says. When drinking only the broth, the ministry's magazine says you may leave them on the rest.
- **Shared dishes.** Use the serving chopsticks, which are separate from your own.
- **Sushi.** JNTO says chopsticks or hands are both fine; it is not a strict rule, and many Japanese people use both.
- **If you cannot use chopsticks.** A 2008 government manual advised businesses to prepare forks, knives and spoons on request, since not every foreign guest can use chopsticks. It is advice to businesses; whether a given restaurant does so is its own choice.

## What people get wrong

- **"There is one correct way, and everyone follows it."** The ministry lists moves considered bad form. It does not survey behaviour.
- **"Watashi-bashi means passing food."** In the ministry's list it means resting chopsticks across a bowl.
- **"Sushi must be eaten with chopsticks."** JNTO says hands are fine.
- **"Chopsticks came to Japan with the Nara court."** That is when they flourished; the ministry's magazine dates first use earlier.
