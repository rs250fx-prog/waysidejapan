Three markets appear on almost every first-time itinerary: Kuromon in Osaka, Nishiki in Kyoto, Omicho in Kanazawa. All three are real working markets with long histories. All three now have a second layer built on top of them — stalls that exist for visitors, priced accordingly.

This is not fraud. The prices are posted, nobody is deceived, and the stalls are within their rights to charge what the traffic will bear. The problem is narrower and more useful to know: **the gap between what those stalls charge and what the same dish costs a few streets away is large enough to change how you plan a day.**

## What has been reported

The figures below come from the posts listed at the end of this article, written by people who ate there. I have not verified them at the stall myself, and market prices move — treat them as the order of magnitude, not as a menu.

| Wagyu curry udon | <Price jpy={7000} /> | under <Price jpy={2000} /> for something comparable |
| Steak bowl | <Price jpy={6000} /> | under <Price jpy={3000} /> at an ordinary steak restaurant |

The wagyu curry udon is the one worth pausing on. It is not a dish with an established price, because it is not a dish most Japanese people order. A combination invented for the counter can be priced at whatever the counter likes, and there is no familiar figure for a visitor to compare it against. That is the mechanism, more than any single number.

### Nishiki Market, Kyoto

Nishiki has been covered in the Japanese press for the same reason — prices at the visitor-facing stalls have pulled away from the rest of the city. The market's older trade, selling ingredients to Kyoto kitchens, still operates alongside it.

### Omicho Market, Kanazawa

Seafood bowls reported at <Price jpy={4500} /> and <Price jpy={5500} />. Kanazawa genuinely does have excellent fish, and a good seafood bowl is not cheap anywhere. The question to ask is whether you are paying for the fish or for the address.

## Why the gap exists

Three things, none of them conspiratorial.

**A captive audience with no reference price.** A visitor eating wagyu for the first time has no idea what wagyu normally costs. Without a reference, any number is plausible.

**One visit, not fifty.** A neighbourhood restaurant survives on people who come back. A stall in front of a station or a famous market arcade does not need you to come back.

**Dishes that exist only there.** Where there is no standard version of a dish, there is no standard price.

## What to do instead

Not "avoid these markets" — they are worth walking through, and some of the old trade is still there. But:

- **Eat where the prices are boring.** A set meal at an ordinary restaurant one street back from the arcade will cost a fraction and, more often than not, be better.
- **Look for a posted price before you order**, especially for anything cut or grilled to order.
- **Be suspicious of a dish you have never heard of.** A famous ingredient in an unfamiliar format is the format with the widest pricing latitude.
- **Ask what the same thing costs at a department store food hall.** Same city, same ingredient, prices set for people who shop there every week.

## A note on these figures
