Between 2023 and 2026, the number of people visiting Japan passed its pre-pandemic peak, and a number of towns and sites introduced new rules: a gate and a fee on Mt Fuji, a screen beside a road in Fujikawaguchiko, guides at a level crossing in Kamakura, booking for coach parking in Shirakawa-gō. This column sets out the official numbers, the government's own wording, and what each place did and from when. It is the first of three columns on the subject; the other two will follow.

## What is happening: the numbers

**Visitor arrivals.** The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) counts legal entries by foreign nationals, excluding those who live in Japan permanently and adding certain short-stay landings. The count covers every purpose of visit, not only tourism. JNTO's annual figures, all marked as finalised:

- 2019: 31,882,049
- 2023: 25,066,350
- 2024: 36,870,148
- 2025: 42,683,301

The 2025 figure is about 1.34 times that of 2019 (this site's calculation). For 2026, JNTO's monthly table gives 27,626,300 for January to August, 2.7% fewer than the same months of 2025; the July and August figures in that total are estimates. The September 2026 figure is due on 21 October.

**Spending.** The Japan Tourism Agency's survey of inbound spending put the total for 2025 at ¥9.4549 trillion (finalised figure, published 31 March 2026), against ¥4.8135 trillion in 2019. Average spending per general visitor was ¥228,782. The survey includes business travellers and people visiting family, and excludes foreign residents of Japan. Accommodation was the largest share (36.6%), followed by shopping (27.0%) and food and drink (21.9%). For scale, the agency's 2026 white paper puts all travel spending in Japan in 2025 at ¥37.6 trillion, of which Japanese residents' domestic travel accounted for ¥26.8 trillion.

**Where visitors stay.** In 2025, 67.0% of overseas visitors' guest-nights were in the three largest metropolitan areas (preliminary figure, white paper). This concentration matters more to the rest of this column than the national total does.

## What the government calls it

The Japan Tourism Agency does not define "overtourism". Its documents use the word with "so-called" (いわゆる) in front of it, and describe the problem in terms of crowding and manners. The October 2023 policy package, adopted by the ministerial council on tourism, opens by saying that demand has recovered quickly but is concentrated in some areas, mainly cities, and that in some places and at some times there are concerns about "excessive crowding and breaches of manners" affecting residents' daily lives and lowering visitors' satisfaction. The measures are named "prevention and control", not a ban on anything.

The package has three pillars: dealing with crowding and breaches of manners, encouraging travel to regional areas, and tourism planned with local residents. Under the first came, among other things, support for about 20 pilot areas to draw up plans with residents, and a commitment to unified pictograms and guidance for travellers. That guidance appeared on 29 November 2024 as the agency's "Travel Etiquette for the Future", seven suggested behaviours including avoiding crowds, travelling light and keeping places clean, with a set of pictograms (28 by March 2026) such as "no entry to private land" and "watch your surroundings when taking photos".

**The 2030 target, and its condition.** The Basic Plan for tourism adopted by the Cabinet on 27 March 2026 keeps the target of 60 million visitors and ¥15 trillion in spending by 2030. The target is written with a condition attached: it is set on the premise that attracting visitors and securing residents' quality of life go together, and "after firmly working on the prevention and control of overtourism". The plan adds a new target of 100 areas, from 47 in 2025, that set up a forum including residents and make plans for issues such as excessive crowding and breaches of manners. It also says measures should be taken while being careful that they do not turn into a "negative message" towards the visitors who come.

## Not only overseas visitors

The official sources do not treat this as a question of who the visitors are.

- **Kyoto residents.** In the city's 2025 survey of residents (2,745 responses, published 24 December 2025), 70.6% said that crowding at some sightseeing spots applied to them as something they are "inconvenienced" by (迷惑している), 63.8% said the same of crowded buses and subways, and 56.3% of tourists' breaches of manners. The question says "tourists", with no reference to nationality, and gives its own examples: littering and eating while walking. The wording changed from the past tense in the previous year's survey, so the two years are not strictly comparable.
- **Visitors to Kyoto.** In the city's 2025 survey of visitors, both Japanese and overseas respondents who reported a disappointment most often wrote about crowds and too many people. Both groups also mentioned other visitors' manners and a lack of bins.
- **Kamakura.** The city's manners ordinance, in force since 1 April 2019, applies to "visitors and others", which the city explains as everyone who comes to or passes through Kamakura, not only tourists.
- **Kyoto's messages.** In February 2026 the city announced an etiquette column in an airline's in-flight magazine, described as content for Japanese tourists, asking people to avoid things done "without thinking", such as blocking the road for photos and walking several abreast.

## Where friction shows up: five patterns

The patterns below are this site's own grouping of what the local authorities' documents describe. They are not an official classification; no national one was found.

1. **Time.** Most of the pressure falls on particular days and hours. Kamakura lists long weekends and holiday daytimes; at the Kamakura-Kōkōmae crossing, waiting crowds peaked between 14.00 and 15.00; on Mt Fuji, the summit is crowded around sunrise.
2. **Private land and residents' streets.** Gion, the houses beyond the Kamakura crossing, the roadside in Fujikawaguchiko, the houses and fields of Shirakawa-gō. Several authorities give the same reason: tourist routes and residents' daily lives occupy the same narrow space.
3. **Rubbish, toilets and smoking areas.** Too few bins, or none, and facilities that are hard to find.
4. **Photography.** Not photography as such, but standing in the road, crossing outside crossings, or photographing people who have not agreed to it.
5. **Transport capacity.** Buses and roads that cannot simply add more services. Kyoto's transport bureau says a shortage of bus drivers limits how far it can increase services.

## What places have done

### Gion, Kyoto

**What happened.** In December 2023 residents of south Gion, the teahouse and geiko associations, Kennin-ji temple and the district council issued a joint message. It opens, in translation: "This is a place of daily life, not a theme park." It asks visitors not to stop, touch, follow or photograph geiko and maiko without permission, not to enter private property, not to wait on the pavement or stop in the road to take pictures, and not to leave rubbish. It also lists the existing laws that may apply, such as trespass and obstructing traffic.

**What was introduced, and when.** On 20 October 2025 the city and its tourism association announced etiquette staff on Hanami-kōji on weekdays from 19 November to 12 December, and said the district council would put up signs about photography and entry on private lanes around late November. Staff were placed again from 13 February 2026 "for the time being". Whether the signs are now in place is not confirmed. No official announcement was found of a general ban on private lanes from 2024, or of a fixed fine.

### Kamakura-Kōkōmae, Kamakura

**What happened.** The city says crowding and dumped rubbish around the level crossing near Enoden's Kamakura-Kōkōmae station grew worse despite work with the police and the railway. Kamakura's ordinance already names taking photographs in the roadway in a way that obstructs vehicles, and the city's commentary says this was written with this spot in mind.

**What was introduced, and when.**

- 13 to 16 September 2025: a trial photography area in the park next to the station, staffed by city employees.
- 1 October 2025 to 31 March 2026: a contracted team of five to seven guides each day, 10.00 to 18.00, using a national subsidy.
- 19 December 2025: an AI camera trial, with a live stream of the park in which faces are blurred.
- January 2026: signs at nearby crossings about not entering the houses beyond, and on 20 January a notice that the AI camera is recording.

The city's report says the share of people photographed who were standing in the road fell from 17.7% to 14.6% after the signs went up, and that the city is continuing the work in the 2026 financial year.

### Fujikawaguchiko

**What happened.** The town says that from around 2022 photographs of Mt Fuji above a convenience store spread through social media, and in 2024 the number of photographers rose sharply. It describes crowded narrow pavements, people stepping into the road and crossing between cars, and entry to private land, rubbish and unauthorised parking.

**What was introduced, and when.** Warning signs in four languages, cones and road markings from March 2023, and guards from June 2023. The town's safety works were completed on 21 May 2024: barriers between the pavement and the road, and a mesh screen about 2.5 metres high and 20 metres long. The store's operator, Lawson, added signs and guards of its own in May and June 2024. The town has not announced removing or replacing the screen.

### Mt Fuji

**What happened.** The package of measures adopted in March 2024 by the council for the proper use of Mt Fuji recorded about 221,000 climbers in summer 2023, heavy crowding on certain trails on certain days, and climbers attempting the summit overnight without staying at a hut (*dangan* climbing) or in light clothing.

**What was introduced, and when.**

- **2024.** Yamanashi Prefecture's ordinance (15 March 2024, in force 1 July) set up a gate at the fifth station of the Yoshida trail. From 1 July to 10 September, passage was closed from 16.00 to 3.00, numbers were capped at 4,000 a day (hut guests excepted), and a fee of ¥2,000 was charged.
- **2025.** The ordinance sets the fee at ¥4,000 per person per climb. Shizuoka Prefecture introduced rules for its three trails the same year.
- **2026.** On the Yoshida trail, 1 July to 10 September: ¥4,000 per person per climb, gate closed from 14.00 to 3.00 except for hut guests, a cap of 4,000 a day, an equipment check at the fifth station, and booking recommended but same-day booking possible. On the Shizuoka side, climbers starting between 14.00 and 3.00 must have a hut booking, the fee is ¥4,000 per climb, and registration is through the prefecture's app. The official notices give the amount as written and do not say whether tax is included.

Yamanashi's tourism-agency case study records that in 2024 there were no days above 4,000 climbers, against five in 2023. The rules for 2027 have not been announced; Shizuoka says details will follow once decided.

### Shirakawa-gō

**What happened.** The village's own case study names three issues: congestion on Route 156, the only road, with tailbacks of over two kilometres; smoking in a no-fire zone, rubbish and entry into houses and fields; and crowding inside the village. It writes that some visitors mistake the village for a theme park where they can go anywhere. Nearly 500 people still live in the gasshō-zukuri settlement.

**What was introduced, and when.**

- Since 1 April 2014, visitors are asked not to drive into the World Heritage area between 9.00 and 16.00 and to use the public car parks across the river.
- The village car parks open from 8.00 to 17.00, and the village does not receive sightseers at night, except those staying overnight.
- From 1 October 2025, parking charges rose: for a car, from ¥1,000 to ¥2,000 (the village's figures, without a note on tax). The village gave rising maintenance costs and overtourism measures as reasons.
- From 1 December 2026, the coach park at Seseragi Park requires advance booking, with no same-day bookings.

### Tokyo, briefly

- **Asakusa.** Taitō Ward's coach car parks in Asakusa must be booked at least seven days ahead. The ward asks tour operators to spread their arrival times, and says 10.00 to 14.00 is the hardest slot to book. In winter 2024–25 it handed out take-home rubbish bags and tried a numbered waiting system for queues outside shops.
- **Tsukiji outer market.** The market's guide asks visitors to eat inside the shop or at the designated spot, not while walking, and lists eight points including asking before taking photos and leaving large suitcases in lockers. It is a request, not an ordinance.
- **Shibuya.** The ward says night-time drinking in the street around Shibuya Station has been banned year-round by ordinance since October 2024.

## What this means for a visitor

**Plan around the peaks.** Most measures above are aimed at particular hours and days. Kyoto's tourism site publishes a comfort map with hour-by-hour forecasts for 11 popular spots; Shirakawa-gō has a congestion forecast and live camera; Taitō Ward publishes a coach congestion calendar. Among visitors in the agency's survey who found crowding a problem, 66% said they could not move around smoothly.

**Treat side lanes and fields as private.** Gion, Kamakura, Fujikawaguchiko and Shirakawa-gō all ask the same thing: do not go beyond the shopfronts and the public road. In Shirakawa-gō, that includes houses, fields and crops.

**Carry your rubbish.** Bins are scarce and some places, such as Shirakawa-gō, have none. Of visitors who found too few bins a problem in the agency's 2025–26 survey, 69% said they carried their rubbish away. The government's Basic Plan notes that what is asked differs by place: some areas want rubbish put in public bins, others want it taken home. For eating on the move and bins, see [eating while walking in Japan](/en/column/eating-while-walking-in-japan/).

**Take photographs the way places ask.** Use the designated area at Kamakura-Kōkōmae; do not stop in the road; cross only at crossings in Fujikawaguchiko; do not photograph geiko or maiko without permission in Gion; ask first at Tsukiji.

**Know the booking rules.** Mt Fuji's Yoshida trail can be booked in advance, and coach parking in Asakusa and, from December 2026, Shirakawa-gō must be. For restaurants, see [reservations in Japan](/en/column/restaurant-reservations-in-japan/), [queues and shared tables](/en/column/queues-and-shared-tables/) and [paying at restaurants](/en/column/paying-at-restaurants-in-japan/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Japan has banned tourists from Gion."** The official sources show requests, signs and staff on Hanami-kōji, and existing laws on trespass. No general ban or fixed fine was found.
- **"The rules are aimed at overseas visitors."** Kamakura's ordinance applies to everyone in the city, and Kyoto's surveys and messages cover Japanese travellers too.
- **"Mt Fuji's rules are fixed."** They changed in 2024, 2025 and 2026, and the 2027 rules have not been announced.
