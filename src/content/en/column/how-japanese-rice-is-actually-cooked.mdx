Most visitors notice it within a day: the rice served with an ordinary ¥900 set meal is better than the rice they cook at home. It is not a different grain, and it is usually not an expensive machine. It is a handful of steps that Japanese cooks do without thinking, and that nobody writes down because everybody is assumed to know.

Several of them are not actually common knowledge. Ask around and you will get contradictory answers, because most people learned by watching one household rather than being taught.

## The rinse is about friction, not dirt

Modern rice is not dirty. Rinsing removes surface starch, and the reason to do it is texture: leave the starch on and the grains glue together, so the mouthful arrives as a lump rather than as separate grains.

The first water goes in and comes straight back out. Rice absorbs fastest in the first seconds, and that first water is the cloudiest — leave it sitting and the grain drinks it back in.

## The soak is the step most often skipped

Dry rice needs time to take on water before heat is applied. Skip it and the outside cooks before the centre has softened, which produces grains that are firm in a way people mistake for undercooking.

Thirty minutes is the usual advice in summer, longer in winter when the water is cold. Rice cookers sold in Japan build this waiting time into their programme, which is why the same machine takes far longer than a pot on the stove.

## The ten minutes after it is "done" are part of the cooking

When the switch clicks, the rice is not finished. It needs to stand with the lid on while the moisture redistributes from the bottom to the top. Opening it immediately releases the steam that was about to do the work.

Then the rice is turned over — cut through it and fold, rather than stir — to let the excess steam escape and to keep the bottom layer from compacting. This is the step that separates rice that holds its shape from rice that turns to paste.

## Why it is worth knowing before you travel

Because it explains what you are eating. When rice arrives at a counter in a small town and it is noticeably better than what you had in the city, it is usually not the variety or the price. It is that someone measured the water, waited, and turned it over.

It also tells you something about the places in this guide. A set meal at a station stand and a room at a ryokan are separated by a factor of ten in price, and the rice can be the same standard at both.
