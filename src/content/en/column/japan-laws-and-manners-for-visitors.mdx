faq:
  - q: Is it against the law to bathe in a Japanese onsen with a tattoo?
    a: No law was found that covers it. The current texts of the Public Bath Act, the Hotel Business Act, the Hot Springs Act and their regulations do not mention tattoos. Whether you can bathe is each facility's decision, so ask before you go.
  - q: Do I have to carry my passport in Japan?
    a: Yes. The Immigration Control Act requires foreign nationals in Japan to carry their passport at all times and to show it when a police officer or immigration officer asks. The penalty is a fine of up to 100,000 yen. Children under 16 are exempt from carrying it.
  - q: Is smoking in the street illegal in Japan?
    a: It depends on the local ordinance. In Chiyoda Ward in Tokyo the whole ward is a no-smoking zone for the street, and the ward currently collects 2,000 yen on the spot for cigarettes and heated tobacco. Kyoto collects 1,000 yen in its designated areas. Look for local signs.
  - q: Can I bring CBD products or cold medicine into Japan?
    a: Check before you pack. Since December 2024, CBD products whose THC residue exceeds the health ministry's limits count as narcotics. The customs guide lists some inhalers and decongestants among prohibited stimulants, and medicines above a set share of codeine or pseudoephedrine need permission in advance.
  - q: Do police figures on foreign nationals describe tourists?
    a: No. The police category excludes only permanent and special permanent residents and a few others, so it includes students, trainees and workers. The short-stay figures include people who stayed past their permitted period, and immigration offences that apply only to foreign nationals.

Most of what visitors are asked to do in Japan is a request: walk to one side, keep your voice down, take your rubbish with you. A smaller set of things is fixed by law, with a penalty. And a third set belongs to the owner of a place: a temple's photography rules, a museum's ban on tripods, a bathhouse's policy on tattoos. This column separates the three, using the laws as they stood on 15 October 2026 and what each place's managers say, explains how to read the crime statistics often quoted about foreign nationals, and lists who to call. It is the third of three columns; the first covers [crowds and local rules](/en/column/crowds-and-local-rules-in-japan/), the second [new travel rules and charges](/en/column/new-travel-rules-and-charges-in-japan/).

This is a summary of published texts, not legal advice. Whether a particular act breaks a particular law depends on the facts.

## Three kinds of rule

**Law.** A statute or local ordinance that forbids something and attaches a penalty: imprisonment or a fine; detention or a petty fine, the lightest criminal penalties, used in the Minor Offences Act; or an administrative fine (過料, *karyō*), which ordinances use for things like street smoking.

**A duty to try.** Some ordinances say people "must endeavour" to do or not do something, with no penalty. Kamakura's manners ordinance is written this way. The city's own commentary says it was not made to ban or regulate, and it separates the behaviour it asks for from acts that another law already forbids, which it calls things you must not do.

**A place's own rules.** Shrines, temples, museums, inns and bathhouses set rules for their own grounds. These rules carry no penalty of their own. But entering an area a manager has closed can engage the trespass provisions below, depending on the facts.

The government draws a similar line. The Basic Plan for tourism, adopted by the Cabinet in March 2026, distinguishes "illegal acts" such as entering private land without permission and littering from "breaches of manners" such as taking photographs on public roads, though it does not define where the line falls.

**Drones.** The transport ministry's English page says drones of 100 g or more must be registered and display a registration mark with a Remote ID function. Its registration FAQ says this applies to aircraft brought in from abroad, even if registered in another country. Permission is needed around airports, at 150 m or more, and over densely inhabited districts, among others. Tokyo's construction bureau says flying drones is prohibited in metropolitan parks, and the Metropolitan Police Department's English page for visitors says flying drones is prohibited in most areas of Tokyo. The ministry runs an English drone helpdesk on +81-3-5539-0225, weekdays 9.00 to 17.00.

**White taxis and unlicensed lodging.** The penalties in the Road Transport Act and the Hotel Business Act fall on the person running the service. Among the articles this site read, none is addressed to the passenger or the guest. The transport ministry says licensed taxis have green number plates and private cars have white ones. The Japan Tourism Agency calls lodging run without a licence, notification or special-zone approval "illegal minpaku", and says such places are more likely to have problems with hygiene, emergency response and fire safety. It advises booking through registered sites. Part 2 covers [licensed ride-share](/en/column/new-travel-rules-and-charges-in-japan/).

**Street smoking.** This is set locally. Chiyoda Ward's ordinance dates from October 2002, and the ward says the whole ward is now a no-smoking zone for the street. The ward currently collects ¥2,000, paid on the spot in cash or by payment slip. It covers cigarettes and heated tobacco (heated tobacco since 1 November 2024), not liquid e-cigarettes. Kyoto collects ¥1,000 in designated areas with heavy foot traffic. Shibuya Ward's English notice says drinking in public spaces around Shibuya Station is banned from 18.00 to 5.00 daily, from 1 October 2024.

**Damage to cultural property.** The Cultural Properties Act's fine applies to properties that have been formally designated. The current text sets it at up to ¥1 million; some explanations online give a lower figure.

**Photography.** The provisions above target secret photography of sexual parts, underwear and people in changing rooms or baths, and, in the Minor Offences Act, blocking someone's way or following them. In the laws this site read, no provision punishes photographing people in public as such; that reading was not exhaustive. JNTO's etiquette page asks visitors not to photograph people without permission, including people in traditional dress.

**Drugs and medicines.** Cannabis is legally a narcotic. The health ministry says that from 12 December 2024 using it became an offence, and that CBD products whose THC residue exceeds set limits count as narcotics. The customs guide for travellers lists "stimulants (including Vicks inhalers and Sudafed)" among prohibited goods. Prescription medicine for personal use, up to one month's supply, needs no procedure unless it is banned or specially controlled, according to the health ministry's Q&A. The narcotics control department's list excludes medicines with 1% or less of codeine and 10% or less of ephedrine or pseudoephedrine; above that, permission is needed, applied for at least 14 days before travel.

**Food.** The animal quarantine service says meat products, including jerky, ham, sausage and meat buns, need an inspection certificate from the exporting country, whether for yourself, as gifts or bought at an airport. The plant protection service says most fruit and vegetables and all soil are prohibited.

**Knives.** The law allows folding knives and scissors with a blade of 8 cm or less as an exception. The Tokyo police say good reason means a socially accepted reason, such as taking a newly bought knife home; carrying one for self-defence is not. A shorter blade carried concealed without reason can fall under the Minor Offences Act. Customs says swords and guns need a permit to bring in.

**Passport.** The duty applies to foreign nationals in Japan, who must also show it when an immigration or police officer asks. Children under 16 do not have to carry it.

## Tattoos are not a legal matter

No law was found that governs tattoos in baths. This site searched the full current texts of the Public Bath Act, the Hotel Business Act, the Hot Springs Act and the regulations under the first two for the Japanese words for tattoo, and found none. Prefectural bylaws on public baths were not checked. The Hotel Business Act lists when an inn may refuse a stay, but that concerns lodging, not bathing.

Whether you can bathe with a tattoo is therefore each facility's decision. The Japan Tourism Agency's ryokan Q&A says inns vary widely: some have dropped restrictions, some judge small tattoos case by case, and some offer private baths. JNTO says some traditional onsen do not admit people with tattoos, suggests checking the policy in advance or looking for private baths (*kashikiri-buro*), and says cover stickers suit only very small tattoos. More in the tattoos section of [the water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Places' own rules, as they state them

These differ from place to place; follow what is posted on site.

- **Shrines.** The Association of Shinto Shrines gives the basic form of worship as two bows, two claps and one bow. Some shrines use their own form, and visitors should follow it there. On not walking in the centre of the approach, it says there is no strict rule, but asks visitors to respect each shrine's rules and other worshippers. JNTO notes that you clap at shrines but not at temples.
- **Kiyomizu-dera.** The temple's notice of June 2024 says drones, monopods and tripods cannot be used, group photographs need notice and an application, commercial filming and making works in the grounds without permission are declined, and areas such as the halls and planted beds are closed.
- **Kinkaku-ji.** The temple's FAQ says it allows snapshots with small cameras for personal enjoyment only, and declines photography for publication, including on social media, commercial photography, group photographs, live video streaming and drones.
- **Tōdai-ji.** The temple's English page says photography is not allowed inside its halls other than the Great Buddha Hall.
- **Tokyo National Museum.** The museum says works marked "No photography" cannot be photographed, the second floor of the Heisei-kan is excluded, and flash, extra lighting, tripods, monopods and selfie sticks are not allowed.

Rubbish rules also differ by place. The Basic Plan notes that some areas ask visitors to use public bins and others to take rubbish home. For food on the move, see [eating while walking](/en/column/eating-while-walking-in-japan/).

## How to read the crime statistics

Figures on "foreign crime" are often quoted in discussions of tourism, but the official sources count something else. What follows comes from the documents' own definitions.

**Who is counted.** The National Police Agency publishes figures for *rainichi gaikokujin*, literally "foreign nationals who have come to Japan", in its annual report on organised crime. Its definition: foreign nationals present in Japan, excluding so-called settled residents (permanent residents, spouses of permanent residents and special permanent residents), members of the US forces in Japan and people whose residence status is unknown. The category therefore includes technical intern trainees, students, long-term residents, people on work visas and others living in Japan. It is not a count of visitors, and the definition does not use the word.

**What "short-term stay" covers.** Short-term stay is a residence status. The Immigration Control Act describes it as sightseeing, recreation, sports, visiting relatives, inspection tours, attending courses or meetings, business liaison and similar activities during a short stay. It is not limited to tourism. The police report notes that its short-stay figures add together people in Japan legally and people staying illegally, such as those who stayed beyond their permitted period. On 1 January 2026 the Immigration Services Agency estimated 68,488 people overstaying in Japan, of whom 41,607 had held short-term stay status.

**What is counted.** The figure is *kenkyo jin'in*, usually translated "persons cleared": people the police dealt with for an offence in the year. For offences under laws other than the Penal Code, the report says the count means persons referred to prosecutors. These include Immigration Control Act offences, such as overstaying and not carrying a passport or residence card, which by their nature apply only to foreign nationals. In 2025, Immigration Control Act offences made up 61.5% of the persons counted under laws other than the Penal Code.

**The figures, with these definitions attached.** Persons cleared in the *rainichi gaikokujin* category, all offences:

- 2023: 11,534
- 2024: 12,170
- 2025: 12,777 (report published April 2026)

Of these, people with short-term stay status, legal and illegal stay combined: 2,122 in 2023, 2,214 in 2024 and 2,166 in 2025.

**Why not divide by arrivals.** JNTO counted 42,683,301 visitor arrivals in 2025; the Immigration Services Agency counted 39,184,525 new entries by foreign nationals, 38,458,105 of them on short-term stay. Each counts a different thing, and the agencies publish no rate per visitor; dividing one figure by another does not produce one. For these reasons the figures above cannot describe how tourists behave. This site does not reproduce the breakdowns by nationality.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tattoos are banned in Japanese baths."** No law was found that says so. It is each facility's policy.
- **"Manners are enforced with fines."** Most are requests or duties to try. The fines come from specific laws and local ordinances, such as Chiyoda Ward's street-smoking rule.
- **"Police figures on foreign nationals show how tourists behave."** The category includes residents on most statuses, the short-stay figures include overstays, and part of the count is immigration offences that only foreign nationals can commit.
