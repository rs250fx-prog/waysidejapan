Visitors often arrive in Japan with a list of sushi rules: chopsticks only, soy sauce never on the rice, ginger at the right moment, one bite or else. The official sources say much less than that list, and what they do say is mostly advice rather than rule. This column sets out how sushi is ordered and eaten in the places most people in Japan eat it — the counter, the conveyor-belt chain and the take-away box — and, where the sources give one, the reason behind each habit.

The history of sushi, its regional forms and what it costs are covered on the [sushi overview](/en/eat/sushi/), the [Edo-style sushi page](/en/eat/sushi-edomae/) and the [conveyor-belt sushi page](/en/eat/sushi-kaiten/), so this column touches on them only where they explain how people eat.

## Three different meals

"Going for sushi" in Japan can mean quite different things:

- **A counter shop**, where a chef behind the counter makes each piece in front of you.
- **A conveyor-belt shop**, where plates travel past the seats and, increasingly, you order on a screen.
- **Pressed or boxed sushi**, made ahead and eaten from a box: Osaka's box sushi, Toyama's trout sushi, Nara's persimmon-leaf sushi.

Each has its own way of ordering. Eating a piece is much the same in all three.

## At the counter

**Why there is a counter at all.** The farm ministry's account of Edo-style nigiri is that it began as street food sold from stalls, and that when the stalls became shops they kept the counter and the chef kept making the sushi in front of the customer. The rice portion has changed: the ministry says the Edo rice ball was about three times the size of today's. The counter is a stall that moved indoors.

**Ordering.** At lunch, the simplest order is a set. Yoshino-zushi Honten in Nihonbashi, open since 1879, lists lunch nigiri sets at a few fixed prices, while in the evening it gives a budget rather than a fixed price (its prices are on the [Edo-mae page](/en/eat/sushi-edomae/)) — the kind of evening where the chef chooses, often called *omakase*. Official sources checked for this page do not describe how an *omakase* meal is ordered or paced, so this column does not set out steps for it.

**Market price.** Some sushi menus give no price for certain items, only *jika*, market price. Official sources do not explain what a shop must tell you about such items. The health ministry's paper on the sushi trade notes that the price of the fish a sushi shop buys swings sharply, which is the background to pricing by the day. The finance ministry's rule that displayed prices must include tax applies when a price is shown; it does not oblige a shop to show one. The practical step is the one in the [column on reading menu prices](/en/column/reading-menu-prices-in-japan/): ask the price before you order.

## Eating a piece

This part applies wherever you eat sushi.

### Hands or chopsticks

JNTO's manners page says sushi may be eaten with chopsticks or with your hands, that this is not a strict rule, and that many Japanese people use both. GO TOKYO's sushi guide does not address the question either way. No official source checked for this page says one or the other is required.

If you use your hands, the damp towel you are given when you sit down is for wiping them; a JNTO blog post says you can put it back on its tray afterwards and use it again. The [column on Japanese table customs](/en/column/japanese-table-customs/) covers the towel in more detail.

If you use chopsticks, the farm ministry's list of five moves considered bad form applies as it does at any meal — among them hovering over the dishes while deciding, and drawing a dish towards you with your chopsticks. The [column on chopsticks](/en/column/chopsticks-in-japan/) has the full list and the reasons the sources give.

### Soy sauce

GO TOKYO advises a little soy sauce, on the topping. That is the only official guidance found for this page, and it gives no reason. Other official sources checked for this page do not mention dipping the rice side at all, so the advice is GO TOKYO's alone rather than a rule several bodies agree on.

### One bite

GO TOKYO also advises eating each piece in one bite. Again, it gives no reason, and no other official source checked for this page addresses it.

### Scent

The same guide asks diners to go easy on perfume, and gives no reason either.

### Pickled ginger

Official sources do not describe when or how pickled ginger (*gari*) should be eaten. It is served with sushi; any rule about using it is not one this column can source.

### Wasabi is flavour, not protection

The health ministry's guidance on *anisakis*, the parasite behind 330 of Japan's 1,037 recorded food-poisoning cases in 2024, is explicit: vinegar, salt, soy sauce and wasabi do not kill it. Freezing at −20 °C for at least 24 hours, or heating to 70 °C, does. Whatever you put on a piece is a matter of taste. Food safety is the shop's handling of the fish.

## At a conveyor-belt shop

The belt was invented in Higashi-Ōsaka in 1958; its history is on the [conveyor-belt sushi page](/en/eat/sushi-kaiten/). What matters for eating there is that the belt is no longer the main way sushi reaches the seat.

**Ordering on a screen.** Sushiro's "Digiro" shops are fully to-order: you choose from a screen and the sushi is made for you. The company counted 123 such shops by its 2025 financial year. In the industry's 2026 consumer survey by Umios, about three-quarters of respondents said they order pieces to be made rather than relying on what passes on the belt.

**Taking from the belt.** Kura Sushi still runs plates on the belt, and manages how long a plate may stay on it with a time-limit system it introduced in 1997; since 2011 it has also used a device it calls *Sendo-kun* — *sendo* means freshness. The point of both is that a plate taken from the belt should not have been going round for long.

**The conveyor sign that is a counter.** Kanazawa's tourism association notes that many shops there carry the conveyor-belt sign but work on orders: you ask, and the sushi is made for you. The [Kanazawa sushi page](/en/eat/sushi-kanazawa/) describes how that works.

**What people choose.** The same 2026 survey puts salmon first among conveyor-belt toppings for the fifteenth year running, with an average of 9.9 plates per visit. Salmon is not a Tokyo Bay fish; the farm ministry notes that today's nigiri uses toppings such as salmon from well beyond the bay that gave Edo-style sushi its name.

The prices, the plate system and the chains' city and suburban tiers are on the conveyor-belt sushi page.

## Boxed and pressed sushi

Not all sushi is eaten at a counter or a belt. In Osaka, the farm ministry says, Yoshino-zushi in the merchants' quarter of Senba devised a box sushi in the Meiji era that the merchants took as a box lunch for the theatre interval. Toyama's trout sushi, by the ministry's account, spread nationwide as a station lunch box in the Taishō era. These are eaten from the box, cut into portions; the [Osaka box sushi](/en/eat/sushi-osaka-hako/) and [Toyama trout sushi](/en/eat/sushi-masu/) pages describe them.

## What Japanese diners actually do

The sources describe habits rather than rules, and they describe a wide range of them:

- **At conveyor-belt shops, most order rather than take from the belt**, by the Umios survey.
- **Both hands and chopsticks are used**, by JNTO's account.
- **Where the menu says market price, people ask.** The [column on reading menu prices](/en/column/reading-menu-prices-in-japan/) covers why.
- **The city that spends most on sushi eaten out is Kanazawa**, not Tokyo, by the government household survey; the [sushi overview](/en/eat/sushi/) has the figures.

No survey of how many people dip fish-side down, eat in one bite or use their hands was found for this page, so this column gives no figures for them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sushi must be eaten with chopsticks."** JNTO says hands are fine, and many Japanese people use both.
- **"There is an official sushi etiquette."** The advice found for this page comes from tourism guides and is short: a little soy sauce on the topping, one bite, go easy on perfume.
- **"Wasabi kills parasites."** The health ministry says it does not.
- **"Market price is a trick."** It reflects a trade where the price of fish moves sharply. It is a reason to ask, not to avoid the shop.
- **"Conveyor-belt sushi means taking plates off a belt."** At many shops you now order on a screen, and in Kanazawa the conveyor sign often means a counter where you order from the chef.
