Walk into many ramen shops in Japan and the first thing you meet is not a person but a machine: a panel of buttons by the door, each with a dish name and a price. You pay the machine, take a paper ticket, hand it to the cook, and sit. Nobody takes your order at the table and nobody brings a bill. It is simple once you know the sequence, and the reasons for it are mostly about the kitchen.

## What happens

Japan's national tourism organisation sets out the steps in its ramen guide:

1. **Pay first.** Put money in the machine.
2. **Choose.** Press the buttons for your ramen, drinks and any sides.
3. **Take your change and the printed tickets.**
4. **Hand the tickets to staff**, then sit and wait.

Tokyo's official tourism site describes the same: at ramen shops and other casual places there may be a machine near the entrance; buy your ticket first, hand it to staff, then sit where you are shown. Many shops have multilingual menus. Exactly when you hand the ticket over — before you are seated, or once you are — differs from shop to shop.

Not every shop uses one. Both sources note that some take your order from staff in the usual way.

## What the machine accepts

This is where visitors get stuck, and the honest answer is that it depends on the machine.

- **Cash.** JNTO's guide says only "put money in". The product page for one major maker's model, Glory's VT-T21, lists recycling units for ¥10,000, ¥5,000 and ¥1,000 notes.
- **Cards and phones.** The same page lists electronic money, such as transport IC cards, and QR-code reading as *options*, with the usable brands to be confirmed. So one shop's machine may take Suica and the next shop's only cash.
- **Language.** The same model can display English, Korean, and simplified and traditional Chinese. That is one model's specification; I could not find how many shops' machines do.

In the Japan Tourism Agency's latest survey of foreign visitors (4,110 responses, winter 2025–26), 10.7% named using credit or debit cards as a problem during their trip, and 10.9% too few or unclear multilingual signs. Those figures cover the whole trip, not ticket machines in particular.

## Why shops use them

The clearest explanation comes from a maker, and should be read as a maker's case. Glory's 2025 article for restaurant owners argues that with the customer operating a machine for ordering and payment, staff can concentrate on cooking and serving — which helps with labour shortages; orders entered by customers themselves mean fewer mistakes; simple operation helps turnover at busy times; and no cash changes hands between staff and customers, which it presents as more hygienic. It is a sales argument, not an independent study.

**An early example.** Omron's company history records that in 1963 it delivered a meal-ticket vending machine to a department store — a multi-function ticket machine at the Daimaru store in Kyoto. I have not found a reliable source for which machine was Japan's first, so I do not call it that. I could not find statistics on how many restaurants use ticket machines.

## How Japanese customers use them

Buy at the entrance before sitting, hand over the ticket, and wait. That is all the official sources describe. If a machine has no English, ask staff.

## What people get wrong

- **"You order at the table and pay later."** At a ticket-machine shop, you pay the machine first.
- **"Every machine takes cards."** Card and IC payment are options on the machine; some machines may be cash only.
- **"Every machine has English."** Some models do; not all shops' machines.
- **"Every ramen shop has one."** Some take orders in person.
