Japan’s tourism agency has written a guidebook for restaurants and hotels on welcoming Muslim visitors, and the most striking thing in it is a caution about a single word. It asks businesses not to use “halal” lightly, and to leave the judgement of what is halal to the traveller. This column sets out what that guidebook, the farm ministry and the national tourism organisation, JNTO, say about pork, alcohol, meat and prayer, in their own terms. It does not say which Japanese dishes are halal. None of the official sources I read gives such a list, and they are clear that the answer belongs to each person and each restaurant.

## The guidebook and what visitors said

The Japan Tourism Agency’s *Muslim Hospitality Guidebook* comes in a basic-knowledge volume, a practical volume and two appendices; the agency’s page shows it as updated on 22 March 2024, and notes that a collection of case studies was added in March 2018. The statistics inside are 2017 estimates. I could not tell from the text when the first edition came out.

The guidebook lists inconveniences reported by Muslim travellers and travel companies:

- Menus only in Japanese.
- English menus that give only the name of a dish, which is not enough to judge whether it can be eaten.
- No indication of non-pork or non-alcohol dishes.
- Few places to pray at airports, stations, shopping malls and the like.
- Not enough information about places that cater for Muslims.

## What halal means, in the sources’ words

The farm ministry describes halal as what is permitted under Islamic law. The tourism agency’s guidebook explains the idea of *halal* (permitted acts and things) and *haram* (forbidden acts and things), and names pork and alcoholic drinks as the typical haram foods.

It is equally clear about the limits. The guidebook says interpretation and practice differ by school, country, region, culture and individual, and that there is no worldwide standard for how businesses should cater for travellers. The practical volume tells businesses to leave the judgement of what is halal to Muslim travellers and not to use the word “halal” carelessly.

## Pork, beyond the meat

The guidebook says the concern is not only pork itself but ingredients derived from pigs. Its examples:

- Bacon and sausages.
- Pork-bone soup and lard.
- Emulsifiers, shortening, gelatin and collagen.

It notes that Western-style sweets may contain pig-derived emulsifiers and the like, and tells businesses to check ingredient labels or ask the manufacturer.

## Alcohol, including mirin

- **Mirin.** The guidebook says many Muslims do not consume alcohol, mirin included. Mirin is classed as a liquor in Japan (a “mixed liquor”).
- **Soy sauce.** It is not a liquor, the guidebook says, but some people are concerned about alcohol added to it. Whether to avoid the small amounts of alcohol in seasonings such as soy sauce is a matter of debate and differs between individuals.
- **What non-alcohol means.** For a business, the guidebook says, offering non-alcohol dishes means not using sake, beer and the like, and also not using cooking sake or mirin.
- **Substitutes.** As case examples, it mentions restaurants that use sugar, honey or apple juice in place of mirin.

JNTO’s English page adds that cooking sake, mirin and soy sauce are basic to Japanese seasonings such as noodle sauce.

## Meat, fish and vegetables

The guidebook says animal foods other than pork have rules too, and that meat other than meat slaughtered in the Islamic way (halal meat) is to be avoided. The practical volume tells businesses that meat from Brazil or Australia is often halal meat, while still advising them to check for a halal label. It says unprocessed raw fish and vegetables are considered halal ingredients, but that some Muslims avoid prawns, shellfish and the like.

## What restaurants are told to do

The practical volume sets out stages:

1. **Non-pork.**
2. **Non-alcohol.**
3. **Dishes made with halal ingredients.**

On the kitchen, it says some Muslims want dedicated cooking utensils, separate cooking and storage areas, separate tableware and cutlery (some prefer disposable), and a separate place to eat.

**Sample wording.** The guidebook gives English sentences for menus and signs, including:

- “Non-pork dishes are available.”
- “The non-pork dishes do not contain any pork or pork-related ingredients.”
- “The non-alcohol dishes do not contain or use any cooking sake (alcohol) or sweet cooking rice wine.”

Menus can use text or pictograms, though it notes that some Muslims find a picture of a pig unpleasant. Even a business that does not cater specifically for Muslims, it says, can help by writing dish names and ingredients in English.

**The word itself.** Calling something “halal food” or a “halal restaurant” may be taken to mean strict handling all the way through cooking and storage, the guidebook warns. It advises saying accurately that the *ingredients* are halal: “a dish made with halal ingredients”.

## Examples from the guidebook

- **Ramen at a tourist facility.** Six of nine ramen shops offered ramen with a plant-based soup and no animal-derived toppings, and the ticket machines had English and pictograms.
- **A hotel.** It marked dedicated utensils with green tape, and at the buffet showed about 20 pictograms for ingredients, alcohol among them.

## Prayer

The basic volume says Muslims pray five times a day. For a restaurant without a permanent prayer room, the practical volume suggests offering a corner or an empty private room for the time being, letting guests use a washbasin such as the one in the toilets for washing, showing the direction of Mecca (the qibla), giving prayer times, and providing a substitute mat, such as a towel or plastic sheet without pictures of people or animals. It expects airports, theme parks, large shopping centres and hotels to have dedicated prayer rooms.

JNTO’s English page says prayer rooms and mosques are found mostly in big cities, and that dedicated spaces are increasing at major airports, tourist sites and Muslim-friendly hotels. I could not find an official list of prayer spaces.

## Muslim-friendly, and certification

The farm ministry held an exhibition of halal food in its consumer room from 12 to 23 May 2025. There it described *Muslim-friendly* as efforts to accommodate Muslims as far as possible, regardless of whether halal certification has been obtained.

JNTO’s English page says Japan has no central halal certification body; certified products are mainly frozen meat, spices and single ingredients, and are rarely found in ordinary supermarkets. It says a place calling itself halal-friendly does not necessarily have a dedicated kitchen. JNTO’s vegetarian guide mentions restaurants showing certificates issued by the Japan Muslim Association or a halal business association. There is more than one certifier, not a single system. I could not find how many restaurants in Japan are certified.

**Finding food.** JNTO points to restaurant guides for Muslims, including its own *Japan Welcome Guide for Muslim Travelers*, to hotel concierges, and to cooking for yourself in accommodation with a kitchen.

**Other religious diets.** I found almost nothing in official sources on kosher or Hindu dietary needs in Japan, and so this column does not cover them.

## What people get wrong

- **“Halal-friendly means a halal kitchen.”** JNTO says it does not necessarily.
- **“No pork on the menu means no pork.”** The guidebook lists lard, gelatin, emulsifiers and more as pig-derived.
- **“Japanese food is alcohol-free if you skip the sake.”** Mirin and cooking sake are seasonings, and the guidebook counts them.
- **“There is one rule for all Muslims.”** The guidebook says practice differs by school, country, region, culture and person.
