Ikaho lies at the northern foot of Futatsudake, the newest lava dome of Mount Haruna, in Shibukawa city, Gunma. It is a town of stone steps climbing a hillside, with two different waters: one that turns brown in the air and one that stays clear. This page explains how it works using Shibukawa city, the Shibukawa Ikaho tourism association, Gunma prefecture, the Japan Meteorological Agency and JNTO. Where they tell things differently, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn or bath.

Ikaho is not one of the environment ministry’s *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the designated national health resort onsen; the ministry lists three plans for Gunma, for Shima, Kazawa and Minakami town. So there is no ministry plan with figures for Ikaho. For what the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). Ikaho’s neighbours in this series are [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/), [Shima](/en/soak/shima/) and [Minakami](/en/soak/minakami/).

## Where it came from

### The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Mount Haruna as a large composite volcano about 20 km across at its base, with Futatsudake its newest lava dome. It says Ikaho Onsen lies on Futatsudake’s northern foot and that no steaming is seen near the summit. Activity resumed in the 5th century, with three eruptions by the middle of the 6th, among them the Futatsudake–Ikaho eruption of 525–550; the agency takes these dates from the national geological survey’s database.

The city says Ikaho’s water came out through the volcanic activity of Futatsudake. The tourism association’s chronology puts it in the Kofun period, before 600, "with the explosion of Futatsudake". Gunma prefecture’s English guide says the spring is thought to have formed before 600 through an eruption of Futatsudake.

The weather agency’s page does not link the eruptions to the spring; it says only where the spring lies. So I give the eruption story as the city’s and the association’s explanation. I found no official source on where the iron in the water comes from.

### The founding stories

The city says the spring is "said to have been found" about 1,900 years ago, in the reign of Emperor Suinin, the eleventh emperor. The tourism association gives two stories, discovery under Suinin and discovery by the monk Gyōki, and says a book from the Nanboku-chō period already describes the spring. I do not choose between them.

The city says Ikaho appears in 9 of the 25 *Azuma-uta*, the eastern songs, of the *Man’yōshū* anthology. The association’s chronology dates the anthology, with a question mark, to 700–780; Gunma prefecture’s English guide says around 759. I do not settle it.

### The name

The association offers an Ainu word, *ikahop*, "boiling water", or *ikazuchi*, thunder, with *ho*, fire. The city lists the Ainu *ikabop*, *yukawa*, "hot-water river", and *iikao*, "a good face", meaning a fine view, and says there are various theories. I do not choose.

### The stone steps

The official pages agree on the 1570s but tell the story in different ways.

- **The tourism association’s chronology.** In 1576 (Tenshō 4), after the battle of Nagashino, Takeda Katsuyori ordered Sanada Masayuki to build a place for soldiers to recover. In 1590 the town moved down from the source to where it is now.
- **Another association page.** In 1576, the Kogure family and six others moved their settlement from the source to the present site and built the steps. Water was piped from the source, run down the middle under the steps and divided left and right to the plots on either side. The association calls this Japan’s first planned onsen resort town.
- **The city.** The steps are "said to have been formed" around 1576, "420 years ago". The city’s area page says "about 440 years ago" and calls it a town laid out on Japan’s first onsen town plan. The two counts do not match, and neither page explains why.
- **Gunma prefecture and JNTO.** Both describe a place of recovery for warriors, the prefecture after Nagashino in 1576, JNTO built by Sanada Masayuki in the late 16th century.

The steps you climb today are newer. The association says they were rebuilt in granite over five years from 1980, and extended in 2010 to 365, "so that the town would be lively all 365 days of the year". The city calls them one of Japan’s three great stairways, with Yamadera and Konpira, without saying who chose the three.

### Edo and Meiji

The city says writers such as Takizawa Bakin and Jippensha Ikku came in the Edo period. In Meiji, the city and the association say, an imperial estate was set up at Ikaho in 1890, and the town became the setting of Tokutomi Roka’s novel *Hototogisu*. The association also says Erwin Bälz, the German physician, gave Japan’s first medical guidance on bathing here in the early Meiji years; I found nothing else on that.

## The water

### Golden and silver

Shibukawa city gives two waters, with these official names:

- **Kogane no Yu**, "golden water": a calcium-sodium sulphate, hydrogen-carbonate and chloride spring, neutral, hypotonic.
- **Shirogane no Yu**, "silver water": a neutral, hypotonic cold mineral spring that counts as an onsen under Article 2 of the Hot Springs Act because its metasilicic acid is above the legal limit.

The tourism association explains the colours. The golden water is colourless when it comes out and turns brown in the air, because it contains iron. Originally Ikaho had only the golden water. The silver water was "confirmed in recent years" (I found no year), flows at 110 litres a minute, and is colourless, tasteless and odourless.

So the iron explains the golden water’s colour but is not in its name. An iron spring needs 20 mg or more of iron per kilogram, as the [page on iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/) explains; I found no official iron figure for Ikaho, so I do not call it one. The silver water is an onsen in law because of metasilicic acid, not heat; the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/) explains that rule, and sulphate springs are covered on the [page on sulphate and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/).

Gunma prefecture’s list of springs by type (31 March 2026) appears to place Ikaho under hydrogen-carbonate springs, classing places with several waters by their main component; I read that from a PDF’s text order, so treat it with care.

### How much

The association says the No. 2 source outlet, one of the outlets of the golden water, is the only place where you can watch about 1,400 litres a minute rise inside a domed building. It gives no year. I found no official total for the number of sources, the total flow or the temperature.

## How the town works

### The steps as plumbing

The association’s page on the steps describes how the water runs through them. *Koma-guchi*, outlets that branch the water flowing down from the source, are spread along the steps, four in all. There is a *yutaki*, a stretch where spring water flows over the steps. Signs of the twelve zodiac animals beside the steps mark where twelve leading inns stood more than 200 years ago, and a poem by Yosano Akiko is carved into the stone. Shops, *manjū* makers, game halls and a footbath line both sides. JNTO says the steps climb about 300 m to Ikaho Shrine, with free footbaths along the way.

### Kajika bridge and the source

At the far end of the town, the city describes Kajika bridge as a vermilion arched bridge next to the source outlet and Ikaho Rotenburo. It says the leaves start to turn in late October and peak in early November.

### Ikaho Rotenburo

The city calls Ikaho Rotenburo the open-air bath at the source of the golden water, with no showers. The association says it uses the golden water *kakenagashi*, straight from the source, and has no washing area. As checked on 12 October 2026, the association gives:

- **Hours.** 9:00–18:00 from April to September, 10:00–18:00 from October to March, last entry 17:30.
- **Closed.** The first and third Thursdays, open on public holidays and every day in August.

The city adds that children under one may not bathe. Both pages give prices, but neither says whether they include tax, so I do not give them.

### Ishidan no Yu

The city runs Ishidan no Yu. As checked on 12 October 2026, its page gives 10:00 to 20:00, last entry 19:30, closed on the second and fourth Tuesdays (the next day if that is a holiday), with these hours in place since 1 April 2024. It lists 13 and 27 October and 10 and 24 November as closing days. The page gives fees for several groups but does not say whether they include tax, so I do not give them.

## Bathing there

- **The city’s advice.** Start with three to ten minutes, do not rinse off afterwards unless you have sensitive skin, and take a doctor’s guidance for any cure. The city and the association list conditions the water is said to help; this page does not repeat them.
- **Scars.** Shibukawa city is encouraging inns and day baths to accept *omoiyari nyūyokugi*, bathing wear that covers scars from breast cancer surgery or skin grafts, and is listing places that do. This is for scars, not tattoos.
- **Tattoos.** The tourism association’s English pages have Stay, Sightseeing, Onsen, Eat, Souvenir and Access, and I found nothing on tattoos. Gunma prefecture’s general English guide says many onsen do not allow tattoos and that a small one may be fine covered with a plaster, but to check first. Ask each inn or bath in advance. The general picture is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Ikaho source that compares its water with baths in the cities.

## What it is eaten with

**Mizusawa udon.** The farm ministry’s page on Gunma’s udon says Mizusawa udon of Shibukawa is "said to be" one of Japan’s three great udon, with Inaniwa and Sanuki, glossy and firm, eaten by dipping. It does not mention Ikaho or the spa. The tourism association’s food page lists a shop on the stone steps described as a sister of one in the Mizusawa udon street, and another with an address in Ikaho-machi Mizusawa. Mizusawa was one of three villages that merged in 1889 to form Ikaho town, the association and the city say. Gunma prefecture’s English guide suggests the udon shops by Mizusawa Kannon temple. So you can eat Mizusawa udon at Ikaho, but I do not call it the spa’s own dish. The wider picture is in [Kantō’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/).

**Yu-no-hana manjū.** The association says the glossy brown *onsen manjū* began in Ikaho, and became known across Japan after being chosen as a gift to the imperial household. The prefecture’s English guide says the town "claims" it began there, and JNTO says it originated in Ikaho. I found no third-party record of the origin, and other spa towns sell *onsen manjū* too, so I give it as Ikaho’s own account. The farm ministry’s list of Gunma dishes does not include it.

## Getting there

As checked on 12 October 2026. Fares and timetables are not on this page; the city and the association link only to timetable PDFs.

- **Bus from Shibukawa.** The city gives 30 minutes by Kan-etsu Kōtsū bus from Shibukawa station on the JR Jōetsu and Agatsuma lines. The prefecture’s access guide (updated March 2024) gives about 25 minutes, to the Ishidangai-guchi stop; JNTO says about 30.
- **Train from Tokyo.** The prefecture’s Ikaho page (updated April 2026) gives 1 hour 40 minutes from Ueno to Shibukawa by the limited express Kusatsu, or the shinkansen to Takasaki in 50 minutes and the local line to Shibukawa in 25. JNTO gives an hour to Takasaki and 30 minutes to Shibukawa.
- **Highway bus.** The association says the Jōshū Yumeguri and Tokyo Yumeguri buses link Ueno, Shinjuku and Tokyo station with Ikaho, and that the Ikaho–Shima Onsen bus from Haneda, Tokyo station and Kawagoe is currently suspended. The city’s page still describes a Haneda service, so check with the operator. The prefecture gives about 2.5 hours from Shinjuku and about 3 from Tokyo station; JNTO a little over 2 from Shinjuku.
- **Car.** About 10 km from Shibukawa-Ikaho interchange on the Kan-etsu expressway, says the city.

The association says "as little as two hours from Tokyo". Taken together, the sources put it at about two to three hours.

## Ikaho beside Kusatsu

Kusatsu is built around the Yubatake, an open source in the middle of town. At Ikaho the source lies at the far end of the town, by Kajika bridge and the open-air bath, and the water is branched down the steps. The official pages compare them no further.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ikaho is an iron spring."** The golden water turns brown because of iron, but its official name is a sulphate, hydrogen-carbonate and chloride spring, and I found no iron figure.
- **"The silver water is just a weaker golden water."** It is a different water, an onsen in law because of metasilicic acid.
- **"The volcano made the spring, officially."** The city and association say so; the weather agency says only that the spring lies at the foot of Futatsudake.
- **"The 365 steps are 400 years old."** The town dates to the 1570s, but the steps were rebuilt from 1980 and reached 365 in 2010.
- **"Ikaho is a national health resort onsen."** It is not on the environment ministry’s list.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
