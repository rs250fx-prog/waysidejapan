Jozankei Onsen is in Minami ward, at the southern edge of Sapporo city, along the gorge of the Toyohira river. Its tourism association puts it about 26 km south of Sapporo and about an hour by car, and describes its water as a colourless sodium chloride spring with a mild saltiness. The town tells its own story through a mountain monk who found the spring in 1866, and through the kappa, the river creatures of Japanese folklore, whose statues stand around the streets. This page sets out what the association, JNTO and the bus operators say about the water, the history, the free footbaths and getting there. Where sources disagree I give each. It does not rate any inn or bath.

For how Hokkaido’s onsen towns compare, see the [Hokkaido overview](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/). For what the parts of a spring’s name mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/) and the page on [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/).

## Where it came from

### The monk Jōzan

The association says the town began in Keiō 2, 1866, when Miizumi Jōzan, a *shugen* (mountain ascetic) monk, came upon the source with the help of Ainu guides. The place was named Jozankei, "Jōzan’s valley", for his work. The association’s history page tells it in the hearsay form, as something that is said to have happened, and gives no other version. It does not give an Ainu meaning for the name; it treats the name as coming from the man.

The association’s English top page counts 160 years since the first bath opened, in 1866. JNTO, on a national park page drawn from environment ministry material, calls Jozankei a hot spring town dating back to the mid-19th century.

Beyond "with the help of Ainu guides", I could not find any official account of the Ainu and the spring.

### After the monk

- **1918.** A railway opened between Jozankei and Shiroishi. The association’s photo history starts here, saying the town grew with Sapporo’s rapid growth after the war.
- **1996.** On the 130th anniversary of the opening, the town made a "health resort declaration", says the association.

### The kappa pool

The association tells a story about a deep pool in the Toyohira river. Until the dam of the Chōshi-no-kuchi power station was built in Meiji 42 (1909), the river ran strongly there. A young man working on the road was fishing in the pool when he sank, as if pulled under. On the night of the first anniversary of his death he appeared to his father in a dream and said he was living happily with his kappa wife and child. Since then, says the association, the place has been called the Kappa Pool.

The association’s page of kappa spots lists 15 of them by name, among them a kappa king and a playing kappa, but tells no story there. Neither page says when or where the legend comes from, and I could not find out.

## The water

### What the association says

The association’s page about the town calls the water "colourless and transparent, with a mild saltiness", a sodium chloride spring that is neutral, hypotonic and high-temperature. Its separate page on spring types names it a sodium-chloride spring and adds the old label *jaku-shokuen-sen*, weak salt spring. JNTO also describes Jozankei’s water as rich in sodium chloride.

Neither of the association’s pages gives a pH. "Neutral" is the association’s word; I have no figure behind it.

### How much

The association’s spring-type page gives 56 sources, a flow of 8,600 litres a minute and a temperature at the source of 60 to 80°C. It does not say who measured them or when, and does not say what area they cover, though they read as figures for the whole town. I read this page through a summarising tool, so the numbers are worth checking against the page itself.

### Other springs nearby

The same table lists three springs nearby that are not Jozankei’s own water:

- **Hōheikyō Onsen**: sodium-calcium-hydrogen carbonate-chloride spring (the old label is *jūsō-sen*, bicarbonate of soda spring).
- **Koganeyu Onsen**: simple sulphur spring.
- **Usubetsu Onsen**: calcium-sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring (old label, bicarbonate of soda and earthy bicarbonate spring).

For what those names mean, see [hydrogen carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) and [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/).

### Where the heat comes from

The association’s Japanese page describes Shikotsu-Tōya National Park, which includes Jozankei, as known for landforms made by volcanic activity, such as hot springs and vents of sulphurous gas, and calls the mountains around the area (Muineyama, Soranumadake, Sapporodake) volcanic. It does not say that Jozankei’s water comes from a volcano. Its English top page gives a different kind of explanation: water from melting snow and the mountain forests soaks into the ground and is warmed by the earth’s heat. I read that page through a summarising tool and give it as the association’s account.

So I do not tie Jozankei’s water to any volcano. The association does not, and neither does anything else I read.

The association also lists what the water is said to be good for. I do not repeat that here.

## How the town works

The association lists free places to dip your feet or hands:

- **Futami footbath.** A semi-outdoor footbath looking over the Toyohira river.
- **Jozan Gensen Park.** A footbath park that, the association says, recreates the scene of Jōzan finding the spring. Free, opened in November 2003. There is a pot of hot water in the park where you can boil eggs.
- **Kappa family wishing hand bath.** A hand bath fed from a source.

The English list marks these as free. The association’s FAQ gives opening hours of 7:00 to 21:00 for Jozan Gensen Park and 7:00 to 20:00 for the Ashi no Fureai Tarō no Yu footbath, all year. The FAQ still carries a note about social distancing and has no update date, so check before you plan around the hours.

## Bathing there

**Day bathing.** The association’s Japanese and English lists give adult prices for day bathing at each facility, some including a meal. Neither says whether tax is included, so I do not give any figures.

**Tattoos and manners.** I found nothing on tattoos or bathing manners on the association’s Japanese or English pages, including its day-bathing, FAQ, about and access pages. On the English FAQ I could read only the headings, not the answers, and the association points readers to each facility’s own website. Ask before you go; the general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

**Compared with city baths.** I found no official Jozankei source that compares its water with baths in the city. What the association does say is that the town is about an hour by car from central Sapporo. That is a fact about distance, not about the water.

## Getting there

Checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

**By car.** The association gives about 50 minutes from Sapporo, 60 from Otaru, 70 from Niseko and 80 from New Chitose Airport.

**By public transport.** The association lists the rapid Airport train from New Chitose Airport to Sapporo (about 40 minutes), the Namboku subway line to Makomanai (about 18 minutes), and buses: Dōnan Bus’s Sapporo-Tōyako line, Jōtetsu Bus’s Kappa Liner and other Jōtetsu services, Hokuto Kōtsū, and taxis that must be booked. The times it gives range from about 35 to 90 minutes, and from what I read I could not tell which time belongs to which route and starting point, so I give none of them. The association warns that connections from Makomanai station to the local bus may not work because of operating hours.

JNTO gives 1 hour 15 minutes from the Sapporo station bus terminal to Jozankei Onsen on Jōtetsu buses 7 and 8. The difference from the association’s times is probably a matter of route and starting point, but neither page says so.

**In winter, direct from the airport.** The Jozankei Liner is a winter-only bus from New Chitose Airport. Its official site says:

- It runs every day from 19 December 2026 to 21 February 2027.
- It takes about 100 to 120 minutes, with no change.
- It is reservation only, booked by 18:00 the day before. It seats 30, and it skips stops where no one has booked.
- It is organised by Co-op Travel and run with Jōtetsu Bus, Hokuto Kōtsū and Dream Kankō Bus.
- Cancellations and long delays are announced on its official X account on the morning of the day.
- It runs as a trial funded by Hokkaido’s accommodation tax.

For the other towns in this series, see [Toyako Onsen](/en/soak/toyako/), [Sounkyo Onsen](/en/soak/sounkyo/) and [Noboribetsu Onsen](/en/soak/noboribetsu/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Jozankei’s water is volcanic."** The association calls the surrounding mountains volcanic, but does not tie the water to a volcano. Its English page says the water is snowmelt and forest water warmed by the earth’s heat.
- **"It is officially Sapporo’s *okuzashiki*."** I did not find that phrase, roughly "Sapporo’s inner parlour", on the association’s Japanese or English pages. They say "about an hour by car from Sapporo".
- **"The pH is about 7."** The association calls the water neutral but gives no figure.
- **"The kappa legend is ancient."** The association tells it as a story from before a dam built in 1909, and does not say when or where it was first told.
- **"There are two founding stories."** There is one on the association’s pages: Jōzan, guided by Ainu people, in 1866.
- **"There is no bus from the airport."** In winter there is the Jozankei Liner, booked in advance.
- **"The water treats a condition."** The association lists indications. This page makes no health claims.
