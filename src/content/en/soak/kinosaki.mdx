Kinosaki Onsen, in Toyooka city in northern Hyogo, is built around its *sotoyu*: public bathhouses you walk between, usually in a cotton *yukata* and wooden *geta*. Its tourism association describes the whole town as one large inn — the station is the entrance, the streets are the corridors, the inns are the guest rooms and the public baths are the big baths. This page covers how the water reaches those baths, what the town says about its age, which baths are open in 2026, and what the official English guides say about tattoos and bathing. The ten official spring types and how to read the analysis sign are in the separate guide to onsen water types, and what free-flowing water means is in the *kakenagashi* piece; the Honshu overview places Kinosaki among the other Kansai springs.

## Where it came from

The tourism association says the spring opened more than 1,300 years ago, in the Nara period, that the monk Dōchi came in 717, and that 2020 marked 1,300 years. It adds that the town has appeared in poetry since the Heian period and was loved by many writers, Shiga Naoya among them. Visit Kinosaki, an English guide, says Dōchi founded Onsen-ji temple, and JNTO says it was once customary to pray at the temple before bathing.

The bath trust that supplies the water (the Kinosaki-chō Yushima *zaisanku*, a public property trust) tells a different set of stories on its English page, one for each public bath:

- **Kou no Yu** — a stork was seen healing a wound in the water and so revealed the spring, about 1,400 years ago.
- **Ichi no Yu** — about 300 years ago a doctor rated it the best in Japan.
- **Mandara Yu** — it welled up after the monk who founded Onsen-ji prayed for 1,000 days.
- **Yanagi Yu** — said to spring from under a willow transplanted from West Lake in China.
- **Jizō Yu** — a sacred image is said to have been found at the source.
- **Gosho no Yu** — an emperor’s sister is said to have bathed here about 600 years ago.

The stork story’s "about 1,400 years" and the association’s 717 do not match. Both are traditions, and this site does not choose between them.

## The water

The association’s public bath page gives one spring type for the public baths: sodium-calcium chloride, high temperature (*natorium-karushiumu enkabutsu kōonsen*), at 42°C. That is the page’s figure; I could not tell whether it is the source or the bath. I could not find an official count of sources or a flow rate from the association, Toyooka city or the bath trust. The trust’s site has headings for the water’s components, indications and contraindications, and for its central supply system, but I could not read the text under them. I also could not find a public source explaining why the water rises here.

## How the town works

### One supply

The bath trust says the spring water goes to a limited set of places: the seven public baths, the baths of some inns, five footbaths and three drinking fountains set up by the trust and Toyooka city. Since December 2023 it has been handing out a certificate, the *Kinosaki Onsen Natural Hot Spring Supply Certificate*, to places that use the natural water. Toyooka city’s hot spring management office lists the sources, the supply and the seven public baths as its responsibilities. I could not get technical detail on how the central supply works.

### Sotoyu and uchiyu

The association distinguishes *uchiyu*, the bath inside your inn, from *sotoyu*, the shared public baths, and says walking the *sotoyu* is what Kinosaki is known for. From Jizō Yu to Kou no Yu is about 15 minutes on foot, by the association’s figure. Walking in *yukata* and *geta* is the custom, according to both the association and JNTO, and JNTO says inns lend *yukata*; there are also rental shops.

### Which baths are open

Toyooka city lists seven public baths: Ichi no Yu, Jizō Yu, Gosho no Yu, Yanagi Yu, Mandara Yu, Kou no Yu and Sato no Yu. As of 10 October 2026, the official pages say:

- **Sato no Yu** has been closed long-term since 31 March 2024 (one Toyooka city notice gives 1 April).
- **Kou no Yu** is closed for long-life renovation. The bath trust gives 28 October 2026 for reopening; Visit Kinosaki gives the closure as 11 May to 30 October 2026. The two do not agree, and I give both.

So five or six baths are open, depending on the date. The association’s public bath page lists six, while the town still speaks of "seven public baths".

## Bathing there

The official pages give an adult and a child price for a single bath and for a one-day pass (called *Yumepa* on the association’s page). None of them says whether the price includes tax, so I do not give the figures. The official English guides say guests at the town’s inns can use the public baths free, with tickets given out by the inn. The association says you must enter by 30 minutes before closing. Opening days and hours differ from one official page to another, so I have left them out; check the bath trust or the association before you go.

### How to bathe, according to the town’s guides

Visit Kinosaki’s bathing guide sets out the steps: use a locker, wash first, tie up long hair, keep your towel out of the water, and dry off before going back to the changing room. It lists what is not allowed: swimsuits or underwear, photography, swimming, eating or drinking, shaving and brushing your teeth. The bath trust’s own manners page adds pouring water over yourself before getting in (*kakeyu*), washing before entering, keeping towels out of the bath, not bathing after drinking alcohol, and not using any device with a camera in the changing room or bath. I could not find an official recommendation on how long to stay in each bath.

### Tattoos

Visit Kinosaki says all seven public baths accept tattoos, and that you do not need to cover them, whatever their size. It adds that this applies only to the public baths: inn baths may have different rules, so check with your inn in advance. JNTO also calls the seven public baths tattoo-friendly, without the condition. The bath trust’s site has nothing on tattoos and directs questions to the city office, and I could not find anything on tattoos on the association’s Japanese pages. I could not confirm who runs Visit Kinosaki from the site itself; the bath trust links to it as the English site for the public baths. The general picture across Japan, including private baths as an option elsewhere, is in the water types guide.

## Food

The association’s winter page says the snow crab (*matsuba-gani*) season opens on 6 November, and that its "Crab Kingdom" promotion runs from then to the end of March, with crab offers at fish shops, restaurants and souvenir shops. The page does not give a year. JNTO also names *matsuba-gani* for winter, and Tajima beef. The farm ministry lists boiled *matsuba-gani* — the San’in name for snow crab — among Hyogo’s local dishes, but the entry I could see does not name Kinosaki, so I say only that both sides feature the crab.

## Getting there

From the association’s access page, which carries no update date (checked 10 October 2026):

- **Osaka** — about 2 hours 40 minutes by the limited express Kounotori.
- **Kyoto** — about 2 hours 20 minutes by the limited express Kinosaki.
- **By air** — Osaka Itami to Kounotori Tajima Airport, about 35 minutes, then about 40 minutes by bus to Kinosaki Onsen station.
- **Highway bus** — about 3 hours 10 minutes from Osaka Umeda, about 3 hours 40 minutes from the Karasuma exit of Kyoto station.
- **By car** — about 2 hours 30 minutes from Osaka, about 3 hours from Kyoto.

JNTO gives about 2.5 hours by direct limited express from Kyoto or Osaka on one page, and about 3 hours from Shin-Osaka by Kounotori on another. Visit Kinosaki notes that the JR limited expresses from Osaka and Kyoto need seat reservations, and that there is a direct Zentan bus from Osaka. I could not find official directions from Kansai Airport. Fares are not on this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Seven baths means seven open."** In 2026 one is closed long-term and another is closed for renovation.
- **"The one-day pass is the only way in."** Inn guests get tickets from their inn, according to the official English guides.
- **"Tattoo-friendly means everywhere in town."** Visit Kinosaki limits it to the public baths and says inn baths may differ.
- **"Kinosaki is 1,300 years old, full stop."** That is the association’s count from 717; the stork legend of Kou no Yu is put at about 1,400 years.
- **"Any bath in the area is Kinosaki water."** The bath trust says the water goes only to the public baths, some inns, the footbaths and drinking fountains, and it now issues a certificate to places that use it.
