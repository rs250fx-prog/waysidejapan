Yugawara is a spa town in Kanagawa, along the Fujiki and Chitose rivers, about five minutes by train from [Atami](/en/soak/atami/) and covered by the same geopark as [Hakone](/en/soak/hakone/). Its most unusual feature is how the water is shared out: the town buys hot water from the owners of private sources and pipes it on to users, a scheme it says was born "in a form found nowhere else". It also claims a poem in the *Man’yōshū*, the old poetry anthology. This page uses the town, the prefecture’s institute, the Hakone Geopark, the tourism association and JNTO. Where they differ, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

The tourism site at yugawara.or.jp is run by a council that includes the tourism association, the town and the inn cooperative; I call it the tourism association’s site. Its English site, at yugawara-resort.com, has no update date, and its information page carries the note "As of April 2021". I treat what it says as dating from about then.

For spring types and temperature bands, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for the notices about added water, heating and recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Where it came from

### The Man’yōshū poem

The sources say different things about one poem:

- **The town** says Man’yō Park has a stone carved with the only poem in the *Man’yōshū* that sings of a hot spring.
- **The Hakone Geopark** says there are several stories of how the spring began, but that a poem in the *Man’yōshū* beginning *ashikari no tohi no kafuchi ni izuru yu no* "is said to" be about this spring.
- **The English site** says that among the anthology’s 4,500 poems, Yugawara’s is the only one about a hot spring named by name.
- **JNTO**, undated, says Yugawara appears in the *Man’yōshū*.

That the poem refers to Yugawara is what is told. I do not decide how the poem should be read.

### Medieval and Edo

The geopark says the spring was called *Kogome-yu* or *Kokomi-yu* in the Middle Ages, and that by the late Edo period there were three sources. The English site says Yugawara appeared in the Edo-period rankings of hot springs, and that it was designated a place of recuperation in the Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars, without giving years. I do not repeat its ranking.

### Yoritomo and the Doi clan

The town introduces the local Doi family, Sanehira and his son Tōhira, who followed Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the Kamakura shogunate, when he rose in arms, and the *Shitodo no Iwaya*, the cave where Yoritomo is said to have hidden after losing the battle of Ishibashiyama. The geopark tells the same story. I found nothing that ties Yoritomo directly to the hot springs.

### The railway and the writers

The geopark says Yugawara grew fast after the Tanna tunnel opened in 1934 and the Tōkaidō line passed through. It also names Kōfūsō, the only site of the 26 February incident of 1936 outside Tokyo; the town lists Kōfūsō too.

The town’s page on sights names these writers and artists:

- **Kunikida Doppo** came three times late in life to rest at the former Nakanishi inn, and wrote *Yugawara yori* and *Yugawara yuki*. There is a literary monument to him in Man’yō Park.
- **Shimazaki Tōson** relaxed at the Itōya inn while writing *Yoake mae*. A poem stone stands there.
- **Natsume Sōseki**’s last novel, *Meian*, moves to Yugawara from its 170th instalment, with the Amanoya inn where he stayed and the Fudō falls.
- **Yosano Akiko** often visited the former Shinjusō in Yoshihama in the early Shōwa period and wrote many poems about Yugawara.

The town gives no years for Tōson’s or Sōseki’s visits.

### The volcano

The Hakone Geopark, whose office is in Hakone town hall, says Yugawara Onsen is a gift of the **Yugawara volcano**, part of the Hakone volcanic area, and that the water rises in the volcano’s eroded caldera. Its older page says the Yugawara volcano, one of the outer rims of the Hakone volcano, was worn into a wide valley, and that the spa town grew along the Fujiki and Chitose rivers on the valley floor. I found no official source that explains how the heat becomes hot water.

The Izu Peninsula Geopark names a Yugawara volcano too, as one of three old volcanoes behind the springs of Atami and Itō. The two geoparks are separate bodies, and I am only noting that the name appears in both.

## The water

### Spring type

The town’s page on the water (updated 23 August 2022) gives it as a **sodium-calcium chloride-sulphate spring**, under the old naming a gypsum-bearing salt spring. It calls it weakly alkaline, hypotonic and high-temperature, with water arriving in the supply at 71.1°C on average in fiscal 2019. The figures are an extract from an analysis issued by Kanagawa’s hot springs research institute.

The town’s booklet on the town-run supply (November 2025) gives the same spring type for the "Yugawara Onsen mixed spring", from four sampling points: Oku-Yugawara, Gongenyama, Miyakami and Dohi. The water is **58.2 to 61.7°C**, **pH 8.10 to 8.30**, with 1,545 to 2,055 mg/kg of dissolved matter in all, analysed on 25 November 2019. Both the page and the booklet list what the water is said to help; I do not repeat that. What the long name means is in the guide to [chloride springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/).

### How many sources

Two official counts, years apart, counted differently:

| | Kanagawa hot springs research institute (end of March 2017) | Town booklet (fiscal 2024) |
|---|---|---|
| Sources | 111 (69 in use, 42 not) | 157 (75 in use, 33 not, 49 suspended) |
| Flow | 6,459 L/min | 5,618 L/min pumped |
| Notes | Excludes dry and suspended sources | 25 sources, 1,790 L/min, feed the town-run supply |

The two counts differ in year and in what they include, so I do not say the water has fallen.

### The town buys the water

The booklet explains the town-run supply. The owners keep their sources. Each pumps its water into the town’s main pipe; the town values it where it enters, using 65°C as the standard, buys it and distributes it to users. The scheme began in 1956, and in fiscal 1957 the town secured 1,850 litres a minute from 13 sources and began supplying 190 users. As of April 2025 the town itself owned 11 sources. In fiscal 2024 the water feeding the supply averaged 70.3°C over the year, and it made up 31.86% of all pumped water in the town.

### Saving the springs

The booklet says that in the 1955–1964 decade, over-drilling and over-pumping made water levels fall fast and running dry became a serious problem. As a lasting answer the town built a centralised supply system and began central management in fiscal 1994. It says water levels at six sources in the town began to rise around fiscal 1996 and have been stable since fiscal 1998, adding that these are spot surveys and that the overall trend needs more study. The Hakone Geopark says the springs faced drying up from over-drilling in the early Shōwa period, which is earlier, and that they are now strictly protected and centrally managed by the town. The two put the crisis at different times; I do not choose.

## How the town works

### Man’yō Park and the Sōyu

Man’yō Park is a green park along the Chitose river, in the middle of the spa town. The town lists more than 80 kinds of plants from the *Man’yōshū*, the thatched teahouse Man’yōtei, the poem stone and the monument to Doppo; the town-run Kogome no Yu’s site says the teahouse was designed by Horiguchi Sutemi. The English site says the park is one of Japan’s "100 best historic parks", without saying who chose it.

The town’s page on the park’s facilities (updated 18 June 2024) says the ground floor of the entrance terrace has a café and tourist information, with a footbath outside, and the first floor offices of the tourism association and the inn cooperative. It says money from Kanagawa prefecture’s horse-racing proceeds has been used to develop the park. The town also says the Doppo no Yu, which was a footbath, closed and its site became the Sōyu Terrace.

The tourism association says the **entrance terrace is free**, apart from the café, and that the footbath is beside the café.

### Sōyu Terrace

The Sōyu Terrace is a day-bathing facility in the park, run by a designated operator. It is the only one of the three towns on this page where I found an official bath price stated with tax. The tourism association’s page gives:

- **¥3,500 including tax and bathing tax**, for a stay of three hours, without a meal.
- **¥6,800 including tax and bathing tax**, for five hours, with a meal.

It is for junior high school age and above, and booking is required in principle. The same page says it closed from 14 October 2025 and reopened on 18 September 2026. Checked on 12 October 2026.

### Kogome no Yu

The town runs a day bath, **Kogome no Yu**. The tourism association calls its water a weak salt spring and gives hours of 9:00 to 21:00, last entry 20:30, closed on Mondays (or the next day when Monday is a holiday). Its fee table does not say whether tax is included, so I do not give a price.

I could not find a full official list of shared baths and footbaths in Yugawara. The tourism association has a town walking map from October 2025, which I did not open.

## Bathing there

The tourism association’s English site has the only official English bathing guide among the three towns on this page. In summary:

- Wash at the washing area first, then pour water over yourself, then get in slowly.
- After bathing, do not shower again.
- Do not bathe after drinking alcohol.
- Do not add cold water, even if the bath is hot.
- Keep your towel out of the water, and tie up long hair.
- Swimsuits are, as a rule, not allowed.
- Drink water after bathing.

**Tattoos.** The English site says that at many hotels and inns, people with tattoos cannot use the baths because of a cultural prejudice, and tells you to check with your inn in advance. The town’s booklet gives general advice in Japanese but says nothing about tattoos. Remember the English site’s note of April 2021; ask each inn or bath. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

**Compared with city baths.** I found no official Yugawara source that compares its water with day spas or bathhouses in the cities. What the town does describe is how the water reaches users: bought from owners at the point it enters the town’s pipe and distributed under central management.

## What it is eaten with

**Mikan.** The town’s page on mikan picking (updated 24 August 2026) says the secret of Yugawara’s mikan is sunny slopes and the warm sea. It gives the 2026 season as 10 October to 17 December, with an information point under the JR Tōkaidō line bridge by the Chitose river, among others. It lists an entry fee without saying whether tax is included, so I give none. The tourism association’s site gives the same dates and suggests enjoying Yugawara’s autumn leaves and hot springs at the same time. Its English site lists Yugawara mikan, October to December, under regional cuisine.

**Kibimochi.** The English site also lists *kibimochi*, which it says many inns serve as a welcome. I found this only on the English site.

**Not on the ministry’s list.** The farm ministry’s list of Kanagawa’s local dishes has 23 entries, among them pond smelt simmered sweet from Lake Ashi in Hakone and Odawara *kamaboko*, but none from Yugawara. See [local food of Kantō](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/). The English site mentions onsen eggs and dried fish only as examples of an inn breakfast, not as Yugawara specialities, and I found no town or tourism association page tying dried fish to Yugawara.

## Getting there

As checked on 12 October 2026, from the tourism association’s access diagrams unless noted. The diagrams have no date. Fares are not on this page.

| From | Route | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Tokyo | Limited express Odoriko | About 80 min |
| Tokyo | JR Tōkaidō line | About 100 min |
| Tokyo | Kodama shinkansen to Odawara, then JR Tōkaidō line | About 34 min + about 16 min |
| Tokyo | Kodama shinkansen to Atami, then JR Tōkaidō line | About 47 min + about 5 min |
| Shinjuku | Odakyū limited express Hakone to Odawara, then JR Tōkaidō line | About 75 min + about 16 min |
| Shinjuku | JR Shōnan-Shinjuku line special rapid, then JR Tōkaidō line | About 75 min + about 16 min |
| Atami | JR Tōkaidō line | About 5 min |

The English site gives different times: 40 minutes by shinkansen to Odawara and 17 minutes on, and 1 hour 15 minutes by Odoriko. I use the Japanese diagrams and note the difference. JNTO, undated, gives about 1 hour 45 minutes from Tokyo station by JR Tōkaidō line.

**To Man’yō Park.** The town and the tourism association give about 9 to 10 minutes by bus towards Oku-Yugawara, getting off at Ochiaibashi. The town says the park is about 2.5 km from the station, about 10 minutes by taxi.

## What people get wrong

- **"The Man’yōshū poem is certainly about Yugawara."** The geopark says it "is said to" be. The town and the English site state it more firmly.
- **"Yugawara’s flow has dropped from 6,459 to 5,618 litres a minute."** The institute’s figure (2017) excludes suspended sources; the town’s (fiscal 2024) comes from a count that includes 49. The two cannot be read as a trend.
- **"The town owns all the water."** The owners keep their sources; the town buys water where it enters the pipe. The town owns 11 sources.
- **"Tattoos are fine everywhere" or "never allowed".** The English site says many inns refuse them, and to ask your inn.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide.
