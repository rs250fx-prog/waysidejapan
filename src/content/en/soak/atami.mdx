Atami, in Shizuoka, is a city where hot water is, in part, a public utility: the city itself owns sources and supplies the water to whole districts. It also has a well-known walk past seven historic springs, none of which you can bathe in. This page explains how Atami’s hot springs work using the city’s pages, the city tourism association and its English site, and JNTO. Where they differ, I give each. It does not rate any bath.

For spring types, temperature bands and how to read the analysis sign, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the notices about added water, heating, recirculation and chlorine, see the piece on *kakenagashi*.

## Where it came from

Atami’s early history is told largely as tradition, and the city’s own pages handle the dates differently.

- **The city’s hot spring page** says the *Izu Fudoki* of 713 is said to mention the "great spring" (*Ōyu*), and that a temple record states that in 749 the monk Mangan of the Hakone Gongen shrine led the hot water up to the hillside of Atami.
- **The city’s page on the origin of the name** tells, as a Nara-period story with no year, how Mangan heard fishermen lament that the sea was boiling and killing the fish, and moved the vein of hot water up into the hills — today’s Ōyu. The name, it says, comes from *atsu-umi-ga-saki*, "the cape where the sea is hot", which became *Atami*.
- **JNTO** says Atami’s water has drawn people since the Nara period (710–794).

I give 713 and 749 side by side and do not say either is the beginning.

The city’s page goes on: the regent Toyotomi Hidetsugu came to bathe in 1593, and Tokugawa Ieyasu came with two of his sons in 1604. JNTO adds that Ieyasu liked it enough to try to have the water carried to Edo. The city also notes a Western-style analysis report on the water in 1874, and the founding of the *Kyūkikan* in 1885.

## The water

The city’s hot spring page (updated 28 January 2025, with no survey year) gives:

- More than **500** sources in total.
- A total flow **approaching 20,000 litres a minute**.
- About **nine in ten** sources at 42°C or above — high-temperature springs in the official banding.
- An average temperature of about **63°C**.
- **Chloride springs** are the most common across the city. It gives no percentages.

**Why here.** I could not find an official explanation of where Atami’s heat comes from. The city has a page on the Izu Peninsula Geopark, which UNESCO recognised as a Global Geopark on 17 April 2018, but it says nothing about hot springs and volcanoes. The meteorological agency lists the East Izu volcano group as active, as the Honshu hot springs guide notes, but I found no official source connecting it to Atami’s springs, so this page does not connect them.

## How the town works

### The city-run supply

Atami runs a municipal hot spring supply. The city’s waterworks page (updated 7 August 2026) says it serves the Izumi, Atami and Minami-Atami districts, that the city has 59 municipal sources including suspended ones, and that the business was authorised on 1 July 1936. In other words, in those districts part of the hot water supply is run by the city itself.

### The Seven Springs walk

The *Atami Nanayu Meguri*, "tour of Atami’s seven springs", is a walking route past seven historic sources scattered around Atami Ginza: Ōyu Geyser, Kawara-yu, Sajirō no Yu and Me no Yu, Seizaemon no Yu, Furo no Yu and Mizu no Yu, Kozawa no Yu and Heizaemon no Yu, and Nonaka no Yu. The city says it was redeveloped in 1997 for the city’s 60th anniversary, and is clear that these are **not bathing facilities**: they are monuments recreating the facilities of the time.

The Ōyu Geyser, the city says, spouted naturally until the early Taishō era and weakened after the Great Kantō Earthquake. In 1962 it was rebuilt as an artificial geyser, and it is a city cultural property. The city writes, in the past tense, that it "was counted as one of the world’s three great geysers".

### The footbath at the station

*Ieyasu no Yu*, a natural hot spring footbath in front of Atami station, was installed in March 2004 to mark 400 years since Ieyasu’s visit. The tourism association lists it as free, 9:00–16:00 (page updated 31 August 2026; it was closed for a pipe failure and reopened on 1 September 2026).

### Day bathing

The city lists 19 day-bathing facilities, including Yamada-yu, a municipal shared bath (updated 27 February 2026). It gives no prices or hours and says to ask each facility. I could not find an official price for the shared bath, so there are no prices on this page. The tourism association has pages on day bathing and footbaths, and its English site has guide pages on day-trip onsen and private baths at ryokan, which I could not read in full.

## Bathing there

Atami’s tourism bureau publishes an English guide to bathing (updated 20 August 2026). In summary:

- No phones or cameras.
- Wash at the washing area before getting in.
- Bathe without a swimsuit or clothes.
- Keep the small towel out of the water — on the edge or on your head.
- Talk quietly; do not swim or splash.
- No eating or drinking in the bath.
- Tie up long hair.
- Dry yourself with a towel before going back to the changing room.

**Tattoos.** The same site has a page on tattoos (updated 20 August 2026). Its message is that having a tattoo does not mean giving up on onsen. It suggests a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, which costs more and is best booked early, and a skin-coloured waterproof cover sticker — a proper one prepared in advance, as improvised covers can be refused. It says facilities’ rules and fees can change, so check their official sites before you go.

## What it is eaten with

The tourism association’s souvenir feature calls *onsen manjū*, steamed buns, the number one standard souvenir, and JNTO and the association’s English site also name them. The same feature describes Atami pudding, made with local Tanna milk and steamed, as "now Atami’s signature sweet"; *himono*, dried fish; and sweets made with *mugi-kogashi*, roasted barley flour used as an offering at Kinomiya shrine. The pudding and dried fish come from the tourism association only. The farm ministry’s list of Shizuoka’s local dishes includes none of these, and I could not open the association’s page on where the dried fish is made.

## What people get wrong

- **"The Seven Springs are baths."** The city says they are not bathing facilities; they are monuments on a walking route.
- **"The Ōyu Geyser is natural."** It spouted naturally until the early Taishō era. Since 1962 it has been an artificial geyser.
- **"Atami’s heat comes from the Izu volcanoes."** That may be so, but I could not find an official source that says it, and the city’s geopark page does not.
- **"Atami dates from 713" or "from 749".** Both years appear in the city’s account, alongside a version with no year at all. This page does not choose.
- **"Atami’s pudding is a traditional local dish."** The tourism association calls it Atami’s signature sweet today; the farm ministry’s list of local dishes does not include it.
- **"Tattoos mean no onsen in Atami."** The tourism bureau’s own English advice is to look at private baths or proper covers, and to check each facility in advance.
