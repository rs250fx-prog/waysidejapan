Atami, in Shizuoka, is a city where hot water is, in part, a public utility: the city itself owns sources and supplies the water to whole districts. It also has a well-known walk past seven historic springs, none of which you can bathe in. This page explains how Atami’s hot springs work using the city’s pages, Shizuoka prefecture, the Izu Peninsula Geopark, the city tourism association and its English site, and JNTO. Where they differ, I give each. It does not rate any bath.

For spring types, temperature bands and how to read the analysis sign, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the notices about added water, heating, recirculation and chlorine, see the piece on *kakenagashi*.

## Where it came from

Atami’s early history is told largely as tradition, and the city’s own pages handle the dates differently.

- **The city’s hot spring page** says the *Izu Fudoki* of 713 is said to mention the "great spring" (*Ōyu*), and that a temple record states that in 749 the monk Mangan of the Hakone Gongen shrine led the hot water up to the hillside of Atami.
- **The city’s page on the origin of the name** tells, as a Nara-period story with no year, how Mangan heard fishermen lament that the sea was boiling and killing the fish, and moved the vein of hot water up into the hills — today’s Ōyu. The name, it says, comes from *atsu-umi-ga-saki*, "the cape where the sea is hot", which became *Atami*.
- **JNTO** says Atami’s water has drawn people since the Nara period (710–794).

I give 713 and 749 side by side and do not say either is the beginning.

The city’s hot spring page goes on: the regent Toyotomi Hidetsugu came to bathe in 1593, and Tokugawa Ieyasu came with two of his sons in 1604. The city’s page on the Edo period adds that Ieyasu first bathed here in 1602, that the 1604 stay with his sons Yoshinao and Yorinobu lasted seven days, and that later that year he sent Atami water to Kikkawa Hiroie, who was recuperating in Kyoto. The city also notes a Western-style analysis report on the water in 1874, and the founding of the *Kyūkikan* in 1885.

### Water carried to the shogun

JNTO says Ieyasu liked the water enough to try to have it carried to Edo. The city describes the practice that followed, *okumiyu*: water drawn from Ōyu, sealed in new cypress barrels that were not allowed to touch the ground, and carried to Edo Castle by relays of porters from village to village, later by boat. Only the masters of the *yuto*, the households that piped water from Ōyu into their own baths, drew it.

The city’s two pages differ on the details, and this page does not choose:

- **When it began.** A 2020 article in the city’s local-history series says the Kanbun era (1661–1673); the city’s Edo-period page says the time of the fourth shogun, Ietsuna, without a year.
- **How many barrels.** Both give 3,643 barrels from 1726, under the eighth shogun Yoshimune, but the series article says over nine years and the Edo-period page says over eight.

### Ajiro

Ajiro, in the south of the city, is a later spring town. The city’s local-history series says a test well by Takei Kakutarō, begun in spring 1937, reached water above 60°C at a depth of about 1,500 *shaku* around August 1938. The same article cites a local history saying a Tokyo doctor named Sano struck low-temperature water earlier, in 1924, and argues his part deserves more credit. I give both.

## The water

The city’s hot spring page (updated 28 January 2025, with no survey year) gives:

- More than **500** sources in total.
- A total flow **approaching 20,000 litres a minute**.
- About **nine in ten** sources at 42°C or above — high-temperature springs in the official banding.
- An average temperature of about **63°C**.
- **Chloride springs** are the most common across the city. It gives no percentages.

**Shizuoka prefecture’s survey.** The prefecture’s hot spring survey, as of 1 February 2023, has a row for Atami city. It counts **522** sources, of which **282** are in use, with a total flow of about **19,168 litres a minute** and an average temperature of about **62.8°C**. In that table every source in use in Atami is pumped; none flows out on its own. The table divides the city into six districts, of which the Atami district has the most sources (331).

**The innkeepers’ figures.** The Atami hotel and ryokan cooperative, an industry body, gives different numbers on its own site, with no year or surveying body: 542 wells and a total flow of 16,564 litres a minute. It is also the only source I found with a breakdown by type: chloride 60.2%, sulphate 27.6% and simple 12.2%. I found no national, prefectural or city figure for the breakdown.

So the count is "more than 500" (city, no year), 522 (prefecture, 2023) or 542 (cooperative, no year). The sources do not say why they differ, and I do not treat them as one figure.

**Why here.** The city’s Izu Peninsula Geopark page for Atami (updated 1 August 2018) says the steep slopes the town is built on are the eroded eastern half of the Taga volcano, a large land volcano, and that the city’s scenery and hot springs, with the opening of the Tanna tunnel, made it a major resort. The Izu Peninsula Geopark’s own site says the springs of Atami and Itō are said to be a gift of three volcanoes — Yugawara, Taga and Usami — active from about a million to about 300,000 years ago. Neither explains how that heat becomes hot water, and I found no meteorological agency, geological survey or university source that does.

The same city page also says that none of the small volcanoes of the East Izu volcano group are found in the city. The meteorological agency lists that group as active, but this page does not connect it to Atami’s springs.

## How the town works

### The city-run supply

Atami runs a municipal hot spring supply. The city’s waterworks page (updated 7 August 2026) says it serves the Izumi, Atami and Minami-Atami districts, and that hot water can be piped not only to hotels and inns but also to homes and holiday houses. The city has 59 municipal sources including suspended ones, and the business was authorised on 1 July 1936 as a town enterprise, becoming a city one the following year.

### The Seven Springs walk

The *Atami Nanayu Meguri*, "tour of Atami’s seven springs", is a walking route past seven historic sources scattered around Atami Ginza: Ōyu Geyser, Kawara-yu, Sajirō no Yu and Me no Yu, Seizaemon no Yu, Furo no Yu and Mizu no Yu, Kozawa no Yu and Heizaemon no Yu, and Nonaka no Yu. The city says it was redeveloped in 1997 for the city’s 60th anniversary, and is clear that these are **not bathing facilities**: they are monuments recreating the facilities of the time.

The city’s page tells a story for several of them. Seizaemon no Yu is named for a farmer who fell into the spring with his horse. At Kozawa, high-temperature steam can cook eggs, though there is less in some weather. The steam of Furo no Yu was used to steam buns and warm sake, while Mizu no Yu is cooler and has no salt. At Nonaka, the water rises from mud. Sajirō no Yu was in the grounds of a man called Sajirō.

### The Ōyu Geyser

The city gives Ōyu as a city-designated cultural property, designated in 1977. It was rebuilt in 1962, and today it spouts by human hand for three minutes roughly every five minutes, 8:00 to 19:00, and is free to see (city page updated 21 July 2026).

Its history is told in different words:

- The city’s Seven Springs page says it spouted naturally until the early Taishō era and **weakened** after the Great Kantō Earthquake.
- The Izu Peninsula Geopark says its flow fell from the mid-Meiji period and that it **stopped** with the earthquake.
- On how often it spouted, the city’s hot spring page quotes the 713 *Fudoki* as saying twice in a day and a night, and its local-history series says six times a day, regularly, in the Edo period. These describe different centuries.

The city also writes, in the past tense, that it "was counted as one of the world’s three great geysers".

### The footbath at the station

*Ieyasu no Yu*, a natural hot spring footbath in front of Atami station, was installed in March 2004 to mark 400 years since Ieyasu’s visit. The tourism association lists it as free, 9:00–16:00 (page updated 31 August 2026; it was closed for a pipe failure and reopened on 1 September 2026).

### Day bathing

The city lists 19 day-bathing facilities (updated 27 February 2026). It names two as communal baths, *kyōdō yokujō*: Yamada-yu and the Atami Ekimae Onsen Yokujō, also called Tawara Yokujō. It notes that at some communal baths you need to bring your own towel, soap and shampoo. It gives no prices or hours and says to ask each facility.

The tourism association adds that hotels and inns may not take day bathers, depending on their bookings. Its day-bathing page carries only holiday-season notices for the two communal baths, not regular hours or prices. As checked on 11 October 2026, I could not find an official price for either communal bath with its tax status, so there are no prices on this page.

## Bathing there

Atami’s tourism bureau publishes an English guide to bathing (updated 20 August 2026). In summary:

- No phones or cameras.
- Wash at the washing area before getting in.
- Bathe without a swimsuit or clothes.
- Keep the small towel out of the water — on the edge or on your head.
- Talk quietly; do not swim or splash.
- No eating or drinking in the bath.
- Tie up long hair.
- Dry yourself with a towel before going back to the changing room.

**Tattoos.** The same site has a page on tattoos (updated 20 August 2026). It says policies differ from facility to facility, and its message is that having a tattoo does not mean giving up on onsen. It recommends a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, which costs more and is best booked early. Some facilities accept tattoos that are covered — by a rash guard, a towel or a proper cover sticker prepared in advance, as improvised covers can be refused — and some allow them only in private baths. It says facilities’ rules and fees can change, so check their official sites before you go. I found no tattoo guidance on the city’s own hot spring pages.

## What it is eaten with

**Dried fish.** Shizuoka prefecture’s food culture site lists *himono*, dried fish, under Atami: horse mackerel is landed in quantity in Sagami Bay, it says, and the fish are made with brine from sun-dried sea salt and the sea breeze. The tourism association’s souvenir feature says you can buy dried horse mackerel, squid, *kinmedai* (splendid alfonsino), barracuda and mackerel at the station building, the shopping streets by the station and old shops on Ginza-dōri. In Ajiro, the 51st Ajiro Onsen dried fish festival is listed by the tourism association for 14–15 November and 12–13 December 2026, and 10–11 January and 13–14 February 2027, 10:00–14:30, free entry.

**Onsen manjū.** The tourism association calls steamed buns the number one standard souvenir, and JNTO also names them. Shizuoka prefecture’s food culture site (published 31 January 2024) says Atami’s Enmeidō is regarded as their birthplace, and that, as the story is told, its first owner thought of steaming them with hot water that spouted out at high temperature. It gives no founding year.

**Pudding.** The tourism association’s souvenir feature describes Atami pudding, made with local Tanna milk and steamed, as "now Atami’s signature sweet". That comes from the tourism association only: it is not on the prefecture’s Atami food page or in the farm ministry’s list of Shizuoka’s local dishes, which includes none of these three.

## What people get wrong

- **"The Seven Springs are baths."** The city says they are not bathing facilities; they are monuments on a walking route.
- **"The Ōyu Geyser is natural."** It spouted naturally until the early Taishō era. Since 1962 it has been artificial, spouting by human hand about every five minutes.
- **"The Ōyu Geyser is a national monument."** The city’s page calls it a city-designated cultural property. I could not confirm any national designation.
- **"Atami’s heat comes from the active Izu volcanoes."** The geopark links Atami’s springs to old volcanoes — Taga, Yugawara and Usami — and the city says the small volcanoes of the active East Izu group are not found in Atami. No official source I read explains the mechanism.
- **"Atami dates from 713" or "from 749".** Both years appear in the city’s account, alongside a version with no year at all. This page does not choose.
- **"Atami’s pudding is a traditional local dish."** The tourism association calls it Atami’s signature sweet today; neither the prefecture’s Atami food page nor the farm ministry’s list of local dishes includes it.
- **"Tattoos mean no onsen in Atami."** The tourism bureau’s own English advice is that it varies by facility, to look at private baths or proper covers, and to check each facility in advance.
