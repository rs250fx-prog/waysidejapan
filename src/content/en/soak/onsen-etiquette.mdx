faq:
  - q: Do I have to wash before getting into an onsen?
    a: Yes. The environment ministry’s standard says to pour water over yourself, starting with your hands and feet, and wash before getting in. JNTO says to wash at the washing area first. Town guides such as Visit Kinosaki add that you should sit down to wash and rinse off all the soap.
  - q: Can I wear a swimsuit in an onsen?
    a: Not in most baths. JNTO says to bathe naked, without a swimsuit. A few mixed baths are different. Shin-Hotaka no Yu in Okuhida requires swimwear or a bathing wrap, and Shirahama’s Shirasuna is entered in swimsuits.
  - q: Should I rinse off after bathing?
    a: The ministry’s standard says not to rinse the onsen water off, but to towel dry, dress and rest for about 30 minutes. It adds that people with sensitive skin are better off rinsing after a strong spring, such as an acidic or sulphur spring, or where chlorine is used.
  - q: Where do I put the small towel?
    a: Not in the water. JNTO says to put it on your head or on the edge of the bath. The ministry’s standard also says to keep towels out of the bath to keep the water clean.
  - q: Can children use the onsen?
    a: The ministry’s standard says children, like older people, should preferably not bathe alone. Some places set age limits for bathing on the side of the opposite sex. Shirahama’s three town baths put it at roughly seven and over.

Most of what you need to know about bathing in a Japanese onsen fits on one sign, and Japan already has two official versions of it. One is the environment ministry’s standard for the precautions every onsen must post, written for bathers in Japan. The other is the guide that JNTO, the national tourism organisation, writes for visitors. This page sets out both, step by step, and adds what individual onsen towns tell their own guests where it differs or goes further. It does not say what any water will do for your health.

## What it is

### Two official sources

**The ministry standard.** Under the Hot Springs Act, every onsen offered to the public must post precautions for bathing. The environment ministry sets what those precautions say in a standard revised on 1 July 2014. It applies to inns, public baths and similar places that offer onsen water, but not in full to hospitals using onsen as part of treatment. It is about safe bathing — when not to bathe, how long, what to do if you feel faint — rather than manners. The ministry also publishes a Japanese leaflet and, since March 2019, an English booklet, *The ABCs for the Safe and Secure Use of Onsen*, along the same lines.

**JNTO’s guide.** JNTO’s English guide, *How to Best Enjoy Japan’s Onsen*, covers the visitor’s side: what to take off, where to wash, what to do with the towel. It carries no date.

The two agree on the two points that matter most to the people around you: wash before you get in, and keep your towel out of the water.

## Step by step

### Before you go in

Leave at least some time after a meal, and do not bathe after drinking. Both the ministry and JNTO say so, and many town guides repeat it: Dōgo Onsen’s official English manners begin "Do not bathe under the influence of alcohol", and Yugawara’s and Beppu city’s advice says the same. Drink a glass of water before you start, as the ministry suggests.

### The changing room

Changing rooms are separate for men and women; Visit Kinosaki notes that, there, the women’s side is marked in red and the men’s in blue or purple. Take everything off — JNTO says swimwear stays in the changing room too, and Visit Kinosaki lists swimsuits and underwear among things not allowed. Lock your things away and, as Dōgo and Kinosaki both say, keep the locker key on your wrist.

Take only the small towel into the bathing room.

### Washing first

Sit down at the washing area — Dōgo’s English manners ask you not to use the shower standing up — and wash with soap and rinse it all off before you go near the bath. Then pour some bath water over yourself. This is *kake-yu*. The ministry says to start with the hands and feet to get used to the heat. Echigo-Yuzawa’s shared-bath guide says to start from the feet. A Sendai tourism foundation page, crediting the Japan Health Development Foundation, suggests about ten scoops, starting far from the heart, at the ankles and wrists. Shirahone’s English page gives the order legs, arms, then belly.

Some baths have no washing area. Okuhida’s tourism association says that at Hirayu no Yu, which has none, you pour water over yourself before getting in.

### In the bath

- **The small towel stays out.** On your head or on the edge, as JNTO puts it.
- **Hair stays out.** Tie it up if it is long. Visit Kinosaki lists letting your towel or hair touch the water among the things not to do.
- **Get in slowly and stay quiet.** The ministry asks for quiet bathing. Gero’s inn cooperative asks people using its bath pass to bathe in silence (*mokuyoku*) so that talk does not disturb others.
- **No swimming, diving or splashing.** JNTO, Dōgo and Kinosaki all say so; Kinosaki adds no climbing the walls, no shaving or brushing teeth, and not taking over a spot.
- **No food or drink in the water.** Atami’s and Kinosaki’s guides say so, and JNTO’s 2024 article on etiquette asks the same.
- **Ask before adding cold water.** Free-flowing baths can be very hot. Beppu’s tourism association asks you not to add cold water yourself but to ask the local bathers first. Echigo-Yuzawa’s guide asks you not to thin the water without need, and Shibu’s guide says to turn the tap off once the bath is bearable. Yugawara’s English guide says not to cool the water with cold water even if it is hot.
- **Greet people.** In small shared baths, local guides ask you to greet those already in the bath: Beppu’s association says to do so when you come in and again when you leave, and Shibu’s and Echigo-Yuzawa’s guides say to say a word to the people there.

### Photos and phones

JNTO says no phones or photos in the bath. Dōgo extends that to the changing rooms as well as the bathing rooms, and Kinosaki lists cameras and photography as not allowed. Kurokawa’s inns’ association bans photos in the baths and changing rooms but says the streets are fine. Beppu’s association notes that, at Takegawara, a group may be allowed to take one phone in at a time, and to ask the staff. Unless a sign or the staff tell you otherwise, leave the phone in the locker.

### How long, and feeling faint

The ministry suggests about 3 to 10 minutes at a time to begin with, extending to 15 or 20 once you are used to it, and once or twice a day for the first few days. Beppu city’s page suggests 3 to 10 minutes at first. Older people and people with high blood pressure, heart disease or a past stroke should avoid baths of 42°C or more, according to the standard. Kusatsu’s ryokan cooperative, in English, suggests putting your lower body in first and getting out when sweat appears on your forehead or nose.

If you feel dizzy or unwell, the ministry says to call for help from someone nearby, keep your head low, get out slowly and lie down until you recover. JNTO says to get out and drink cold water if you overheat, and Visit Kinosaki says to get out and cool down if you feel too hot or light-headed. Stand up slowly when you leave the bath.

The Japanese word *yuatari* is sometimes translated as overheating, but in the ministry standard it means something else: feeling unwell, sleeping badly, stomach trouble or skin inflammation roughly three days to a week into a course of spa bathing. The standard says to stop or bathe less until it passes.

### Rinsing off, or not

This is the step where advice differs most, so here is the ministry’s own wording, translated. After bathing, do not wash the onsen components off your body with warm water; wipe off the water with a towel, dress, and keep warm and rest for about 30 minutes. The exception, in brackets: people with sensitive skin are better off washing the onsen components off with warm water after a strong spring, such as an acidic or sulphur spring, or where chlorine disinfection is used. The ministry’s leaflet says the same, in shorter form.

Several town guides follow the main line. Yugawara’s English guide says not to shower again after the bath; Shirahone’s English page says not to rinse off with fresh water; the Sendai tourism foundation page says rinsing is better skipped, depending on the water. None of them repeats the ministry’s exception, so if you have sensitive skin and the sign says the water is acidic, or a sulphur spring, or chlorinated, the ministry’s wording is the one to follow. Which springs are which is explained in [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/), and how to tell whether a bath is chlorinated is in [*kakenagashi* explained](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

### After

Dry yourself before going back to the changing room, as Atami’s and Kinosaki’s guides ask. Drink a glass of water, as both the ministry and Dōgo’s manners suggest, and rest. The ministry suggests about 30 minutes.

## Children

The ministry standard says children, like older people and people with disabilities, should preferably not bathe alone. Drinking onsen water is a separate matter: the same standard says children aged 15 and under should generally not drink it unless a doctor advises otherwise.

Kurokawa’s inns’ association, in Japanese, gives bath temperatures of about 40 to 43°C and says this is on the hot side for babies and small children, suggesting short baths.

Age limits for bathing on the side of the opposite sex are set locally. At Shirahama’s three town baths — Shirara-yu, Muro no Yu and Saki no Yu — the tourism association says that, after a change in the ordinance, children of roughly seven and over may no longer do so. Other places may set the age differently; ask at the desk.

## Mixed bathing

Most baths are separated by sex. Mixed baths (*kon’yoku*) do exist, each with its own rules, and official sources describe them only case by case:

- **Sukayu** (Aomori). The environment ministry’s plan for the area states that the large Sennin-buro is mixed. It says that, as manners have declined, some women find it hard to use; that screens and awareness campaigns had limited effect; and that the question remains open. The operator has a women-only hour from 8:00 to 9:00 and, since May 2024, rents bathing wear (*yuami-gi*) to men and women; wearing it is optional and you may not bring your own.
- **Yubara** (Okayama). The free open-air Sunayu is mixed, with simple changing areas separate for men and women. The Maniwa tourism bureau and Okayama’s tourism federation recommend a rented bathing wrap for women, say a towel may be wrapped around the body, ask men to wrap a towel too, allow swimwear, and do not allow underwear or other clothes.
- **Misasa** (Tottori). Tottori’s tourism federation describes the riverside Kawara-buro as free, open all day, mixed, with a simple changing area.
- **Okuhida** (Gifu). The tourism association says swimwear or a *yuami-gi* is required at the mixed open-air Shin-Hotaka no Yu.
- **Takaragawa, Minakami** (Gunma). The inn says its own bathing wear is included in the day-bathing fee and you may not wear your own.
- **Shirahama** (Wakayama). Shirasuna, an open-air bath on the beach, is mixed and entered in swimsuits, according to the town.
- **Kurokawa** (Kumamoto). The inns’ association says that, because of a change in Kumamoto prefecture’s rules, its open-air baths are no longer mixed, and suggests private baths for families and couples.

Read the rules for each bath where you are; what is allowed in one mixed bath does not carry over to another.

## Tattoos

Official sources describe tattoo rules as varying from facility to facility. The tourism agency says ryokan differ widely, and JNTO says to check in advance and that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option. The full picture, with what each onsen town’s official sources say, is in [onsen and tattoos](/en/soak/onsen-tattoos/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Washing first is just a polite extra."** It is the one step that both the ministry standard and JNTO’s guide insist on, together with keeping the towel out of the water.
- **"You must never rinse off."** The ministry’s standard says not to rinse as a rule, but makes an exception for sensitive skin after acidic or sulphur springs, or where chlorine is used.
- **"Overheating is *yuatari*."** In the ministry’s wording *yuatari* is feeling unwell days into a course of bathing. Feeling faint in the bath is handled differently: head low, get out slowly, lie down.
- **"Mixed baths mean anything goes."** Each has rules, from bathing wear being optional (Sukayu) to being required (Shin-Hotaka) to being supplied by the inn (Takaragawa).
- **"Adding cold water is fine if it is too hot."** In shared baths, local guides ask you to check with the people there first.
