Unzen is a hot spring town at about 700 m, roughly in the middle of the Shimabara peninsula in Nagasaki prefecture. Its centre is the *jigoku*, the "hells": a stretch of bare white ground where hot water and steam come out of the earth. In the Meiji era it was a summer retreat for Western traders from Nagasaki and Shanghai, and in 1934 it became one of Japan’s first national parks. This page uses the Unzen tourism bureau, Unzen city, the Japan Tourism Agency, the environment ministry, the Japan Meteorological Agency, Shimabara city and the Shimabara Peninsula Geopark, the Japan Hot Spring Association and JNTO. Where sources disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

**Unzen is not Obama.** Obama Onsen, on the coast, is a different hot spring town in the same city, with different water. This page is about Unzen Onsen in the mountains.

For how Unzen compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the [Kyushu hot springs overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/). For what its spring names mean, see the page on [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) and, for the iron spring, [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/). The Saga towns of [Takeo](/en/soak/takeo/) and [Ureshino](/en/soak/ureshino/) have their own guides.

## Where it came from

### How far back

The tourism agency’s commentary says Unzen’s history can be traced back about 1,300 years, to the arrival of Buddhism. Another tourism agency text, quoted on the [Kyushu overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/), dates it to 701 and the monk Gyōki. The two texts put the starting point differently, and I give both.

### A summer resort for foreigners

The tourism agency says that in the Meiji era, Western traders came each summer from Nagasaki and Shanghai to escape the heat of the cities, using the town and its surroundings as a highland resort. The Japan Hot Spring Association says Unzen flourished as a summer resort for foreigners in the Meiji and Taishō eras. Nagasaki prefecture’s tourism federation calls it an international resort known early on even in Europe.

### One of the first national parks

The environment ministry’s history page says that on **16 March 1934**, Setonaikai, Unzen and Kirishima were designated as Japan’s first national parks, all three together. Its page for the park calls Unzen one of the country’s first national parks, and says the Amakusa area was added in 1956 to form Unzen-Amakusa National Park.

The other sources word this differently. The tourism agency says "one of Japan’s first national parks". The Japan Hot Spring Association calls it "national park No. 1", and the prefecture’s tourism federation says it was designated Japan’s first national park in 1934. The ministry lists the three on the same day and does not rank them, so I say "one of the first three".

The Japan Hot Spring Association also says Unzen is a designated national health resort onsen, *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*. I could not confirm it on the environment ministry’s own list, which I could read only in part.

## The volcano

### The mountain

Shimabara city’s geopark page (updated 27 October 2015) says the Unzen volcano began erupting about 500,000 years ago, that the peninsula began about 4.3 million years ago with basaltic eruptions on the sea floor, and that the centre of the peninsula is a block sinking between faults, the Unzen graben. It does not explain the hot springs. The geopark’s glossary describes Unzen Onsen as a hot spring area roughly in the middle of the peninsula, and the hells as a geothermal area within it, bare of plants, hot and smelling of sulphur, suggesting hell. I could not find an official explanation of how the hells form in the material I read.

### The 1990–95 eruption

The Japan Meteorological Agency’s record of the Unzendake eruption runs as follows.

- **1990.** On 17 November, steam eruptions began at two craters east of the summit of Fugendake, the Jigokuato crater and the Tsukumojima crater.
- **1991.** On 20 May a lava dome was seen in the Jigokuato crater, and from 24 May pyroclastic flows, avalanches of hot gas and rock, came often. The flow of **3 June** left **43 people dead or missing** and damaged 179 buildings. Further flows caused damage on 8 June and 15 September.
- **1993.** Flows on 23 and 24 June killed one person and damaged 187 buildings.
- **1995.** The lava dome stopped changing in late January, and the last pyroclastic flow came on 11 February. From 1991 to 1995 about 200 million cubic metres of lava came out, with about 9,400 pyroclastic flows.

Shimabara city’s disaster chronology (updated 1 April 2016) adds that on 20 May 1996 the city and the town of Obama named the lava dome **Heisei Shinzan**, and that on 3 June 1996 the end of the eruption was declared. It gives the toll of 3 June 1991 as 43 dead, without "missing"; the agency writes "dead or missing". I give both wordings.

I found no official source that connects the eruption with Unzen Onsen’s springs, so I do not say how one affected the other. They are on the same volcanic mountains; that is as far as the sources go.

### As checked on 12 October 2026

The Japan Meteorological Agency’s page for Unzendake showed an **eruption forecast at alert level 1**, with a reminder that it is an active volcano. Alert levels have been in use there since 1 December 2007. The page warned of fumaroles around Heisei Shinzan and said that entering that area could be life-threatening. Its latest monthly commentary was for September 2026, issued on 8 October. Levels can change at any time, so check the agency before you go.

## The water

### What kind

- **The Unzen tourism bureau**: acidic hydrogen sulphide springs, hydrogen sulphide-containing acidic alum springs and *ryokuban* (iron sulphate) springs, generally called sulphur springs. It says the water is strongly acidic.
- **The Japan Hot Spring Association**: an iron spring, an acidic spring (hydrogen sulphide type) and a sulphur spring. Its overview of Unzen and Obama calls Unzen a sulphur spring.
- **The geopark glossary**: a sulphur spring.

Several of the bureau’s names are older forms. How the modern names work, including the label "hydrogen sulphide type", is explained on the [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) page.

### Why some baths are milky

The bureau says the water turns cloudy white at some inns because, when it is piped from the hells, mud containing sulphur gets mixed in. That is the bureau’s explanation.

### No figures

I could not find an official count of sources, flow, pH or source temperature for Unzen Onsen. The bureau gives no pH or temperature figures, and the Japan Hot Spring Association’s entry has no update date, temperature, count or flow. Its overview says Obama has about 30 sources but gives none for Unzen.

## How the town works

### The hells

The tourism bureau’s page describes the hells as an area between the Koyu and Shin-yu districts, covered in white altered clay, where hot water and steam blow out and the smell of sulphur hangs in the air.

- **More than 30 named hells**, among them Daikyōkan, Oito and Seishichi. The Daikyōkan viewpoint is where the steam is most violent.
- **Walkways.** A wooden walkway crosses a mud-volcano area. Unzen city’s tourism page (updated 18 November 2021) says the walk takes about 30 minutes, that eggs steamed in the hells’ steam are sold along the way, and that plants designated as a national natural monument grow there.
- **History.** A memorial to Christian martyrs stands in the hells, and a festival for them is held every year on the third Sunday of May.
- **Access.** Entry is free and open 24 hours. There is no lighting at night. About 200 paid parking spaces are nearby.
- **Gas.** The bureau says the steam contains hydrogen sulphide and other gases and has a strong sulphur smell, and that because it is a natural phenomenon, conditions can change suddenly.

The bureau’s page gives no update date and no information on closed paths. The ministry’s general rules on hydrogen sulphide are on the [acidic and sulphur springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) page.

### Foot steaming and the footbath

- **Foot steam.** The bureau describes a rest area where you feel the ground’s heat and steam through your feet. It is open all year but closed in rain or when it is too hot. The bureau warns that bare feet, or keeping your feet in one place for long, can cause burns.
- **Footbath plaza** in Shin-yu, roofed: 9:00 to 17:00, open all year, a round bath about 4 m across for about ten people. The bureau gives no fee.

### Night walk

The environment ministry’s national park page describes a guided walk of about 60 minutes through the hells at night, carrying paper lanterns, in spring, summer and autumn, with stories of the Christians and of Oito. It names the tourism bureau as the contact. I do not give a price.

### Communal baths

The bureau’s FAQ says there are communal baths and footbaths. I could not find their prices with a tax status, so none are given.

## Bathing there

I found no official prices for baths in Unzen with a stated tax status, so none are given.

- **Tattoos.** The bureau’s FAQ in Japanese does not mention tattoos, and its old English page returned an error. I found no official guidance. JNTO’s general guide (undated) says some onsen refuse people with tattoos, that you should check in advance, that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option, and that small cover sheets only suit very small tattoos. The wider picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **The water.** The bureau says it is strongly acidic. I did not find its guidance on rinsing after bathing.
- **Manners.** JNTO’s guide says to leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room, wash before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water, tie up long hair, not swim or splash, and not take phones or cameras in.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Unzen source that compares its water with baths in the cities.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish tied to Unzen Onsen in official sources.

- **Obama champon is Obama’s.** The farm ministry’s champon page says pork-bone Nagasaki champon and chicken-bone Obama champon are called the prefecture’s two main kinds, and that the **Obama-machi Onsen ryokan association in Unzen city** uses Obama champon in events to promote the area. That is the coastal town, not Unzen Onsen. The ministry says champon is said to have been created in 1899 by the owner of the Chinese restaurant Shikairō, with several theories on the name. More is in the guide to [champon](/en/eat/champon/).
- **Shimabara gu-zōni.** The farm ministry places it in the Shimabara peninsula and says it is said to have begun with the defenders of the 1637 Shimabara Rebellion. The page does not mention Unzen or hot springs.
- ***Rokubei.*** The ministry places it in Shimabara city and Tsushima, and ties it to famine after the collapse of Mount Mayuyama in 1792. The page does not mention Unzen, Obama or hot springs.
- **Eggs in the hells.** Unzen city’s page mentions eggs steamed in the hells’ steam, sold on the walkway.

More of Nagasaki’s dishes are on the [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/) page.

## Getting there

As given by Nagasaki prefecture’s tourism federation and the Unzen tourism bureau, checked on 12 October 2026. Neither shows an update date. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Nagasaki station.** Direct prefectural bus, about **1 hour 40 minutes**. About 1 hour 20 minutes by car.
- **From Nagasaki Airport.** Prefectural or Shimatetsu bus to Isahaya, about 30 to 45 minutes, then a Shimatetsu bus, about 1 hour 20 minutes: about 2 hours 5 minutes in all, by the federation’s figure, which varies by service and does not include waiting time. The bureau gives about 35 minutes to Isahaya station, and about 1 hour 20 minutes by car from the airport.
- **From Isahaya station.** Shimatetsu bus, about **1 hour 15 to 20 minutes**. The federation gives 1 hour 15 minutes and the bureau 1 hour 20 minutes.
- **From Sasebo.** JR rapid to Isahaya, about 1 hour 20 minutes, then the Shimatetsu bus, about 1 hour 20 minutes.
- **From Shimabara.** From Shimabara station, about 40 minutes by car or about 50 minutes by Shimatetsu bus. The bureau gives about 30 minutes by car from Shimabara port.
- **By car.** About 50 minutes from the Isahaya interchange.
- **From Kumamoto.** The bureau mentions car ferries from Kumamoto port, about 30 minutes by fast boat and about 60 minutes by ordinary boat. From what I could read, it was not clear which port they arrive at.

I could not find official routes or times from Fukuoka or Hakata, or overland from Kumamoto.

## What people get wrong

- **"Obama champon is an Unzen Onsen dish."** The farm ministry ties it to the inns’ association of Obama, a separate town on the coast.
- **"Unzen and Obama are one onsen."** They are two towns in one city, with different water.
- **"Unzen was Japan’s first national park."** It was one of three designated on the same day in 1934.
- **"The eruption heated the springs," or "made the hells."** I found no official source that links the 1990s eruption to the springs, or that explains how the hells form.
- **"The hells are always the same."** The bureau says the steam contains hydrogen sulphide and that conditions can change suddenly, and the meteorological agency warns about the area around Heisei Shinzan.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications by spring type is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
