Search for "onsen Japan tattoo" and you will find confident answers in both directions: tattoos are banned everywhere, or nobody minds any more. The official sources say neither. They say it depends on the place, and they say how to find out. This page sets out what Japan’s tourism agency and JNTO, the national tourism organisation, say; what the options are if a bath says no; and, for each onsen town in this series, what its own official sources say — or that they say nothing. It names no individual inn or bath as tattoo-friendly unless an official source does.

## What it is

### No single rule

The official sources used here do not point to any national law on tattoos in baths. The tourism agency, in a Q&A written for ryokan, treats it as a matter on which inns differ widely, and JNTO tells visitors to check each facility’s policy. Whether the Hot Springs Act or other bathing law mentions tattoos is not something these sources address, so this page does not say either way.

### The tourism agency’s Q&A

The agency’s answer, in summary:

- **Practice varies widely** from inn to inn.
- **Why refusals happen.** In Japan, tattoos have been associated with organised crime, and inns have turned tattooed bathers away to avoid making other guests uneasy.
- **What is changing.** With wider understanding of tattoos as part of overseas visitors’ culture, some inns now set no restriction, and some judge a small, single tattoo case by case.
- **The alternative.** Where an inn has a private bath or a bath in the room, a guest with tattoos can enjoy bathing there.
- **The advice.** If you are concerned, ask the inn in advance.

The Q&A carries no date.

### JNTO’s guidance

JNTO’s English guide to onsen (undated) says some traditional onsen do not accept guests with tattoos but that the number of places that do is growing. It says to check the policy beforehand, points to the private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, as an option, and notes that some places sell or lend cover stickers, which suit only very small tattoos.

A JNTO blog post of 8 December 2020, under a named writer, gives the background. It links refusals to tattoos’ association with organised crime and to bathhouses wanting to avoid unsettling other customers, and notes that people have tattoos for many reasons, including self-expression, culture and religion.

A later JNTO article, of 20 June 2024, describes the situation as very much case by case and says many onsen owners do not mind. It says small to medium tattoos can be covered with stickers, that a private bath booked in advance, for an extra fee, is the surest option, and that you should ask each place directly.

### The 30% figure

The 2020 JNTO blog post says that about 30% of lodgings allowed guests with tattoos to bathe, citing a tourism agency survey from 2015. Nagano prefecture’s official English guide uses a similar figure, saying about 30% of facility and hotel managers do not refuse tattooed guests, according to the tourism agency. The survey itself is not among the sources used here, so the number of places that answered, how the question was asked and the share that refused are not confirmed. The figure is also more than a decade old. Treat it as JNTO reports it — a rough picture from 2015, not a current count.

## The options

### A private bath

Both the tourism agency and JNTO point to private baths. A *kashikiri-buro* is a bath reserved for you or your group for a set time; some inns also have baths in the guest rooms. The Atami Tourism Bureau notes that private baths cost more and are best booked early, and JNTO’s 2024 article calls advance booking the surest route. Kurokawa’s inns’ association says some of its inns offer private baths to day visitors; they generally cannot be booked, so ask the inn whether one is free.

### Covers

Cover stickers come up often, with conditions:

- **JNTO** says they suit only very small tattoos.
- **The Atami Tourism Bureau** says some facilities accept tattoos covered by a rash guard, a towel or a proper waterproof cover sticker prepared in advance, and that improvised covers can be refused.
- **Gunma prefecture’s** English guide says a small tattoo may be acceptable covered with a plaster, but to check first.
- **The Kumano Tourism Bureau** suggests covering tattoos that might look aggressive with tape or a bandage, but notes that some places do not allow taped skin in the water.
- **Aso’s inn cooperative**, in a 2026 notice about a free-bathing scheme, listed people with tattoos, including body paint and stickers, among those who would be turned away. That notice covered one scheme, but it shows that a sticker is not always an answer.

### Asking ahead

All the official advice comes back to this. Kurokawa’s inns’ association asks you to use its contact form and give details such as the size of the tattoo, which is a good model: say where the tattoo is and how large, and ask whether it can be covered or whether a private bath is available.

## How to read all this

- **Official statements are usually about one thing.** Gero’s rule is for one pass, Takeo’s for one bath, Aso’s for one scheme, Kinosaki’s for the public baths but not the inns. Read what each covers before you generalise.
- **Dates matter.** Many of these pages carry no date, and Kinosaki’s and Kusatsu’s show how official pages can lag or disagree. Atami’s page, updated in August 2026, warns that rules change.
- **The direction of travel is described, not measured.** The tourism agency, JNTO and Visit Kaga all say more places are accepting tattoos. The only figure, about 30%, is from 2015 and reached here only through JNTO and Nagano’s guide.
- **Private baths are the common answer.** Every national source and several local ones point to them.

For how bathing works once you are in, see [onsen etiquette](/en/soak/onsen-etiquette/); for what the sign by the bath says about the water, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Tattoos are banned in Japanese onsen."** The tourism agency says inns differ widely, and some set no restriction.
- **"Tattoos are fine now."** JNTO says some traditional onsen still do not accept them, and several towns’ official sources say many places refuse.
- **"There is a law."** The official sources used here do not cite one; they treat it as each facility’s policy.
- **"A sticker solves it."** JNTO says stickers suit only very small tattoos, Atami says improvised covers can be refused, and one Aso notice counted stickers among grounds for refusal.
- **"No mention means no problem."** For most towns the official pages say nothing either way. Ask.
- **"Kinosaki’s rule covers my inn."** Visit Kinosaki says the inns’ own baths may have different rules.
