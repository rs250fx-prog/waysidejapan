Iizaka Onsen is a spa town in Fukushima city, split in two by the Surikami river. Its character comes from its shared public baths: small bathhouses used by local people, run by the city or by a local property ward, and often very hot. This page uses Fukushima city, the Iizaka Onsen tourism association (whose site also carries the Iizaka Onsen ryokan cooperative’s name), Fukushima City Tourism Development Co. (which runs the guide site for the shared baths), the Fukushima prefectural tourism body’s English site, the Cultural Agency and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, each is given with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the simple springs named below, see [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/); for why "radium" is not a spring type, see [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). For reading the sign at any bath, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### Eight baths, six supply names

The tourism association’s page on the shared baths gives, for each bath, the source it draws on, the spring type, the source temperature, the pH and the month of analysis. In summary:

- **Six source names** feed the eight baths: Yuzawa, Wakatake, Hakoyu, Daimon, Hachiman no Yu and Fujiya. Some baths share a supply tank.
- **Spring types**: four baths are listed as "simple spring" and four as "alkaline hypotonic spring", the association’s wording.
- **Source temperatures**: 48.8°C to 62.0°C.
- **pH**: 8.4 to 8.6.
- **Analysed**: between 1996 and 2010.

The analyses are old, and the table has a few oddities. Daimon no Yu and Totsuna-yu share the Daimon tank with the same temperature and pH, but are dated April and November 1996. Dōsen no Yu and Senki no Yu share the Wakatake tank and temperature but differ in pH and year. These may be copying slips on the page, so this page does not quote bath-by-bath figures. These are also figures for the baths’ supplies, not for Iizaka as a whole; official sources do not give the total number of sources or total flow for the spa.

### The heat ranking

Fukushima city says, on a page updated 21 January 2026, that the eight public baths are supplied with water at about 50°C to 70°C at the tap. It also publishes a *atsusa banzuke*, a heat ranking laid out like a sumo table, measured at the outlet of each bath’s tap. For the **2025–26 winter**:

| Bath | Managed by | Water at the tap |
|---|---|---|
| Hakoyu | Fukushima city | 52.6°C |
| Dōsen no Yu | Fukushima city | 52.7°C |
| Sabako-yu | Fukushima city | 53.5°C |
| Senki no Yu | Fukushima city | 63.9°C |
| Tennōji Anahara-yu | Iizaka-machi property ward | 58.2°C |
| Totsuna-yu | Iizaka-machi property ward | 67.8°C |
| Daimon no Yu | Iizaka-machi property ward | 72.3°C |
| Hachiman no Yu | Iizaka-machi property ward | 77.0°C |

The top of the ranking, 77.0°C, is above the city’s round "50 to 70". The ranking is the more specific and newer figure. It compares temperature, not quality. It also measures something different from the association’s source temperatures, which is why Sabako-yu shows 53.5°C here and 51.0°C there.

The temperature in the bath is lower and is posted at each bathhouse. The operating company’s site rates the baths with stars and gives, without a date, about 44°C at Sabako-yu and, at Hakoyu and Dōsen no Yu, a cooler tub of about 43°C to 44°C and a hotter one of about 45°C.

### Where the heat comes from

Official sources do not say where Iizaka’s heat comes from. What they describe is the setting: the Surikami river dividing the town, and Daimon no Yu looking out over the Fukushima basin.

### Not a health resort

Iizaka does not have a plan as a national health resort hot spring on the environment ministry’s list.

## Where it came from

### The Sabako legend

The tourism association’s history page says Iizaka’s history goes back to the Jōmon period. Around the **second century**, it says, the legendary prince Yamato Takeru fell ill on his campaign east and recovered at once after bathing in the "Sabako-yu"; the name Sabako seems to have taken hold from a poem in the anthology *Shūi wakashū*. It also gives the legend of Tōta-yu, linked to the warrior Tawara Tōta, with a timeline of years from 1125 to 1992 that it presents as tradition.

The age is told differently by each source:

- The association’s Japanese history page: around the second century.
- The association’s English site: "believed to have been discovered about 2,000 years ago".
- The Fukushima prefectural tourism body’s English page: more than 1,000 years.
- The operating company: over 1,200 years, for Hakoyu.

This page does not choose between them.

### Bashō and the travellers

The association says Iizaka became widely known from around the Kyōhō era in the mid-Edo period, when better roads brought travellers. The poet Bashō, in *Oku no hosomichi*, wrote the place as "Iizuka". He stayed in a lodging like a fisherman’s hut, says the association, and so wrote nothing kind about it. At the time Iizaka had four inns, 326 people and 74 households. The association adds that the poet Masaoka Shiki and the poet Yosano Akiko came, and that Helen Keller stayed in 1937 and visited twice.

On its page about Sabako-yu, the association calls it the oldest bath in Iizaka and says Bashō **is said to have bathed** there; the operating company says Sabako-yu is where Bashō is said to have stopped in the Genroku era. The history page talks about where he slept, the bath page about where he bathed; neither is presented as certain.

### Sabako-yu’s building

Fukushima city says Sabako-yu has long been loved as Japan’s oldest wooden public bathhouse. The tourism association says the same, then adds that it was rebuilt in **1993** as a re-creation of the Meiji-era bath, with a granite tub. The association’s history page gives the restoration as **1992**, and says the 1889 building had stood in a small park 30 m away. So the building you see is a re-creation, by the association’s own account; the year differs between its two pages.

### The other baths

The association gives:

- **Hakoyu**: about 1,200 years of history; rebuilt and reopened in January 2011.
- **Senki no Yu**: found in 1618, moved in 1967.
- **Tennōji Anahara-yu**: Tennōji-yu (1805) and Anahara-yu (opened 1789), moved together in 1986.
- **Dōsen no Yu, Daimon no Yu and Hachiman no Yu**: opened in 1962.
- **Totsuna-yu**: opened in 1968.

### The "three old springs"

A Sendai city page describing [Akiu](/en/soak/akiu/) calls it one of the three old springs of Ōshū, with [Naruko](/en/soak/naruko/) and Iizaka. Iizaka’s own official pages do not use that title. The prefectural tourism body’s English page says locals take pride in the phrase "Beppu in the West; Iizaka in the East". Neither names any body that chose these groupings.

## How the town works

### Eight, not nine

The eight shared baths are Sabako-yu, Hakoyu, Dōsen no Yu, Senki no Yu, Hachiman no Yu, Daimon no Yu, Totsuna-yu and Tennōji Anahara-yu. A ninth, **Kiri-yu**, closed on **14 November 2024**, according to the tourism association. Older counts of nine date from before that. The association’s English site still says "9 Public Baths and 3 Footbaths", lists Kiri-yu and shows fees that look like the old ones.

Fukushima city manages some baths and the Iizaka-machi property ward the others (see the table above).

### Hours and fees

- **Hours.** Each bath has its own hours and closing day, and some split the day into morning (6:00 to 13:00) and afternoon (13:00 to 21:00) sessions that swap by weekday. Sabako-yu, Hakoyu and Dōsen no Yu open 6:00 to 21:00 (last entry 20:40), each closing on one weekday. Totsuna-yu’s sessions changed from 1 August 2025. The English site’s "6:00 to 22:00 for all baths" does not match the Japanese pages.
- **Fees.** The association says bath fees were revised on 1 February 2025. The city, the association and the company all list fees, but none says whether they include tax, so they are not given here. The company mentions coupon books and reductions for disabled people (free for Fukushima city residents), and announced free bathing on Respect for the Aged Day in a notice of 17 September 2026.
- **What to bring.** Soap and shampoo are not provided but are sold at each bath. Only Hakoyu has showers. Coin lockers are free (the coin comes back). Child fares apply from age 1 to under 12.
- **Closures.** The company posts frequent temporary closures: in 2026, for example, the women’s side of Senki no Yu reopened on 18 June and closed again on 15 August, with notices for Tennōji Anahara-yu (21 June) and Sabako-yu (14 July). Check its notices before you go.

### Footbaths

The association’s Japanese page lists four official footbaths, including the hand-and-foot bath at the former Horikiri residence (9:00 to 21:00, free) and the one in Hakoyu park (6:00 to 21:00, free). The company and the English site say three.

## Bathing there

### How to use a shared bath

The operating company’s Japanese page "How to use the shared baths" is the only official bathing guide, and it is not in English:

- Wash before you get in, and pour water over yourself first (*kake-yu*).
- Keep your towel out of the tub.
- The baths are free-flowing from the source and hot. If you add cold water, think of the people around you.
- No smoking, hair dyeing or photography inside.
- Take care with sudden temperature changes.

### Tattoos and English

Official sources do not give a tattoo rule for Iizaka, in Japanese or English. More in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask at each bath or inn in advance.

### Compared with city baths

No official Iizaka source compares its water with baths elsewhere. The operating company describes the shared baths as having long served people in Iizaka as their own home baths, and the association reminds visitors to bring shampoo and soap because there is none. For what "free-flowing" means on a notice, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Food

**Radium tamago.** Fukushima city’s *rajiumu tamago*, a hot-spring egg, is a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2022, modern). The agency says it started in Iizaka, where radium was first confirmed in Japan; that many shops use the shared source; that inns have always served it at breakfast; and that Fukushima people say "radium tamago", not *onsen tamago*. The tourism association says the eggs are set in source water of 63°C to 70°C for half an hour to an hour. It credits Manabe Kaichirō, a medical student at Tokyo Imperial University, with first confirming radium in a Japanese hot spring here and presenting it to an academic society, after which the eggs were sold under the radium name. The "first in Japan" is the agency’s and the association’s statement. "Radium" here is a customary name, not the spring type; the association’s analyses call the water simple or alkaline and give no radon figure.

**Ika-ninjin.** The farm ministry gives Fukushima city as where it is handed down, says it has been eaten for more than 100 years as a winter preserve and is a must at New Year. The Cultural Agency, which made it a 100-year food (fiscal 2021, traditional), says it has come down in northern Fukushima since the late Edo period. The tourism association’s food page lists it as a local dish of the Fukushima area. The ministry does not mention Iizaka by name.

**Enban gyōza.** Not in the farm ministry’s list; a Cultural Agency 100-year food (fiscal 2021, future). The association lists eight shops, and shops for Iizaka ramen, which neither the ministry nor the agency lists.

**Peaches and sake.** The farm ministry counts peaches among Fukushima’s leading produce, and the association advertises a peach festival it calls "Japan’s number one", peach parfaits and fruit picking. "Number one" is the association’s event name; the ministry treats peaches as produce, not as a local dish. The association also features a sake named after the Surikami river from Kinsuishō, which it calls Fukushima city’s only brewery.

Prices are not given. More in the [Tōhoku local food guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-tohoku/). Aizu’s *kozuyu* is a Fukushima dish but not an Iizaka one; the ministry places it in Aizu.

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Fukushima station.** By the Fukushima Kōtsū Iizaka line to **Iizaka-Onsen** station: about 25 minutes by the association’s Japanese page and the company, about 20 minutes by the association’s English site and the prefectural tourism body.
- **By car.** From the Fukushima-Iizaka interchange of the Tōhoku expressway, take route 13 towards Yonezawa, then prefectural road 3 for about 2 km (the association). The prefectural tourism body’s English page says about 10 minutes from the interchange.
- **From Tokyo.** Take the Tōhoku shinkansen to Fukushima. Official pages here do not give a clear journey time.

The times above are the sources’ round figures, not a timetable. For other Tōhoku spa towns, see [Higashiyama](/en/soak/higashiyama/) in Aizu, [Akiu](/en/soak/akiu/) near Sendai, [Zao](/en/soak/zao/) and [Ginzan](/en/soak/ginzan/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Iizaka has nine public baths."** It has eight since Kiri-yu closed on 14 November 2024. The association’s English site is out of date.
- **"It is a radium spring."** "Radium" is a customary name. The association’s analyses say simple or alkaline, and no official type is called radium.
- **"Sabako-yu is the original old building."** The association says it was rebuilt as a re-creation in 1993 (or 1992, on its history page).
- **"Bashō praised Iizaka."** The association says he wrote nothing kind about his lodging.
- **"The water is about 42°C."** That is the English site. The city’s ranking gives 52.6°C to 77.0°C at the tap.
- **"Kozuyu is Iizaka’s dish."** The farm ministry places it in Aizu.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so.
