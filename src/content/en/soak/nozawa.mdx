Nozawa Onsen, a village in Nagano, is organised around thirteen small bathhouses that belong to the village, not to any inn. They are looked after by groups of villagers called *yu-nakama*, and visitors use them in exchange for a voluntary donation. In the village there is also a spring that boils in the open air, where people cook. This page explains how it works using the village office, the village’s tourism bureau, the ski resort’s English site and JNTO. Where they differ, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what the spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for what "free-flowing" and "no added water" mean on a notice, see the piece on *kakenagashi*. The Honshu hot springs guide covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

### The legend

The tourism bureau (the Nozawa Onsen Mountain Resort Tourism Bureau, which runs the official tourism site) gives one founding legend: in the reign of Emperor Shōmu, the monk Gyōki and a *yamabushi*, a mountain ascetic, found the spring. It gives no other account and no year, and the village’s, JNTO’s and the ski resort’s pages give none either.

### The record

The village office’s profile gives what is on record, separately from the legend:

- The name "Yuyama village" appears in 1272 (the ninth year of Bun’ei).
- By the early Edo period there were already said to be 24 inns.
- A record from Meiji 3 to 5 (1870–72) counts 24,863 *tōji* visitors, people staying for days or weeks for the water.

It describes the climate as that of the Sea of Japan side: an average temperature of 9.5°C, 2,022 mm of precipitation a year, and a maximum daily snowfall of 107 cm (in 2010), which it calls among the heaviest snowfall in Japan.

### The yu-nakama

The tourism bureau says that under the *yu-nakama* system, which has continued since the Edo period, the village baths have been kept as the village’s shared property. The villagers maintain and manage them through the *yu-nakama*. Its FAQ adds that each *sotoyu* enshrines one of the Twelve Heavenly Generals. JNTO says they are maintained by neighbourhood organisations and that each is dedicated to a different bodhisattva. Official sources do not say how the Ogama is managed, beyond the village’s list giving its owner as the Nozawa-gumi.

### Designations

The village’s list of designated cultural properties (as of 6 August 2019, the most recent edition available) has one national entry: the Dōsojin fire festival, held every 15 January, designated an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property in 1993. It also lists the lantern procession at Yuzawa shrine on 8 September as a village-designated intangible folk cultural property.

Nozawa is not on the environment ministry’s list of national health resort onsen areas (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*); the five Nagano entries are elsewhere.

## The water

The tourism bureau says there are more than 30 sources in the village and 13 *sotoyu*, outside baths, dotted around it. It gives no survey year. Neither the village nor the environment ministry, whose figures are by prefecture, gives an official total flow or count of sources, and JNTO’s page gives no count.

**The spring types of the *sotoyu*.** The bureau’s hot spring page gives a spring name and temperature for each bath, with no year of analysis. The baths fall under two spring names:

- Simple sulphur spring: Ōyu (weakly alkaline, 66°C), Kawarayu (about 60°C), Akiba no Yu (about 90°C), Kumanotearai-yu (about 40°C), Matsuba no Yu (about 90°C), Shinyu (about 55°C) and Jūōdō no Yu (about 90°C).
- Sulphur-bearing sodium-calcium sulphate spring: Ogama no Yu, Kamitera-yu, Nakao no Yu, Shinden no Yu and Yokochi no Yu (each about 90°C), and Taki no Yu (about 78°C).

The bureau does not say whether these are temperatures at the source or in the bath. The ski resort’s English site gives the source water as 40°C to 90°C and the bath water as usually 45°C to 49°C. The water types guide explains what the two spring names mean.

### The Ogama

The bureau’s "About Nozawa Onsen" page gives the source as about 90°C, flowing at about 500 litres a minute, with no survey year, and made up of five pools: Ōgama, Marugama, Yudegama, Takenoshigama and Shimogama. Villagers boil vegetables and eggs in it, and because it is also a place where people gather, it is called "the village’s shared kitchen". JNTO says locals boil eggs in the square near Ōyu, that the water is a constant 90°C, and suggests going early in the morning. The bureau says the source is off limits to anyone but villagers, and the ski resort’s English site says to watch from a distance.

**What kind of designation.** The bureau calls the Ogama a natural monument designated **by the village**. The village’s own list of cultural properties gives it as a village-designated **place of scenic beauty** (*meishō*), designated on 1 November 1979, owned by the Nozawa-gumi. The village-designated natural monuments on that list are three trees, not the Ogama. So the two official sources agree it is a village designation and differ on the category. This site does not choose. Neither is national.

## Why the water is here

The bureau says most of the village’s land was formed by material from the Kenashi volcano, and that volcanic activity and faults produce the hot springs. The national geological survey (AIST) lists Mount Kenashi as a composite andesite volcano active roughly 1.6 to 1.0 million years ago, not an active volcano, with no recorded eruptions. So the volcano and its age are on record, but no public source says the heat of Nozawa’s springs comes from it.

## How the village works

### The thirteen sotoyu

The tourism bureau lists them as Ōyu, Kawarayu, Akiba no Yu, Ogama no Yu, Kamitera-yu, Kumanotearai-yu, Matsuba no Yu, Nakao no Yu, Shinden no Yu, Shinyu, Taki no Yu, Yokochi no Yu and Jūōdō no Yu. Its *sotoyu* map shows walks of about one to eight minutes between neighbouring baths.

The bureau describes them as natural hot spring water, flowing straight through (*kakenagashi*), with no water added. That is the bureau’s own description of its baths.

**Akiba no Yu is closed.** The bureau announced on 19 May 2026 that Akiba no Yu cannot be used from 11 May to 30 October 2026 because of demolition work, with the dates given as planned. Its stamp stand has moved to the tourist information office. The reopening date is not confirmed.

**Hours.** The two main sources differ, so I give both, as checked on 11 October 2026:

- **The bureau (Japanese).** Most baths: 5:00 to 23:00 from April to November, 6:00 to 23:00 from December to March. Taki no Yu: 5:00 to 21:00 and 6:00 to 22:00. Shinyu and Jūōdō no Yu: 6:00 to 22:00; Yokochi no Yu: 6:00 to 23:00, with no seasonal difference given.
- **The ski resort’s English site.** Open all year, 5:00 to 22:00 from April to November and 6:00 to 23:00 from December to March, with Shinden no Yu and Taki no Yu closing at 21:00.

Neither is dated. Check the notice at the bath. The bureau says late-night bathing is not allowed for security and other reasons, and gives no cleaning times or closing days.

### Paying: a donation, not a fee

The bureau’s FAQ says the thirteen shared baths are managed by the villagers’ *yu-nakama* and that visitors may use them. Payment is *sunshi*, a voluntary contribution to the *yu-nakama*, put in the offering box. It gives no suggested amount. JNTO calls the *sotoyu* free and does not mention a donation. The ski resort’s English site says entry is free, that there is a donation box at each entrance, and asks for "a few coins" towards upkeep, without a figure. The bureau is the one that asks; none of them names an amount.

### Laundry baths and egg pools

Some baths have a separate pool for washing clothes. The bureau’s hot spring page says three do — Ogama no Yu, Matsuba no Yu and Jūōdō no Yu — and that the one at Ogama no Yu is still used by the *yu-nakama*; Matsuba no Yu has its laundry bath on the ground floor and the bath upstairs. Another bureau page says six of the thirteen have one. Both are the bureau; I do not pick a number.

Some baths and squares also have pools for boiling eggs. The bureau’s hot spring page and the ski resort’s English site name the same five: Kamitera-yu, Kumanotearai-yu, Matsuba no Yu and Jūōdō no Yu, plus the small hot spring square Yurari, which is not a *sotoyu* and is closed in winter. That makes four *sotoyu* and one square. Another bureau page says four, without explaining the difference.

### Furusato no Yu

Separate from the *sotoyu*, the village runs a day bathhouse, Furusato no Yu, in Ogama Onsen Park, with indoor baths (hot and lukewarm) and an outdoor bath; the bureau says it was renovated in 2024. The village page, as checked on 11 October 2026, gives its hours as 10:00 to 19:00 (last entry 18:30) from April to mid-December and 11:00 to 20:00 (last entry 19:30) from mid-December to March, closed on Thursdays unless a public holiday, with no car park. Its fee is not given here, because the village page does not say whether it includes tax.

### The stamp walk

The bureau runs a *shūin meguri*, a stamp walk, with 27 stamp points around the village — not only baths but shrines, monuments and roadside guardian stones. Collecting ten or more, and twenty or more, earns a commemorative gift. The stamp book costs 550 yen including tax (as checked on 10 October 2026) at the tourist information office and souvenir shops. In snow the stamp stands can be buried. The bureau does not say how many of the 27 are baths.

## Bathing there

- **The bureau’s rules for the *sotoyu*.** Wash before getting in; do not put towels in the bath; once the bath is cool enough to enter, turn off the cold tap; no bathing late at night; and leave a contribution for the *yu-nakama*. The bureau asks visitors to watch its etiquette video before using the baths.
- **The ski resort’s English site** gives its own list: sit down and wash before getting in, no swimsuits, bring your own towel and soap, and pour water over yourself before getting in, especially when it is very hot. It says you may cool the bath with cold water if you check that the other bathers agree. That reads differently from the bureau’s "turn off the tap once you can get in"; I give both and do not choose. Its other pages add: no jumping in, keep towels and hair out of the water, and dry off before going back to the changing area.
- **No showers or dryers.** The bureau’s FAQ says the *sotoyu* have neither. It also says pets cannot use the village’s hot spring facilities.
- **Heat.** The bureau says the baths are basically hot because no water is added. JNTO says they are very hot and to get in carefully.
- **Village life.** The village office’s English "Welcome Nozawa" page asks visitors not to smoke while walking or drop litter, to keep quiet at night, and to put rubbish out in the designated bags marked with their lodging and name. It notes that morning buses are crowded. It links to the bathing etiquette video; the page itself has no written bathing rules.
- **Language.** The "ENG" link on the tourism bureau’s site goes to the English site of the ski resort, not to an English version of the bureau’s own pages. Official pages do not say who runs that site or whether the baths have signs in English.
- **Tattoos.** The bureau’s pages, the village’s English page, JNTO and the ski resort’s English site say nothing on tattoos. The bureau’s FAQ has no entry on them and points visitors to the bureau. Ask each inn or bath in advance, and consider a private bath where there is one.
- **Winter.** No official source says the *sotoyu* close in winter; the bureau gives winter hours, and the ski resort’s site says they are open all year. The bureau warns that stamp stands can be buried in snow and that the area around Kenmeiji temple is sometimes closed because of snow falling from roofs. Car park hours and availability change with the season.

## What it is eaten with

*Nozawana* is the one food that both the farm ministry and the village side tie to Nozawa Onsen.

- **The farm ministry** lists *nozawana-zuke*, pickled *nozawana*, among Nagano’s local dishes, made across the prefecture, with Nozawa Onsen village given as its birthplace. It says a priest of the village is said to have brought back Tennōji turnip from Kyoto in 1756 (the sixth year of Hōreki) and planted it; at about 600 m the plant changed, its root failed to swell and its leaves and stems grew large, and it spread under the village’s name. It is the only one of the ministry’s Nagano entries with "Nozawa" in its name.
- **The tourism bureau’s page for Kenmeiji** says the temple’s eighth priest brought the turnip seeds back from study in Kyoto in the Hōreki era (1751–1763), and that a monument marking the birthplace of *nozawana*, along with a second monument, stands before its gate. The temple is about 200 m up the slope in front of Ōyu. It gives no other origin.

The ministry gives a year, the bureau an era; they do not contradict each other. The bureau says villagers boil vegetables in the Ogama, but no official page says *nozawana* is washed or boiled there, so this page does not say it.

**Eggs.** The ski resort’s English site says villagers use the Ogama to wash things, soak vines and boil vegetables and eggs, that an onsen egg takes about 20 minutes, and that because the Ogama is for villagers only, the souvenir shop next to it sells eggs already cooked.

**Oyaki.** The farm ministry lists *oyaki*, filled buns of wheat or buckwheat dough that are baked or steamed, as a Nagano dish, but does not mention Nozawa. JNTO says roadside shops in Nozawa sell them. So the link to Nozawa comes from JNTO only.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, because none of the official pages says whether they include tax.

- **Rail and bus.** The tourism bureau says the nearest station is Iiyama on the Hokuriku shinkansen, and the direct bus, the Nozawa Onsen Liner, takes about 25 minutes from there, depending on the time and the stop. JNTO gives about 1 hour 40 minutes from Tokyo to Iiyama by shinkansen; this page has not checked it against JR East’s own timetable. The bureau says there is no public transport to the village from Kamisakai or Togari-Nozawaonsen stations on the Iiyama line.
- **The Liner.** The village’s page says it is run by Nozawa Onsen Kōtsū and leaves from stop 4 outside JR Iiyama station. Tickets are sold only at Iiyama station and in Nozawa Onsen; on the bus, IC cards and cash are accepted but not credit cards. Both the village and the bureau say the bus does not always connect with the shinkansen. The ski resort’s English site says to use the Chikuma River exit and the ticket machine by the stop.
- **Timetables and winter.** The village gives the winter timetable as 13 December 2025 to 29 March 2026 and the summer one as 30 March to 30 November 2026 (planned). As of 11 October 2026 no timetable for the coming winter was available. The bureau has announced a "Nozawa Onsen Rideshare" planned from 11 December 2026 to 25 March 2027, 8:00 to 22:00, between the Iiyama station area and the village, for trips that start or end in the village. The ski resort’s site says Uber was available in the 2025–26 winter.
- **From the airports in winter.** JNTO says the Nagano Snow Shuttle runs direct from Narita and Haneda in about six hours, only in the ski season from December to March, and that there are winter direct buses from Osaka, Kyoto and Nagoya.

## What people get wrong

- **"The village baths are free."** JNTO and the ski resort’s site say entry is free; the tourism bureau asks for a voluntary contribution to the *yu-nakama* who run them. No one gives an amount.
- **"The baths belong to the inns."** The *sotoyu* are village property kept by the *yu-nakama*, by the bureau’s account.
- **"The Ogama is a national natural monument."** It is a village designation: a natural monument according to the bureau, a place of scenic beauty according to the village’s own list.
- **"All thirteen are open."** Akiba no Yu was closed for demolition work from 11 May to 30 October 2026 (planned), and its reopening is not confirmed.
- **"Nozawana is washed in the Ogama."** No official source says so. What is confirmed is that the village is given as the plant’s birthplace.
- **"There are four egg pools," or five.** The bureau says both; its hot spring page names four *sotoyu* and one square.
- **"Nozawa is a national health resort onsen."** It is not on the environment ministry’s list.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
