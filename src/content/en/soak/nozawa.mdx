Nozawa Onsen, a village in Nagano, is organised around thirteen small bathhouses that belong to the village, not to any inn. They are looked after by groups of villagers called *yu-nakama*, and visitors use them in exchange for a voluntary donation. In the village there is also a spring that boils in the open air, where people cook. This page explains how it works using the village office, the village’s tourism bureau and JNTO. Where they differ, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what the spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for what "free-flowing" and "no added water" mean on a notice, see the piece on *kakenagashi*. The Honshu hot springs guide covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

### The legend

The tourism bureau (the Nozawa Onsen Mountain Resort Tourism Bureau, which runs the official tourism site) gives one founding legend: in the reign of Emperor Shōmu, the monk Gyōki and a *yamabushi*, a mountain ascetic, found the spring. It gives no other account and no year, and I have not added one.

### The record

The village office’s profile gives what is on record, separately from the legend:

- The name "Yuyama village" appears in 1272 (the ninth year of Bun’ei).
- By the early Edo period there were already said to be 24 inns.
- A record from Meiji 3 to 5 (1870–72) counts 24,863 *tōji* visitors, people staying for days or weeks for the water.

It describes the climate as that of the Sea of Japan side: an average temperature of 9.5°C, 2,022 mm of precipitation a year, and a maximum daily snowfall of 107 cm (in 2010), which it calls among the heaviest snowfall in Japan.

### The yu-nakama

The tourism bureau says that under the *yu-nakama* system, which has continued since the Edo period, the village baths have been kept as the village’s shared property. The villagers maintain and manage them through the *yu-nakama*. JNTO says they are maintained by neighbourhood organisations and that each is dedicated to a different bodhisattva.

## The water

The tourism bureau says there are more than 30 sources in the village and 13 *sotoyu*, outside baths, dotted around it. It gives no survey year. I could not find an official total flow or a list of spring types for the village, and JNTO’s page gives no count.

### The Ogama

The bureau’s "About Nozawa Onsen" page calls the Ogama a naturally boiling pool designated a natural monument **by the village**. It gives the source as about 90°C, flowing at about 500 litres a minute, and made up of five pools: Ōgama, Marugama, Yudegama, Takenoshigama and Shimogama. Villagers boil vegetables and eggs in it, and because it is also a place where people gather, it is called "the village’s shared kitchen". JNTO says locals boil eggs in the square near Ōyu, that the water is a constant 90°C, and suggests going early in the morning.

You may see it called a national natural monument. The bureau says village, and I found no entry for it in the national cultural heritage database, so I say village.

### Why the water is here

The bureau says most of the village’s land was formed by eruptions of Mount Kenashi, and that volcanic activity and faults produce the hot springs. That is the bureau’s explanation; I could not confirm it in material from the weather agency, the national geological survey or a university. Mount Kenashi is not on the weather agency’s list of active volcanoes.

## How the village works

### The thirteen sotoyu

The tourism bureau lists them as Ōyu, Kawarayu, Akiba no Yu, Ogama no Yu, Kamitera-yu, Kumanotearai-yu, Matsuba no Yu, Nakao no Yu, Shinden no Yu, Shinyu, Taki no Yu, Yokochi no Yu and Jūōdō no Yu. Its *sotoyu* map shows walks of about one to eight minutes between neighbouring baths.

The bureau describes them as natural hot spring water, flowing straight through (*kakenagashi*), with no water added. That is the bureau’s own description of its baths.

### Paying: a donation, not a fee

The bureau’s FAQ says the thirteen shared baths are managed by the villagers’ *yu-nakama* and that visitors may use them. Payment is *sunshi*, a voluntary contribution put in the offering box. It gives no suggested amount. JNTO calls the *sotoyu* free and does not mention a donation. The two are describing the same thing from different sides; the bureau is the one that asks.

### Laundry baths and egg pools

Some baths have a separate pool for washing clothes. The bureau says the laundry bath at Ogama no Yu is still used by the *yu-nakama*, and that Matsuba no Yu has its laundry bath on the ground floor and the bath upstairs. Another bureau page says six of the thirteen have one; I could not find which six.

Some baths and squares also have pools for boiling eggs. The bureau gives the number as four on some pages and five on another (naming Yurari, Matsuba no Yu and Jūōdō no Yu among them). I do not pick a number.

### Furusato no Yu

Separate from the *sotoyu*, the village runs a day bathhouse, Furusato no Yu, in Ogama Onsen Park, with indoor baths (hot and lukewarm) and an outdoor bath; the bureau says it was renovated in 2024. I have not given its fee, because the village page does not say whether it includes tax.

### The stamp walk

The bureau runs a *shūin meguri*, a stamp walk, with 27 stamp points around the village — not only baths but shrines, monuments and roadside guardian stones. Collecting ten or more, and twenty or more, earns a commemorative gift. The stamp book costs 550 yen including tax (as checked on 10 October 2026) at the tourist information office and souvenir shops. In snow the stamp stands can be buried. I could not find how many of the 27 are baths.

## Bathing there

- **The bureau’s rules for the *sotoyu*.** Wash before getting in; do not put towels in the bath; turn off the tap; no bathing late at night; and leave a contribution for the *yu-nakama*. The bureau asks visitors to watch its etiquette video before using the baths. I have not watched the video.
- **Heat.** The bureau says the baths are basically hot because no water is added. JNTO says they are very hot and to get in carefully.
- **Village life.** The village office’s English "Welcome Nozawa" page asks visitors not to smoke while walking or drop litter, to keep quiet at night, and to put rubbish out in the designated bags marked with their lodging and name. It notes that morning buses are crowded. It links to the bathing etiquette video.
- **Language.** The "ENG" link on the tourism bureau’s site goes to the English site of the ski resort, not to an English version of the bureau’s own pages.
- **Tattoos.** I found nothing on tattoos in the bureau’s FAQ or on JNTO’s page. Ask each inn or bath in advance, and consider a private bath where there is one.
- **Winter.** I found no statement that the *sotoyu* close in winter. The bureau warns that stamp stands can be buried in snow and that the area around Kenmeiji temple is sometimes closed because of snow falling from roofs. Car park hours and availability change with the season.

## What it is eaten with

*Nozawana* is the one food that both the farm ministry and the village side tie to Nozawa Onsen.

- **The farm ministry** lists *nozawana-zuke*, pickled *nozawana*, among Nagano’s local dishes, made across the prefecture, with Nozawa Onsen village given as its birthplace. It says a priest of the village is said to have brought back Tennōji turnip from Kyoto in 1756 (the sixth year of Hōreki) and planted it; at about 600 m the plant changed, its root failed to swell and its leaves and stems grew large, and it spread under the village’s name.
- **The tourism bureau’s page for Kenmeiji** says the temple’s eighth priest brought the turnip seeds back from study in Kyoto in the Hōreki era (1751–1763), and that a monument marking the birthplace of *nozawana* stands before its gate. The temple is about 200 m up the slope in front of Ōyu.

The ministry gives a year, the bureau an era; they do not contradict each other. The bureau says villagers boil vegetables in the Ogama, but I found no official page saying *nozawana* is washed or boiled there, so I do not say it.

## Getting there

As checked on 10 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Rail and bus.** The tourism bureau says the nearest station is Iiyama on the Hokuriku shinkansen, and the direct bus, the Nozawa Onsen Liner, takes about 25 minutes from there. JNTO gives about 1 hour 40 minutes from Tokyo to Iiyama by shinkansen. The bureau says there is no public transport to the village from Kamisakai or Togari-Nozawaonsen stations on the Iiyama line.
- **From the airports in winter.** JNTO says the Nagano Snow Shuttle runs direct from Narita and Haneda in about six hours, only in the ski season from December to March, and that there are winter direct buses from Osaka, Kyoto and Nagoya.
- **Timetables and winter.** The bureau’s access page showed the Liner timetable only for 30 March to 30 November 2026. It has announced a "Nozawa Onsen Rideshare" planned from 11 December 2026 to 25 March 2027, 8:00 to 22:00, between the Iiyama station area and the village, for trips that start or end in the village.

## What people get wrong

- **"The village baths are free."** JNTO says so; the tourism bureau asks for a voluntary contribution to the *yu-nakama* who run them.
- **"The baths belong to the inns."** The *sotoyu* are village property kept by the *yu-nakama*, by the bureau’s account.
- **"The Ogama is a national monument."** The bureau says it is designated by the village.
- **"Nozawana is washed in the Ogama."** I could not find an official source for that. What is confirmed is that the village is given as the plant’s birthplace.
- **"There are four egg pools," or five.** The bureau says both.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
