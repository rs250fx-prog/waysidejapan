Yunomine Onsen is a small spring village in a mountain valley in the Hongū district of Tanabe city, Wakayama, about 4 km from Kumano Hongū Taisha. It is one of the three springs of the Kumano Hongū area, a nationally designated health resort, and it lies on the Kumano pilgrimage, where travellers once bathed to purify themselves. Its best-known bath, **Tsuboyu**, is a small rock pool used by one group at a time. This page uses the environment ministry's plan for the area, Tanabe city, Wakayama prefecture, the Kumano Hongū tourism association, the Tanabe City Kumano Tourism Bureau, research papers, the farm ministry and JNTO. Where they disagree, both are given. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the spring types named below, see [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) and [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). For reading the analysis sign, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### A designated health resort

The environment ministry's plan for the area says the **Kumano Hongū Onsen-kyō** is the collective name for three springs, from north to south Yunomine, Watarase and Kawayu, covering about 106.87 ha inside the Yoshino-Kumano National Park. Yunomine and Kawayu were designated a national health resort hot spring in **September 1957** as "Kumano Yunomine-Kawayu Onsen"; Watarase was added in **March 1985** and the area renamed. The plan was revised in August 2018.

### Fourteen sources

The plan says Yunomine has **14 sources** along the Yunotani river, of which **8 are in constant use**, and most flow out by themselves. Their temperatures run from **53.5°C to 84.3°C**, and the total flow is given as "unmeasurable". It names the water a **sulphur-bearing sodium hydrogen-carbonate and chloride spring**. Its source table, which gives no survey year, includes:

| Source | Temperature | Flow |
|---|---|---|
| Yuzutsu (湯筒) | 84.3°C | 54 L/min, natural flow |
| Tsuboyu (壷湯) | 53.5°C | "rises from the valley floor", natural flow |
| Kan-yu (環湯) | 84.3°C | 203 L/min |
| Moto-yu and Mine-no-yu, mixed | 72.5°C | — |

Temperature and flow are checked on site once a year.

### One spring, many temperatures

The published temperatures depend on where and when they were taken. The ministry's plan gives 53.5 to 84.3°C across the sources. The tourism association gives 92°C on its spring sheet, 93 degrees on its Oguri Hangan page and 90 degrees for the yuzutsu; Tanabe city and the prefecture say about 90°C for the yuzutsu; research papers cite 92.5°C.

The name also varies. The tourism association writes "sulphur-bearing sodium hydrogen-carbonate" without chloride. Wakayama's Red Data Book calls it a sodium bicarbonate spring, says Yunomine contains a lot of hydrogen sulphide, and says the Yunomine and Kawayu springs flow naturally.

### Where the heat comes from

Researchers note there is no young volcano near Yunomine, and they do not agree on the heat source. These are the main accounts, each the view of its authors:

- **AIST, Muraoka (2008).** The heat comes from the high heat flow caused by the subduction of the Shikoku Basin's spreading axis, and the hot water is held in quartz porphyry intrusions of about 14.6 and 14.4 million years ago, more permeable than the rock around them, where it can circulate from deep down. The same paper says that, on the ocean side of Japan's volcanic front, boiling springs of 90°C or more are found only at **Arima and Yunomine**. It does not say the two share an origin.
- **The Japan Nuclear Cycle Development Institute (2004)**, predecessor of the Japan Atomic Energy Agency. The heat of the high-temperature springs of southern Kii, including Yunomine and Shirahama, is thought to come not from magma but from heat carried up by **deep fluids**. A 2004 Japan Geoscience Union abstract also suggests hot fluids released from the subducting slab.
- **Hanamuro and others (2008 and 2007 abstracts).** With no young volcano nearby, they link the high-temperature activity of around 14 million years ago to the large Kumano acidic igneous episode, and the lower-temperature activity of about 5 million years ago to the present to rainwater heated by deep fluids.
- **Wakayama's Red Data Book.** The springs rise along rhyolite (quartz porphyry) dykes, and are thought to be related to the Kumano igneous activity.
- **A 2003 abstract (Tsunoda and others).** A deep fracture system is likely involved.

Much remains unsettled. This page does not choose between them.

## Where it came from

### "About 1,800 years" and "the oldest"

The ministry's plan says Yunomine **is said to have been** found about 1,800 years ago, in the reign of Emperor Seimu, by Ōato no Sukune, governor of Kumano, and **is said to be** Japan's oldest hot spring; it notes Seimu's reign as 131 to 191. Tanabe city and the tourism association say much the same, "about 1,800 years" and "said to be the oldest". The association's same page also dates the discovery to around the 4th century, which does not match the plan's second-century dates. Both are given here.

"Oldest" is what these bodies say is said; none names anyone who chose it. Tanabe city's pamphlet adds that the Edo-period rankings of hot springs listed Yunomine as *kanjin-moto*, the sponsor, a place set apart from the ranked springs.

### The name

Tanabe city's pamphlet says hot water once rose from the chest of the Yakushi Nyorai statue at Tōkō-ji, made from fossilised spring deposit, so the place was called *yu no mune*, "hot-water chest", which became Yunomine. The ministry's plan gives the same origin.

### A purification bath for pilgrims

The plan calls Yunomine a place of *yugori*, purification bathing before visiting Kumano, since long ago. It lists the Yunobori ritual of the April grand festival at Kumano Hongū Taisha, the Yunomine Yōka Yakushi festival in January, and a festival in October at which the first water of the inns is offered to Hongū Taisha. The tourism association says that at the Yunobori ritual on 13 April, priests and children bathe and eat rice gruel cooked in the spring.

### Oguri Hangan

The tourism association gives two tellings:

- **As history**, following the *Kamakura Ōzōshi*: Oguri Sukeshige of Hitachi, defeated with his clan in 1415, was poisoned in Sagami, saved by Princess Terute, led by a wandering monk to Kumano, and recovered in Yunomine's water.
- **As a tale**, in the *sekkyō-bushi* tradition: Oguri, sent back from hell in the form of a starving ghost, was pulled to Yunomine on a cart by Terute and returned to his own form after bathing in Tsuboyu for 49 days.

The page suggests history later became legend. Tanabe city's pamphlet says the story became a subject for puppet theatre and kabuki.

### Ippen

The association says the monk Ippen, founder of the Ji school, received an oracle at Kumano in the late 13th century, and that a stone at Yunomine bears his invocation, said to be scratched with his fingernail; Tanabe city calls it a prefectural historic site.

### World Heritage: the exact wording

Wakayama's World Heritage Centre says the **Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range**, across Mie, Nara and Wakayama, were entered on the list on 7 July 2004. Its inventory of components lists, among the Kumano pilgrimage route's parts, **"Yunomine Onsen" (湯峯温泉)**: a site and landscape, "prehistoric", a place of *yugori* near Kumano Hongū Taisha.

Tanabe city says Tsuboyu was registered in July 2004 as part of the pilgrimage route, a hot spring you can bathe in. The tourism association says the same. Three bodies go further. The prefecture's yuzutsu page calls Tsuboyu the only bath in the world on the World Heritage List; JNTO calls it "the only UNESCO World Heritage-listed hot spring in the world"; and the association's English page calls it "the world's first public hot bath in World Heritage sites". Those are their descriptions.

## How the village works

### The village

The plan describes Yunomine as a spring village at 100 to 120 m above sea level, surrounded on all sides by mountains. It notes more young and foreign visitors at Tsuboyu in recent years. There is one car park, at the southern entrance; camping and barbecues are not allowed (tourism association and tourism bureau).

### The public bathhouse

The public bathhouse has a general bath (hotter and cooler), a medicinal bath (*kusuri-yu*) and a family bath, and runs Tsuboyu. A tap (*kumitori-ba*) lets you take spring water home, 6:00 to 21:00. It is run by Tanabe city's Yotsumuragawa property ward.

- **Hours.** 6:00 to 21:00, last entry 20:30 (tourism association and Tanabe city).
- **Closing days.** Tanabe city says it is open every day, with temporary closures in summer and winter to clean the sources; the association's Japanese page says closures are irregular, for cleaning. Its English page, updated November 2022, says "None".
- **As of 13 October 2026**, the association said Tsuboyu, the bathhouse, the yuzutsu and the water tap were open as usual, and that **all public baths, Tsuboyu included, close on Monday 7 December for scheduled cleaning** (8 December if bad weather prevents it).
- **Paying.** Tanabe city says the ticket machine takes QR-code payments.
- **Prices.** The association, the city's pamphlet and the tourism bureau publish fees without saying whether they include tax, so they are not given here. The association says some fees changed with the renewal opening on 23 April 2022.

### Tsuboyu

Tsuboyu is a natural rock bath enclosed by boards. The tourism association says it holds 2 to 3 people, the tourism bureau 1 to 2. Each group has it for **30 minutes**. It is **said to** change colour up to seven times a day.

The tourism bureau's English page explains the steps:

1. Buy a ticket at the machine.
2. Exchange it at reception for a **numbered tag**, then wait your turn.
3. When your number comes up, hang the tag on the hook by the door, leave your shoes outside, put your clothes in the basket and **lock the door from inside**.
4. Rinse before you get in. If it is too hot, add water from the **green tap** and stir with the paddle.

**No swimsuits, soap or shampoo.** There are **no reservations**; it is first come, first served, as JNTO also says. The association adds that a Tsuboyu ticket includes one entry to the general bath or the medicinal bath.

### The yuzutsu

The yuzutsu is a spring well beside the river. The tourism association says you can boil eggs and vegetables in its 90-degree water, 6:00 to 21:00, with eggs and vegetables on sale at the shops. Tanabe city's pamphlet gives about 10 to 13 minutes for eggs and about 30 for sweet potatoes.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos and English

The tourism association's English pages cover prices, hours, how Tsuboyu works and access, but not tattoos or bathing manners, and the city's bilingual pamphlet says nothing on tattoos. Official sources do not give a tattoo rule for the bathhouse or Tsuboyu.

For the Kumano area as a whole, the Tanabe City Kumano Tourism Bureau's English guide, *How to Enjoy a Japanese Bath*, says some lodgings may refuse tattoos but that this does not necessarily apply to overseas visitors in Kumano, and suggests covering ones that might look aggressive, while noting that some places do not allow taped skin in the water. Ask before you go.

### Compared with city baths

No official source compares Yunomine's water with baths elsewhere. The only related offer is the bathhouse's tap for taking water home. What you can compare anywhere is the posted sign; see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Food

- **Boiled in the spring.** The tourism association, the prefecture, Tanabe city and the tourism bureau all describe eggs and vegetables cooked in the yuzutsu. The farm ministry's list of Wakayama's local dishes has no hot-spring item, so this is the local bodies' account.
- **Mehari-zushi.** The farm ministry lists *mehari-zushi*, rice wrapped in pickled greens, among Wakayama's local dishes, for the southern area, as a packed lunch between fishing and forestry work, with several theories for the name. The tourism association's English page lists "mehari" on a teahouse menu at Yunomine. The ministry does not name Yunomine.
- **At the inns.** The tourism bureau says many inns cook with the local spring water.

More in the [Kinki local dishes guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Bus stop.** JNTO gives about 1 hour by bus from Shingū station and about 2 hours from JR Kii-Tanabe. The tourism bureau says buses run daily from Tanabe, Shingū and Gojō.
- **From Osaka.** The tourism association (pages updated August 2022) gives about 130 minutes from Shin-Osaka to Kii-Tanabe by the *Kuroshio*, then about 120 minutes by bus to Hongū, or about 100 minutes by Meikō Bus's express. JR West's line timetable for 13 October 2026 shows 136 to 150 minutes to Kii-Tanabe.
- **From Nagoya.** The association gives about 3 hours 10 minutes by JR limited express to Shingū, then a bus.
- **From Tokyo.** The association suggests flying from Haneda to Nanki-Shirahama (70 minutes), then a direct bus (140 minutes).
- **From Totsukawa or Hongū.** On Nara Kōtsū's Yagi–Shingū bus, one service leaves Totsukawa Onsen at 13:39 and Hongū Taisha-mae at 14:23, reaching Yunomine Onsen at 14:34.

Check times with the operators before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"You book Tsuboyu."** There are no reservations; it is a numbered queue for 30-minute slots.
- **"Tsuboyu is the World Heritage listing."** The prefecture's inventory names Yunomine Onsen as the component. Tanabe city says Tsuboyu was registered as part of the route.
- **"It is officially Japan's oldest spring."** The ministry's plan, the city and the association all say "said to be", and their dates differ.
- **"The water is 92°C."** That is one figure. The ministry's plan gives 53.5 to 84.3°C across 14 sources.
- **"It must be volcanic."** Researchers note no young volcano nearby, and point to high heat flow, deep fluids and old igneous activity.
- **"It is open every day without exception."** It closes for source cleaning; in 2026 that includes 7 December.
