Masutomi Onsen is a small spa in the mountains of Hokuto city, in northern Yamanashi, at about 1,000 m below Mt Mizugaki and Mt Kinpu. It is best known for radon in some of its water, which local and tourism sources describe with the customary name "radium onsen"; Hokuto city's page calls the area the Masutomi Radium Onsen-kyō. Most of its sources are cool mineral springs, and its city-run day spa has been closed since 2023. This page uses the environment ministry's plan for the spa (July 2018), Hokuto city, the prefectural tourism organisation's site, JNTO and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, both are given. It does not rate any bath or inn, and it does not say what the water will do for you.

For radioactive springs in general, including what "radium" does and does not mean and what public bodies say about safety, see [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). For reading the sign at any bath, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### A national health resort

Masutomi is one of the environment ministry's **national health resort hot springs** (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*). The ministry's list, as of October 2022, gives the designation date as **5 August 1965** under the name "Masutomi Onsen". The ministry's plan, dated July 2018, is titled for the "Masutomi Onsen-kyō".

### Ten sources, five called radioactive

The plan says the **main spring type is a radioactivity-containing sodium chloride spring**, and that **10 sources are in use at 6 facilities**. Its table of sources gives:

| Item | Range |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 17.3 to 32.3°C |
| pH | 5.8 to 6.6 |
| Flow per source | 0.74 to 123.0 litres a minute |
| Year | not given (the prefecture surveys temperature, flow, pH and conductivity every five years) |

Many are **cold mineral springs** (*reikōsen*).

Only **five** of the ten have "radioactive" in their spring name. The other five do not, and one of those five is a source with "radium" in its own name. So a name on a sign is not a guide to the official type; the spring name on the analysis is.

Official sources do not give radon figures for Masutomi. The plan has none, and the prefecture's analysis certificates are not cited here.

### "Radium onsen"

Officially, a radioactive spring is defined by its **radon**, and there is no spring type called "radium spring"; the [radioactive springs guide](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) explains why. Masutomi's sources still use the older name freely:

- **The environment ministry's plan** says Masutomi boasts plentiful radium and radon, and elsewhere "radium and radon content among the highest in the world".
- **Hokuto city** says the source of Masutomi no Yu contains radium rare even in Japan and is a very precious spring.
- **The prefectural tourism site** (English) describes the radium level as high but safe.

None of them gives the figures or comparison behind "among the highest in the world" or "rare in Japan", so treat those as their own descriptions. "Safe" is the tourism site's wording. This site does not give a verdict on safety; the radioactive springs guide sets out what a database run for Japan's nuclear regulator and the Japan Atomic Energy Agency have said about ordinary spa use.

### The rock

The plan explains the landscape as uplift from plate movement around **10 million years ago**, followed by undersea volcanic activity and mountain building, which formed the peaks of Mt Mizugaki (2,230 m) and Mt Kinpu (2,599 m). The rocks are sandstone and slate cut through by **granite** and similar rocks. The plan says many minerals are exposed and that radioactive springs emerge here.

The environment ministry's radiation primer, quoted in the [radioactive springs guide](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/), says granite contains relatively more uranium and thorium. That fits the plan's account, but it does not mean every granite area has radioactive springs, or that only granite areas do. Official sources other than the plan do not explain Masutomi's water.

## Where it came from

### Found three times

The plan says Masutomi's springs are "said to have been discovered three times", citing the history of the former Sudama town:

1. **Around the 4th century**, during the eastern campaign of **Yamato Takeru**, a mineral spring was found, and the Azumaya shrine was built.
2. **In the 16th century**, **Takeda Shingen** found it while developing gold mines, and let soldiers wounded at the battles of Kawanakajima and miners of the Kai gold mines use it for cures. Hence it is called "**Shingen's hidden spring**" (*Shingen-kō no kakushi-yu*).
3. **In 1914**, when the founder of the inn Tsuganerō moved here, his brother in the United States told him the water was high in radium, and it drew attention as "**Japan's first radium mineral spring**". In 1938 Dr Kimura Kenjirō of the University of Tokyo reconfirmed a high radon content.

The plan adds that the writers Takahama Kyoshi and Ibuse Masuji were fond of the spa.

### Other versions

- **Hokuto city** (its photo gallery) says Masutomi is *said* to be a hidden spring Shingen found while developing gold mines, and that it became known as the Masutomi Radium Onsen-kyō after what the city calls radium's medical benefits became known in the Taishō period. Another city page says it has been loved as a place for *tōji*, long-stay bathing, since Shingen's time.
- **The prefectural tourism site** (English) says that, according to legend, gold miners found the hot spring underground.
- **JNTO** calls it one of Shingen's unexpected hot-spring discoveries.

"Hidden spring" is a traditional name, given by the plan as "said to be" and by the city as "handed down", not an official designation. "Japan's first radium mineral spring" is the plan's account, citing the town history; no official body has ranked it. This page records all three discoveries and does not say which is history.

## How the town works

### A bathing village

The plan describes a *tōjiba*, a place for long-stay therapeutic bathing, at about **1,000 m** (the prefectural tourism site gives 1,026 m), in a district that is 95% forest. It gives the local population as 2,650 in 1955 and 450 in the 2016 financial year, with 64% aged 65 or over, and notes there is no hospital, clinic or school. There are **five private inns** and three city facilities: Masutomi no Yu, the Mizugakiyama Riesen Hütte and the Mizugakiyama Green Lodge.

The plan's ideas for the village include a Shōwa-retro townscape, a folk-tale rally map, an innkeepers' board game and the fire festival at the Yu-gongen shrine. It aims to make Masutomi a place for **staying and taking the waters** with doctors, bathing instructors and spa therapists. These are plans, not a description of what is there now.

For visitor numbers, the plan's table gives 35,000, 29,500 and 26,600 overnight guests, and 69,148, 66,529 and 61,704 day visitors, for the 2014, 2015 and 2016 financial years.

### Masutomi no Yu is closed

The city-run day spa **Masutomi no Yu** opened in 1997 and, according to Hokuto city, took about 60,000 bathers a year before the pandemic. After rust and corrosion were found in the bathroom ceiling, it **closed on 1 April 2023**. On **15 June 2026** the city said that building a new bath house and renovating the dining building had been delayed, that the whole facility remains closed, and that it plans to **reopen in January 2027**.

The prefectural tourism site still lists prices for it, without saying whether tax is included and from before the closure, so they are not given here. Official sources do not say which inns take day bathers or at what price.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos and English

The prefectural tourism site has an English page on Masutomi no Yu, which is closed. Official sources do not give a tattoo rule or a guide to bathing for Masutomi. JNTO says public baths decide case by case. More in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn before you book.

### Compared with city baths

No Masutomi source compares its water with baths elsewhere. What applies everywhere is the national posting rule: since 24 May 2005, any bath that adds water, heats, recirculates, uses bath additives or disinfects must say so, and why, on its notice. Where a source is cooler than bath temperature, that notice is where heating should appear. See [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

The word "radium" on a label is also checked by the authorities. An environment ministry committee paper from November 2004 records that on 27 August 2004 Akita prefecture gave oral guidance to an artificial "radium onsen" facility that called itself a "quasi-natural onsen". It has nothing to do with Masutomi; it shows that an attractive name is not the same as an analysis.

## Food

**What the plan lists.** The environment ministry's plan says Masutomi's produce includes game, mountain vegetables, *hanamame* runner beans, Masutomi cucumbers and mineral-rich rice, with miso, tofu and *shimi-daikon* (freeze-dried radish) as processed foods, and that a traditional food culture is still handed down. It credits the cool climate at 1,000 m for the beans, cucumbers and sesame. *Hanamame* and *shimi-daikon* are not among the farm ministry's 30 Yamanashi dishes.

**Hōtō.** The farm ministry lists hōtō for the whole prefecture, and **azuki hōtō**, with sweet red beans, with Hokuto city among the areas where it is handed down, eaten at New Year, Obon, village festivals and rice planting. Its pages do not mention Masutomi. JNTO's overnight itinerary has hōtō for dinner on the night spent at Masutomi, as a meal on the route rather than a local speciality. More in [udon and hōtō](/en/eat/udon-hoto/).

**Not Masutomi's.** The farm ministry places *torimotsu-ni* in Kōfu city, and no official source ties it to Masutomi. The *inobuta* hotpot story mentions the "Mitomi" area, which is on the upper Fuefuki river, a different place despite the similar name.

Prices are not given. More in the [Chūbu local food guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares and current timetables are not on this page.

- **By bus.** From **Nirasaki** station on the JR Chūō line, take the Yamanashi Kōtsū bus bound for "Masutomi Onsen" to the terminus, about **60 minutes**, then walk about 5 minutes (Hokuto city). The prefectural tourism site also gives about 60 minutes by bus from Nirasaki.
- **Seasonal bus.** The Kayagatake Mizugaki Den'en Bus (Mizugaki route) runs for limited periods from Nirasaki to the Masutomi no Yu stop, about 60 minutes (Hokuto city). Its fares are listed without saying whether tax is included.
- **By car.** About 30 minutes from the **Sudama** interchange on the Chūō expressway, via the Masutomi Radium Line road (Hokuto city).
- **From Tokyo.** JNTO's itinerary takes the JR Chūō line limited express from Shinjuku to Enzan, about 1 hour 30 minutes, with sightseeing on the way, then a train to Nirasaki and the bus. Official sources do not give a direct Shinjuku to Nirasaki time.

For another Yamanashi spa, see [Isawa](/en/soak/isawa/); for Nagano, see [Shirahone](/en/soak/shirahone/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Masutomi is a radium spring."** "Radium onsen" is a customary name. The official type is set by radon, and the plan names only five of its ten sources as radioactive.
- **"The source called 'radium' must be radioactive."** The plan shows one source with "radium" in its name whose spring name does not include "radioactive".
- **"It is the world's richest" or "Japan's first".** "Among the highest in the world" is the plan's phrase and "Japan's first radium mineral spring" is the plan's account from the town history; neither is backed by a figure or an official ranking.
- **"Shingen discovered it."** That is one of three discovery stories in the plan, and the city says it is handed down.
- **"You can soak at Masutomi no Yu."** It has been closed since 1 April 2023; the city plans to reopen it in January 2027.
- **"The water is hot."** The plan's sources run at 17.3 to 32.3°C.
