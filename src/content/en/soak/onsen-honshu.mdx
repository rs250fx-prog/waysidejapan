Honshu, the main island, holds most of Japan's hot spring towns, and it holds them for more than one reason. Along a line of volcanoes the heat source is close to the surface. In parts of Kansai there is no active volcano at all and the water is still hot. Under Tokyo, wells hundreds of metres deep bring up ancient seawater warm enough to count as onsen. This page takes the island region by region: what the official figures say, what makes the water, how the best-known towns began, and how they are used. The town guides go deeper; for how to read a spring's name and the sign on the wall, start from the onsen overview.

## Where the hot springs are

The environment ministry's yearly table of hot spring use, for fiscal 2024 (as of the end of March 2025), lists every prefecture. It counts an onsen area only where there is somewhere to stay, and it marks overnight stays as a reference value. It has no ranking column; the comparisons below are mine, made from its figures.

- **Springs.** The most on Honshu are in Shizuoka (2,185), fourth in Japan after Oita, Kagoshima and Hokkaido. Then Aomori (1,089), Nagano (955), Fukushima (799) and Miyagi (740).
- **Onsen areas.** Nagano has 193, second in Japan after Hokkaido. Then Niigata (135), Fukushima (122), Aomori (120) and Shizuoka (118).
- **Overnight stays.** Shizuoka (10,576,818) is second in Japan after Hokkaido. Then Nagano, Gunma, Kanagawa and Hyogo.

Adding up the 34 Honshu prefectures gives 2,133 onsen areas, 14,879 springs and 90,144,920 overnight stays, against national totals of 2,839, 27,899 and 125,414,300.

By region (my grouping, not the ministry's; Mie is counted with Chubu):

- **Tohoku** — 533 onsen areas, 4,049 springs. Most areas in Fukushima, Aomori and Akita; most springs in Aomori.
- **Kanto** — 387 areas, 2,280 springs, and 21,566,569 stays, led by Gunma, Kanagawa and Tochigi.
- **Chubu** (Hokuriku, Koshinetsu and Tokai) — 786 areas, 5,490 springs, 36,840,252 stays. The largest block on all three counts, led by Nagano, Niigata and Shizuoka.
- **Kansai** — 243 areas, 1,431 springs. Hyogo and Wakayama lead.
- **Chugoku** — 184 areas, 1,629 springs. Hiroshima, Shimane and Yamaguchi have the most areas.

More springs does not mean better springs. A spring count includes springs in use and not in use.

## Why here

**The volcanoes.** The Japan Meteorological Agency defines an active volcano as one that has erupted within roughly the last 10,000 years or is now actively steaming, and counts 111 in Japan. Its list puts active volcanoes in Tohoku (from Osorezan and Iwakisan down through Zao, Bandai and Nasu), in Kanto (Akagi, Haruna, Kusatsu-Shirane, Asama, Hakone, the East Izu group, Fuji, and the Izu islands south of Tokyo), in Chubu (Niigata-Yakeyama, Myoko, Yakedake, Norikura, Ontake, Hakusan and others) and in Chugoku (Sanbesan in Shimane and the Abu group in Yamaguchi). It lists none in the Kansai prefectures, and none in Tottori, Okayama or Hiroshima.

**The volcanic front.** The government's Headquarters for Earthquake Research Promotion explains that the line joining Japan's active volcanoes, the volcanic front, runs from the Kurils across Hokkaido, down the middle of Tohoku to the western edge of Kanto, and on through the Izu peninsula and islands. It runs parallel to the ocean trench, and there are almost no active volcanoes on the Pacific side of it. Water squeezed out of the sinking Pacific plate lowers the melting point of the rock above and makes magma. That page does not discuss hot springs, so I have not drawn a map of onsen along the front.

**Volcanic and non-volcanic water.** Kanagawa's Hot Springs Research Institute separates the two. Volcanic springs, like those of Hakone, are heated by a magma chamber; the typical type there is a chloride spring, and near Owakudani the water is an acidic calcium sulphate spring. Non-volcanic springs come in two kinds. Deep groundwater, warmed by the ordinary rise in temperature with depth, is fairly cool (around 40°C at most), low in dissolved matter and strongly alkaline; Nakagawa and Nanasawa are its examples. "Fossil seawater", trapped in the strata long ago, gives Tsurumaki and the brown springs of Yokohama and Kawasaki. The institute adds that most hot spring water starts as rain and snow that soaked into the ground.

**Hot water without a volcano.** A 2024 newsletter from the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST) says most springs are rain or seawater warmed by the earth's heat or by volcanic gas and magma, but that some, like Arima, have no volcano nearby and are hot because deep fluids have mixed in. Its diagram separates volcanic deep fluid (magmatic water) from non-volcanic deep fluid, which it labels "Arima-type hot water". I could not find where AIST draws the edges of "Arima-type", and I do not say where Arima's water ultimately comes from.

**Under Tokyo.** Tokyo's environment bureau says the city's coastal wards, around Ota, can produce pale or dark brown water called *kuroyu*, "black water", long used in public baths: a mineral spring below 25°C, said to come from ancient seawater. Deeper wells, hundreds of metres down and pumped hard, reach water above 25°C that also carries salts from old seawater, which makes it onsen under the law, and more springs are being developed this way. In western Tokyo there are alkaline and sulphurous springs, some flowing naturally and some used for about 600 years. The islands, the bureau says, have many volcanic springs, some above 80°C.

I could not find official sources explaining what makes the springs of Tohoku, Hokuriku or Chugoku as a whole, so I have not generalised.

## The towns

I could not find a ministry or prefectural count of the most common spring type in any region. What follows is what each town's own page says.

**Tohoku.** Osaki city says Naruko has about 370 sources and seven of the ten modern spring types; Miyagi's tourism federation says eight of eleven. The two use different classifications, so the number depends on which list you count against. Hijiori's official site gives a sodium chloride and hydrogen-carbonate spring and says the colour and feel differ from source to source. I could not find a source for Sukayu's spring type.

**Kanto.** Kusatsu's "Senshitsu Shugi" page describes strongly acidic water, the most naturally flowing water in Japan by its own account, poured into the baths without added water. The Japan Spa Association, an industry body, lists acidic, sulphur, aluminium-bearing, sulphate and chloride springs there; "aluminium-bearing" is an older name that is not among today's ten categories. Kanagawa's institute names Yugawara a sodium-calcium chloride-sulphate spring and Tsurumaki a sodium-calcium chloride spring.

**Chubu.** Gero's tourism association gives an alkaline simple spring at pH 9.2. Atami city says chloride springs are the majority in the city, with more than 500 sources, a total flow close to 20,000 litres a minute, about nine in ten above 42°C and an average of about 63°C. I could not find official spring types for Hokuriku or Koshinetsu as regions.

**Kansai.** Arima's tourism association describes *kinsen*, "gold spring", as water with iron and salt, and *ginsen*, "silver spring", as either water with carbonic acid or water "with radon" — both called ginsen. It does not give official spring names. I could not find a spring type on Kinosaki's association pages, and I could not open Shirahama's official site.

**Chugoku.** Tamatsukuri's official site gives a weakly alkaline sodium-calcium sulphate-chloride spring. Yonago city gives Kaike as a sodium-calcium chloride spring with 19 sources and a flow of 4,456.5 litres a minute, the most in Tottori; the city page gives both "70 to 85 degrees" and a top source of 83°C, and the prefecture's tourism federation gives an average of 66.5°C. The federation describes Misasa as radioactive, averaging 52.8°C, with "one of the highest radon contents in the world".

**National health resort onsen.** The environment ministry designates some areas as *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, which it explained in 2017 as onsen areas the minister designates to promote public use, where the benefits of bathing can be expected and the place can serve as a healthy resort. Its list says 79 places as of October 2022. Counting its Honshu entries by region: Tohoku 19 (eight in Yamagata alone, including Zao, Ginzan and Hijiori), Kanto 6, Chubu 17, Kansai 5, Chugoku 12. Osaki city says three parts of Naruko were designated in Shōwa 35 and the whole district in Heisei 28. The ministry's 2017 announcement spoke of 94 places so far; I could not find why that differs from the 79 of 2022.

**How they began.** Several towns give more than one origin, and this site does not choose.

- **Gero**: found in the Tenryaku era (947–957) or the Engi era (901–923), by two accounts. The association says the spring first rose near the top of Mt Yugamine, stopped before 1265 and was found again on the bank of the Hida river, the "white heron legend".
- **Atami**: the city cites a record that the monk Mangan moved the spring in 749, and the Izu *fudoki* of 713 mentioning Atami's hot water. In 1604 Tokugawa Ieyasu came to bathe with two of his sons, and later made Atami land held directly by the shogunate.
- **Hijiori**: opened in 807, with 1,200 years marked in 2007. Of several stories behind the name, the site tells of an old monk who broke his elbow and bathed it here.
- **Kinosaki**: the association marked 1,300 years in 2020 and says the monk Dōchi came in 717.
- **Tamatsukuri**: its site says the date is not certain, that it is counted among Japan's oldest springs, and that the *Izumo fudoki*, compiled in 733, describes people of all ages enjoying water rising by the river.
- **Kaike**: found in 1900, when fishermen came upon hot water in the shallows off the beach.
- **Sukayu**: its operator says the spring was found in 1684 and has been a place for long-stay bathing since the Edo period.

**The three famous springs.** Kusatsu, Arima and Gero are often grouped as Japan's three great springs. Gero's association traces this to writers: the Muromachi-era monk Banri Shūku, who named Gero alongside Kusatsu and Arima, and the Edo-era Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan, who named the same three. I found no government that chose them. Arima calls itself "one of Japan's three ancient springs" without naming the other two or who chose them, so I have not either.

## How people use them

**Long stays.** *Tōji* is the custom of staying at a spring for days or weeks. Osaki city says Naruko is "renowned as a tōji spa of Tohoku", where you can choose a self-catering inn for a long, quiet stay or an ordinary ryokan. Hijiori's site says many inns have kitchens for guests, and that there is a morning market at 5 am every day except in winter. Tottori's federation describes long-stay inns at Misasa where guests cook for themselves and stay three nights or more.

**With food.** Kinosaki's association says the snow crab season opens on 6 November, and its "crab kingdom" promotion runs from then to the end of March. The farm ministry lists boiled *matsuba-gani*, the San'in name for snow crab, among Hyogo's local dishes. The ministry's entry I could see does not name Kinosaki, so I say only that both sides feature the crab. For Kusatsu, Arima, Gero, Atami, Naruko, Tamatsukuri, Misasa and Kaike I could not find a dish that both the town and the ministry name.

**City baths and onsen towns.** On the ministry's table Tokyo has only 22 onsen areas but 142 public baths using hot spring water; Kanagawa has 199 and Chiba 150. Of Tokyo's 168 springs, 72 are below 25°C, about 43%. Under the law, water below 25°C is still onsen if it meets the mineral standards. The difference between an onsen town and a city day spa is mostly in how the water reaches you: flowing up near a volcano, or pumped from deep wells. I could not find an official source saying city baths dilute or recirculate more. What you can always do is read the sign: a bath open to the public must post its water's analysis and say whether, and why, the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected.

## Getting there

These are the times on the towns' or prefectures' official pages, checked on 10 October 2026. Timetables change; check the operator before you go. Fares are not on this page.

- **Kinosaki** — From JR Osaka by limited express Kounotori about 2 hours 40 minutes; from Sannomiya by Hamakaze about 2 hours 30 minutes; from Kyoto by Kinosaki about 2 hours 20 minutes.
- **Arima** — From JR Osaka about 1 hour, changing trains on the way; from Osaka Itami Airport by bus about 30 minutes.
- **Atami** — From Tokyo by Kodama shinkansen about 45 minutes. The city's page was last updated in March 2017, so treat this as old information.
- **Gero** — The association's sample timetable has a train from Nagoya taking about 1 hour 34 minutes. It says every train, including the limited express Hida, stops at Gero.
- **Naruko** — From Sendai by Tohoku shinkansen to Furukawa 15 minutes, then the Rikuu East line to Naruko-Onsen 50 minutes. Highway bus 1 hour 30 minutes.
- **Kaike** — From Yonago station about 15 minutes by car or 20 minutes by bus.
- **Misasa** — From JR Kurayoshi about 20 minutes by car.

Kusatsu's association lists highway buses from Tokyo station, Busta Shinjuku and Shibuya, and Tamatsukuri lists its airports and station, but neither gives a journey time. I could not find official times for Hakone or Shirahama.

## What people get wrong

- **"Onsen need a volcano."** Kansai has no active volcano on the agency's list and still has Arima. Tokyo's deep wells and Kanagawa's fossil seawater are non-volcanic too.
- **"Honshu's onsen line up along the volcanic front."** The front is real, but the source that describes it does not talk about hot springs, and I could not find one that does.
- **"The three great springs are an official list."** They come from writers of the Muromachi and Edo periods.
- **"Silver spring is a radium spring."** Arima says one of its silver springs contains radon. The official classification decides radioactive springs by radon content, and has no "radium spring" category.
- **"Naruko has seven spring types," or eight.** Both figures are official; they count against different lists.
- **"City onsen are fake."** Tokyo's deep-well water meets the legal definition. Whether one place is better than another is not something the official sources say.
- **"The water will treat a condition."** The ministry's rules list indications by spring type and say treatment at a spa is best done with a doctor's guidance. This page does not claim that any town's water cures anything.
