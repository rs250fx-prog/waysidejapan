Ibusuki is a hot spring town in Kagoshima prefecture, on a volcanic shore. It is known above all for its sand bath, *sunamushi*: you lie on the beach in a yukata while staff cover you with sand heated by the spring water underneath. This page explains how the town works using Ibusuki city, the Ibusuki tourism association, the Japan Tourism Agency’s multilingual commentary, Kagoshima prefecture, the Japan Meteorological Agency, the farm ministry, the environment ministry, the Japan Hot Spring Association and JNTO. Where sources disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how Ibusuki compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the [Kyushu hot springs overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/). For what its spring names mean, see the page on [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/); for the notices about recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). [Kirishima](/en/soak/kirishima/), also in Kagoshima, has its own guide.

## Where it came from

### The lords’ baths

The tourism agency’s commentary says that in the 1600s a lord of the Shimazu family, who ruled the area, built a villa here, and that the bath and part of the tilework of the lord’s bath, *Tono-sama-yu*, built in 1831, survive as a cultural property designated by Ibusuki city. It also says the name Ibusuki is thought to come from an old place name meaning "a town rich in spring water". That is the agency’s "thought to", and I leave it there.

The same commentary dates the town’s hot springs further back, through a European visitor’s report. The years it gives for that visitor do not fit together, and official sources do not resolve them, so I do not repeat that part here.

### How old is the sand bath?

Two national sources give different answers.

- **JNTO** (undated) says the sand baths have been enjoyed for **more than 300 years**.
- **The Japan Tourism Agency’s English commentary**, written in fiscal 2024, says **at least 160 years** and names no starting year.

Ibusuki city’s own page on its sand bath programme for older residents (updated 22 March 2024) gives no history. So I give both figures and do not choose. The same tourism agency page also gives the number of visitors differently in different language versions, about 250,000 in one and about 300,000 in another.

## The volcano

### What the tourism agency says

The agency’s commentary places Ibusuki in a volcanic area. The town sits inside the **Ata caldera**, left by a huge eruption about 100,000 years ago, and is dotted with small craters from a large eruption about 6,300 years ago. The cone of **Kaimondake** is an active basaltic stratovolcano. The commentary says the same heat and pressure push groundwater up to the surface as hot water.

The environment ministry lists Lake Ikeda and Kaimondake among the sights of the Kinkō Bay area of Kirishima-Kinkōwan National Park.

### Kaimondake, as checked on 12 October 2026

The Japan Meteorological Agency gives Kaimondake as **924 m**, a basaltic stratovolcano with an andesite lava dome at the top. It has erupted repeatedly since about 4,400 years ago, with large magma eruptions in **874** and **885**; the last lava dome formed in 885.

On 12 October 2026 the agency’s page showed an **eruption forecast**, with a reminder that Kaimondake is an active volcano. The latest item it listed dated from 1 December 2007, and there had been no announcements in the previous week. The page does not say whether alert levels are in use for this volcano. Check the agency before you go.

### Ibusuki and Kirishima

Ibusuki city says a volcanic chain it calls the Kirishima range runs the length of the city. The farm ministry’s Kagoshima page says the Kirishima volcanic belt, with Kirishimayama, Sakurajima and Kaimondake, runs through the prefecture, with 11 active volcanoes. Neither source defines the term or explains any link with the springs of [Kirishima](/en/soak/kirishima/), so I do not call them the same volcano’s water.

## The water

### How many springs

The sources count differently.

- **Ibusuki city**: more than **1,000** spring sources in the city.
- **The tourism agency**: more than 1,000 sources, making Ibusuki one of Japan’s densest hot spring areas.
- **Kagoshima prefecture’s list of hot spring areas** (as of November 2020): **414** sources for the Ibusuki area, **175** in use. The PDF’s columns are broken in extraction, so these figures should be read with some caution.

The gap probably comes from what is being counted, the whole city or one hot spring area, all sources or those in use, but no source explains it. I give each with its source.

Official sources do not give the flow or the pH.

### What kind

- **The Japan Hot Spring Association**: a chloride spring, known for its natural sand bath.
- **The tourism agency**: both clear chloride springs and sharp sulphur springs.
- **The tourism association’s page for the sand bath hall Saraku**: a sodium chloride spring, source temperature **84.7°C**, used with both *kakenagashi* and recirculation.
- **Its page for Sayuri** in Yamakawa: a chloride spring, source **86°C** (measured), *kakenagashi*.
- **Kagoshima prefecture’s list** appears to give the main type for the Ibusuki area as a hydrogen-carbonate spring, but the columns are broken, so this is uncertain.

### "Three to four times"

Ibusuki city says the sand bath is said to be three to four times as effective as an ordinary hot spring, and JNTO writes something similar. Neither gives a basis. This page does not repeat it as fact. The city also says Ibusuki ranks third in a "100 hot springs of Japan" selection, without naming who made it; I do not give the ranking.

## The sand bath

### How it works

The tourism agency’s English commentary describes it like this. Geothermal pressure pushes groundwater up; some becomes natural springs, and the rest soaks into the sand. At the water’s edge the hot water meets colder, heavier seawater, floats, and rises into the beach sand. The rising water is **above 90°C**; the agency does not give the temperature of the sand itself.

Staff dig a hollow about 10 cm deep. You change into a yukata (the English text says "robe"), lie on your back, and they cover you with hot, damp sand up to your neck. Most people stay about **ten minutes**, some 30 minutes or more.

### Precautions, as the sources give them

- **Do not walk barefoot.** JNTO says the sand at the water’s edge can reach about **85°C**.
- **About ten minutes.** JNTO and the tourism agency both give this.
- **Change first.** JNTO says to put on the yukata before going to the beach.
- **Rinse afterwards.** JNTO says to wash the sand off and then bathe in the facility’s hot spring.
- **No soap at Sayuri.** The tourism association says soap and shampoo cannot be used there, because of the facilities.

### The two facilities

As given on the tourism association’s pages, checked on 12 October 2026. Neither page states whether its prices include tax, so I give none. Both say the fee includes the yukata, both have higher fees in special periods, and neither mentions tattoos.

- **Saraku** (sand bath hall). At low tide, the sand bath is on the beach; at high tide or in bad weather, in a roofed sand area. Open 8:30 to 21:00, last entry 20:00; on weekdays no entry from 12:00 to 13:00. Open all year, with temporary closures in July and December for equipment checks. A notice of a fee change from 1 October, on the association’s site, also gives no tax status.
- **Sayuri** (Yamakawa sand bath), with a view of Kaimondake and the East China Sea. Open 8:30 to 17:00, last entry 16:30. Closed on Wednesdays, or the next day if Wednesday is a public holiday, and in stormy weather. About 30 people an hour.

## Bathing there

Official sources give no prices for baths in Ibusuki with a stated tax status, so none are given.

- **Tattoos.** There is no official guidance for Ibusuki. The tourism association’s home page offers other languages through machine translation links, and neither it nor the two sand bath pages mention tattoos. JNTO’s general guide (undated) says some onsen refuse people with tattoos, that you should check in advance, that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option, and that small cover sheets only suit very small tattoos. The wider picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Manners.** JNTO’s guide says to leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room, wash before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water, tie up long hair, not swim or splash, and not take phones or cameras in.
- **Compared with city baths.** No official Ibusuki source compares its baths with those in the cities, and none describes the sand bath being offered elsewhere. That is a gap in the sources, not a finding.

## What it is eaten with

### Eggs from the source water

The tourism association’s local food page lists the ***Ontamaran-don***, a rice bowl whose soft-boiled egg is made, by design, with the source water of Ibusuki’s sand bath. Here the association itself joins the dish and the spring.

### Katsuobushi

The same page says Yamakawa in Ibusuki is said to make about 70% of Japan’s *honkarebushi*, the fully cured dried bonito, and lists *ibukara*, fried chicken made with bonito flakes, and a ramen topped with it. The farm ministry’s geographical indication for **Ibusuki katsuobushi**, registration **No. 169**, registered on **18 March 2025**, names Ibusuki city as the producing area and says it makes more than 70% of the national output of *honkare-honbushi*. Neither page mentions the hot springs, so I do not link the two.

### Others

- **Black pork.** It appears on the association’s page only in dish names. The farm ministry’s Kagoshima page says black pork and black beef can be had in restaurants across the prefecture, without tying them to Ibusuki.
- ***Sume-ryōri*.** A tourism agency text introduces food steamed in the heat of volcanic fumaroles at Lake Unagi. It describes the use of volcanic heat, not the hot springs, and it is a short introduction.
- **Satsuma-age.** The farm ministry places it across the prefecture and does not mention Ibusuki. No official source links the two.

More of Kagoshima’s dishes are on the [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/) page.

## Getting there

As given by Kagoshima prefecture’s tourism site, the Ibusuki tourism association and JNTO, checked on 12 October 2026. The prefecture’s pages show no update date and JNTO’s is undated. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Hakata.** Kyushu shinkansen *Mizuho* or *Sakura* to Kagoshima-Chūō, about **1 hour 16 minutes** at the fastest.
- **From Fukuoka Airport.** About 50 minutes by air to Kagoshima Airport.
- **From Kagoshima-Chūō.** The JR Kyushu limited express *Ibusuki no Tamatebako* to Ibusuki station, about **52 minutes**, all seats reserved. JNTO gives about **1 hour** by train; it does not say which service, and the difference is probably the kind of train. About 1 hour 20 minutes by car via the Ibusuki toll road.
- **From Kagoshima Airport.** Airport bus to Ibusuki station, about **2 hours**, or about 1 hour 40 minutes by car. JNTO gives about 40 minutes by bus to Kagoshima-Chūō and then the train.
- **To Saraku.** JNTO gives about 5 minutes by bus from Ibusuki station.
- **By sea.** The tourism association says the Toppy Rocket fast ferry’s Ibusuki route has been **suspended since 1 April 2026**.

Official sources do not give routes or times from Miyazaki, Kumamoto or Oita.

## What people get wrong

- **"The sand bath is 300 years old."** That is JNTO’s figure. The tourism agency says at least 160 years.
- **"Ibusuki has exactly 1,000 springs."** The city says more than 1,000; the prefecture’s list gives 414 for the Ibusuki area, counted differently.
- **"It is three to four times as effective."** The city and JNTO say this is said, without a basis. This page does not claim it.
- **"Walk to the sand barefoot."** JNTO warns the sand at the water’s edge can reach about 85°C.
- **"Katsuobushi is made with the hot springs."** Neither the GI nor the association’s page says so. The egg bowl is the dish the association ties to the source water.
- **"Ibusuki and Kirishima share one volcano."** The official pages name a Kirishima volcanic belt without explaining any link between the two towns’ springs.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications by spring type is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
