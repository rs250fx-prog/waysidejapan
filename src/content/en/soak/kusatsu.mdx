Kusatsu, in the mountains of Gunma, is built around one open-air source in the middle of town. The water comes out of the ground hot and strongly acidic, and most of what makes the town distinctive — the wooden channels, the paddles, the short timed baths — is a way of dealing with that. This page explains how the town works using the town office, the tourism association, the ryokan association’s site and national agencies. Where they give different numbers, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath.

For what pH, spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean in general, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the posted notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on *kakenagashi*.

## Where it came from

### The legends

The town office lists founding legends tied to Yamato Takeru, the monk Gyōki and Minamoto no Yoritomo, and says no specific founding year is recorded. The tourism association’s chronology gives the same three as things that are "handed down", placing Yoritomo’s visit in 1193, during a hunt at the foot of Mount Asama. Neither gives evidence for any of them, and this page does not choose one.

The town says many warlords came to bathe during the Warring States period, that in the Bunka and Bunsei years of the late Edo period more than 10,000 visitors a year are said to have come, and that the shogun Tokugawa Yoshimune is said to have had the water carried to Edo Castle.

### The name

The tourism association offers two explanations. One traces it to a line in the *Daihannya* sutra that mentions a hot spring called Kusatsu in the south. The other says the smell of hydrogen sulphide made it *kusa-uzu*, "stinking water", which became *Kusatsu*. It does not choose between them.

### Winter

The tourism association’s culture page records that in the past the inns closed from 9 October to 7 April and residents moved down to lower ground for the hard winter.

### The "three famous springs"

Kusatsu’s tourism association calls it one of Japan’s three famous springs, alongside Arima and Gero, without saying who chose them. I found no official body that did; the grouping goes back to the writings of a Muromachi-era monk and an Edo-era scholar, as the separate Honshu hot springs guide explains.

## The water

### How much, and from where

The town says the springs come out at "several tens" of places, a few of them privately owned and most owned and managed by the town, and that all of them flow naturally rather than being pumped. Six are the main sources: Bandai, Yubatake, Shirahata, Jizō, Sainokawara and Nikawa. Both the town and the tourism association say Kusatsu has the largest natural flow in Japan. I could not find an official total count of sources.

The flow figures do not agree, and I have not reconciled them.

- **The tourism association** gives more than 32,300 litres a minute, about 230,000 drums a day, with no survey year.
- **The ryokan association’s site** gives the same 32,300 litres a minute, citing a 1995 hot-spring statistics feature in *Onsen*, the Japan Hot Spring Association’s journal, and the town’s 2018 yearbook. It ranks the top five natural flows as Kusatsu, Beppu, Okuhida, Isawa-Kasugai and Nasu.
- **JNTO**, in an article from January 2022, gives 5,000 litres a minute.
- **An English site run by Kusatsu’s ryokan cooperative** gives roughly 32,300 litres "each day" — a day rather than a minute.

The ryokan association’s site also gives a table by source. The temperature column has no unit, and I have not checked the figures word for word.

| Source | Temperature | pH | Flow (L/min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yubatake | 52.7 | 2.1 | 4,040 |
| Shirahata | 52.2 | 2.1 | 659 |
| Sainokawara | 44.9 | 2.1 | 1,073 |
| Jizō | 49.9 | 2.2 | 252 |
| Nikawa | 48.5 | 2.1 | 817 |
| Bandai | 94.5 | 1.6 | 6,200 |
| Others | — | — | 18,961 |
| Total | — | — | 32,300 |

The rows add up to 32,002, about 300 short of the stated total; the gap is in the table itself. For the Yubatake alone, the town and the tourism association both say 4,000 litres a minute.

The town says the Bandai source comes out at about 94.5°C, so it is passed through a heat exchanger and used in baths at about 60°C, while the other sources come out at around 50°C.

### How acidic

| Source of the figure | pH |
|---|---|
| Town office | Around 2.0 |
| Tourism association (Yubatake source) | 2.1 |
| Ryokan association site, table above | 1.6–2.2 by source |
| Ryokan association site, another page | 1.6–2.1 |
| Kusatsu’s three-bath official site | 2.05 |
| Gozanoyu’s own site | 1.7–2.1 |

Every figure is in the acidic band (under pH 3). The tourism association gives the full name of the Yubatake source as an acidic, sulphur-bearing aluminium-sulphate-chloride spring (hydrogen sulphide type), hypotonic, acidic, high-temperature. How to take that name apart is explained in the water types guide; the association itself does not explain it.

### Why it is like this

Two explanations, from different kinds of source.

- **The national geological survey** (AIST’s explanatory notes for the Kusatsu-Shirane volcano map, a synthesis of older research) says water soaks into the ground on the east flank of Kusatsu-Shirane, picks up heat and acidic components from volcanic gas as it passes beneath steaming ground such as Sesshōgawara, mixes with cooler, more dilute groundwater from the north, and comes out near the town. The notes say they draw on older studies and may differ from the latest findings.
- **The tourism association** says rain and snow falling on Shirane soak deep underground, are warmed by magma and dissolve minerals on the way down, a journey said to take more than 30 years, which is why the flow changes little. It adds a note that "there are various theories".

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Kusatsu-Shirane as a stratovolcano rich in fumaroles and hot springs, "Kusatsu Onsen among them", with steaming activity that gives off hydrogen sulphide.

## How the town works

### The Yubatake

The Yubatake, "hot-water field", is the source in the middle of town, where the water runs through wooden channels (*yutoi*) before it is piped on. The town says it flows at 4,000 litres a minute, was chosen as one of Japan’s 100 scent landscapes in 2002 and designated a national Place of Scenic Beauty in 2017; the ryokan association’s English site gives the designation date as 13 October 2017. JNTO describes the water as about 55°C and strongly acidic, cooled in the wooden channels.

The tourism association says there are seven channels, which cool the water before it reaches the baths. The ryokan association’s English site adds that *yunohana*, mineral deposits, crystallise as the water cools; that about every two months the flow is stopped and they are swept up; and that they are packed into small containers of about 95 g. The town says these are sold in shops, hotels and inns in town. The same site says the artist Tarō Okamoto oversaw the design of the Yubatake and its surroundings, and that in 2000, the town’s centenary, the names of a hundred people connected with Kusatsu were carved into the stone fence around it.

The Yubatake source feeds baths around it; the tourism association’s page for Chiyo no Yu gives its source as the Yubatake.

### Yumomi and jikan-yu

*Yumomi* is stirring the bath with long boards. The tourism association’s page for the Netsunoyu hall gives the reason: Kusatsu’s water is around 50°C, diluting it with water is said to weaken it, so it is cooled by stirring with boards about 180 cm long, which also softens the water and serves as a warm-up. That "weakens it" is the association’s and the ryokan association’s explanation; I found no official test of it.

The same page describes *jikan-yu*, "timed bathing", as follows (I read it in summary):

- Since the Edo period, bathers went into the hot water together and came out together, so as not to disturb it.
- In the Meiji era this became a fixed, timed method, the "traditional bath".
- A bath master, the *yuchō*, calls when to get in and out.
- Bathers pour at least 30 scoops over their heads first to avoid feeling faint, stay in for three minutes, and bathe four times a day.
- At its height it was done at six bathhouses.

Netsunoyu stopped holding *jikan-yu* in 1957. Its yumomi show began in 1960; the building was rebuilt as a tourist facility in 1968, closed in 2014, and reopened in April 2015 in a Taishō-style building. The page says *jikan-yu* now takes place at Chiyo no Yu, but I could not find the page’s date. The town announced the closure of *jikan-yu* at Jizō no Yu on 30 March 2021, without a reason in the notice I could read. Gunma prefecture calls *jikan-yu* a bathing method unique to Kusatsu, on a page last updated in 2011.

When it all began depends on the source. The town calls yumomi a communal bathing custom handed down from the Edo period, and JNTO also dates it to the Edo period. The ryokan association’s English site says the custom is said to date from Meiji but the stirring itself goes back to Edo. What they share is an Edo-period origin and a timed method settled in Meiji. I do not pick a year.

The songs sung while stirring are known together as *yumomi-uta*. The tourism association gives one origin story for the best known, *Kusatsu-bushi* — a geisha born in 1897 set bathers’ calls to the shamisen — and its chronology also says a 1918 work by the poet Hirai Banson is said to be its basis. Two origins, both from the association.

The association’s home page listed an October 2026 schedule for the yumomi and dance show (posted 1 October 2026). I have not given the price, because I could not confirm it includes tax.

### Sainokawara

The town describes Sainokawara park as a place where hot water rises all over, sends up steam and flows away as a stream, once called the "demon’s spring". It has place names such as the "demon’s kettle", a legend that you must not shout there, and memorial stones to the German physician Erwin Bälz and the poet Saitō Mokichi among others. The tourism association’s chronology dates the Bälz memorial to 1935.

### The free public baths

The tourism association lists three free public baths (*kyōdō-yu*, shared baths). As checked on 11 October 2026:

| Bath | Fee | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Chiyo no Yu | Free | 6:00–23:00 |
| Shirahata no Yu | Free | 8:00–23:00 |
| Jizō no Yu | Free | 8:00–22:00 |

The association calls Shirahata no Yu the largest shared bath in Kusatsu and puts Chiyo no Yu about two minutes’ walk from the Yubatake. None of the three pages gives a closing day, so I cannot say they are open every day.

The association gives the same notes for each: shared baths were set up for local people’s daily lives and are used and looked after by them; follow the manners; there are no lockers with keys, so do not bring valuables; and there are cleaning times, mostly in the morning, when you cannot bathe. The town’s page for shared-bath users points to the tourism association for details. I could not find these notes in an official English version.

How many shared baths there are depends on whom you ask. JNTO’s 2022 article says 13 free public baths; the ryokan association’s English site says three are open to visitors; the tourism association lists the three above. I do not give a total.

There are also free footbaths. The association lists the footbath at Onsenmon Square, with a channel modelled on the Yubatake, and others in front of the bus terminal and Jizō no Yu.

### The three large baths

Kusatsu’s three larger bathhouses for visitors are Ōtaki no Yu, Gozanoyu and Sainokawara Open-air Bath, which the tourism association links to as "onsen". Their official site says all three are *gensen kakenagashi*, and sells a pass for one bath at each with no expiry date. Their own pages give these hours:

- **Ōtaki no Yu** — 9:00–21:00, last entry 20:00.
- **Gozanoyu** — 7:00–21:00 from 1 April to 30 November, 8:00–21:00 from 1 December to 31 March, last entry 20:30.
- **Sainokawara Open-air Bath** — the site gave hours, but on 26 July 2026 the tourism association posted a notice of changed hours, and I could not confirm which are current.

I have not given admission fees for these three, for the pass, or for the yumomi show, because none of the pages I read says whether the prices include tax.

## Bathing there

Two sets of advice, both in Japanese.

- **The tourism association** says the water is very hot, so it is cooled by the air before use; pour water over yourself first and get in from the feet; take about three baths a day at most; and expect that clothing can discolour or deteriorate.
- **The three-bath site** says rest at least two hours after drinking alcohol, 30 minutes to an hour after eating and 30 minutes after exercise; bathe no more than three times a day; pour water over yourself and start half-immersed, for 5 to 10 minutes at first; keep the final rinse short; and wear a yukata afterwards if you can, because the sulphur damages fabric.

The English cooperative site’s FAQ suggests first immersing the lower half of your body and getting out when sweat appears on your forehead or nose. Its English page on the Yubatake also reads as allowing you to add some cold water at a public bath with the agreement of others there; I found nothing else to support that.

**Tattoos.** I found no policy for Kusatsu as a whole, and the town and tourism association pages I checked do not mention tattoos. Two English pages from the town point in opposite directions. The ryokan cooperative’s English FAQ says almost all lodgings do not allow tattooed guests in the large shared baths and suggests a lodging with a private bath. Ōtaki no Yu’s English site says most inns and baths in Kusatsu welcome guests with tattoos that are not gang-related. The second is one facility’s site, not a town policy. Ask each inn or bath in advance, and a private bath is an option where there is one.

## What it is eaten with

JNTO names *onsen manjū*, steamed buns, and *onsen tamago*, soft-cooked eggs, as things to eat while walking around the Yubatake, and the tourism association’s food-walk list includes several shops selling *onsen manjū*. The farm ministry’s list of Gunma’s local dishes does not include them, and I found no history of them on the shop pages I read.

The one link that both the ministry and the town side share is *hana-ingen*, the large runner bean. The farm ministry lists simmered *hana-ingen* as a local dish of the Agatsuma area, Kusatsu among its towns, grown in cool highlands at 900 to 1,300 m. The tourism association recommends sweets made from the bean, such as bean-paste wafers and *yōkan*, as souvenirs. The ministry describes simmered beans, the association sweets; what they share is the bean.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026.

- **Rail and bus.** The tourism association gives about 2 hours 33 minutes by the JR limited express Kusatsu-Shima from Ueno to Naganohara-Kusatsuguchi, then about 25 minutes by JR bus. JNTO gives about 2 hours 30 minutes and 25 minutes for the same route.
- **Direct bus.** JNTO says the Joshu Yumeguri bus runs daily from Busta Shinjuku in about 3 hours 45 minutes. The tourism association lists highway buses from Tokyo Station, Busta Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Kichijōji and elsewhere, without journey times.
- **Car.** JNTO says about 3 hours from Tokyo.

Fares are not on this page. The tourism association’s home page carried notices about the reopening of National Route 292 and restrictions near Kusatsu-Shirane (May 2026) and a trial reopening of part of a trail near the crater lake (October 2026); check its notices before you go, especially in winter.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kusatsu’s flow figure is an official measurement."** The 32,300 litres a minute comes from the tourism and ryokan associations, with no survey year given; JNTO has published 5,000. I could not confirm a public measurement.
- **"Yumomi is a show."** It began as a way of cooling water without diluting it, tied to the timed communal bath. The show is a later form, from 1960 at Netsunoyu.
- **"The free shared baths are tourist facilities."** The tourism association describes them as baths for local daily life, run by locals, with no lockers and cleaning times.
- **"There are dozens of free baths."** Depending on the source it is 13 or three. I do not give a number.
- **"Kusatsu is officially one of Japan’s three best springs."** The phrase is used locally, but no official body chose the three.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The tourism association lists conditions it says the water is "generally said" to help. The official list of indications for acidic and sulphur springs is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
