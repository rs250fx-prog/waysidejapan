Nagayu is a hot spring town in Taketa city, in the hills of southern Oita, known for water with carbon dioxide in it and for drinking that water as well as bathing in it. This page explains how the town works, mainly from the environment ministry’s April 2026 plan for the Taketa springs as a national health resort onsen, with the Taketa tourism association’s guide, Oita prefecture, Taketa city, the farm ministry and JNTO. Much of the history is the plan’s own account, and I say so where it is. Where sources disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

Nagayu is already one of the places on the page about [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/), which explains what "carbonated" means officially and why the word on a sign can mislead. This guide keeps to the same sources and adds the town itself. For how Nagayu compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the [Kyushu hot springs overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/). For the town’s declaration about unaltered water, the background is in the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Where it came from

### A health resort, twice

The ministry’s plan says Nagayu was designated a national health resort onsen, *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, by the then Environment Agency in **March 1978**. In **March 2014** the wider **Taketa springs** were designated: three onsen districts, Nagayu, Kujū Kōgen and Taketa–Ogi, with eight hot spring areas between them.

### The Fudoki

The plan says Taketa has "Nagayu Onsen, already mentioned in the *Bungo no Kuni Fudoki*" of the early Nara period. That the name Nagayu appears in the *Fudoki* itself is not confirmed, so this is the plan’s account, not a checked quotation.

### The Oka domain

The plan describes Nagayu as a spring with a history of curative bathing, *tōji*, by the lords and retainers of the Oka domain. Elsewhere it says the third lord, Nakagawa Hisakiyo, set up a lodge for his visits, an *ochaya*, in **1663**. The extracted text of that passage is broken off, so I give only the year and the lord’s name.

### A verse, and Germany

The plan says that in the early Shōwa era a doctor of Kyushu Imperial University, Matsuo Takeyuki, wrote a verse praising the water both for drinking and for long soaking. That is a verse quoted in the plan, not a medical finding, and this page does not treat it as one.

The plan also says Nagayu has a sister-town link with **Bad Krozingen** in Germany, and that German culture can be felt in the town, in the building of a drinking fountain and in the sale of German wine.

## The water

### Two counts in one plan

The plan’s overview of the Nagayu district gives:

- **36 sources**
- **Main types**: hydrogen-carbonate spring and carbon dioxide spring (carbonated spring)
- **Source temperatures**: **25 to 52°C**
- **Flow**: **16 to 291 litres a minute** per source
- **Facilities**: 14 day-bathing places, 17 places to stay and 29 drinking places; two of the day-bathing places and two of the drinking places were closed.

Another section of the same plan, on the state of the spring resources, says **38 sources are used by 31 facilities** for bathing or drinking. So the plan itself gives two counts, 36 and 38, and I give both.

### What "carbonated" means here

In the plan’s source table, names such as "carbon dioxide-containing magnesium·sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring" stand out. The extracted part of the table shows no source named "simple carbon dioxide spring", though the extraction was incomplete. So a visitor looking for "carbonated" on a sign may find "hydrogen carbonate" or "carbon dioxide-containing" instead. The [carbon dioxide springs page](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/) explains the two official thresholds and the naming.

The Japan Hot Spring Association, describing carbon dioxide springs in general, says Nagayu is well known as one with relatively high temperatures.

### "One of Japan’s leading"

The plan says Nagayu was called **"one of Japan’s leading carbonated springs"**, for its concentration of carbon dioxide, its flow and its temperature. A 1991 Oita prefecture publicity piece, reprinted on the prefecture’s site, says that at 1,200 ppm it "is said to be Japan’s best". It gives no basis, and it is a "said to be". The Taketa city site does not make the claim. This page does not call Nagayu the best or the first in Japan.

### Where the gas comes from

Official sources, the plan included, do not explain where Nagayu’s carbon dioxide comes from, so this page gives no explanation of it.

### Not all lukewarm

The plan’s range of 25 to 52°C shows that not every source here is lukewarm. Official sources do not explain why some carbonated baths are cool. The figures for Ramune Onsen-kan below are one facility’s baths, not the whole town.

## How the town works

### Outside baths and drinking

The plan describes two habits. One is bathing in *sotoyu*, "outside baths": public bathhouses and other people’s baths, rather than those in your own inn or home. The other is drinking the water, for which fountains have been set up; the plan counts **29 drinking places**, including closed ones.

The tourism guide’s Q&A puts it more simply: Nagayu is *gensen kakenagashi*, straight from the source, everywhere, and the places to drink are the **Coronada drinking fountain** and the area **in front of the Gozenyu entrance**. That is a different way of presenting it from the plan’s count of 29.

The ministry’s general standard on drinking spring water, summarised in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/), includes that children aged 15 and under should in principle avoid it and that water should not be taken away to drink.

### A declaration of unaltered water

The plan says Nagayu insists on *gensen kakenagashi*, and that in **May 2003** it made **Kyushu’s first "gensen kakenagashi declaration"**. It adds that only nine hot springs in Japan have made such a declaration. These are the plan’s own statements; the document behind the declaration is not confirmed.

### Ramune Onsen-kan

One facility the sources describe in some detail is Ramune Onsen-kan. They give its temperatures slightly differently.

- **The Taketa tourism guide** (page showing 9 September 2026, though it is unclear whether that is an update or a posting date): outdoor carbonated bath **32°C**, cloudy indoor bath **42°C**.
- **Oita prefecture’s tourism article** (9 November 2022): outdoor **32°C**, indoor **41°C**, and a carbon dioxide content of **1,400 ppm**, which it says is the highest in Nagayu.

The indoor figures differ by a degree, perhaps because of the date, but neither source says why. The 1,400 ppm is the prefecture article’s figure, not a confirmed analysis.

As given on the tourism guide, checked on 12 October 2026:

- **Hours:** 10:00 to 22:00.
- **Closed:** the first Wednesday of each month; in January and May, the second Wednesday.
- **Prices:** the guide, the prefecture’s 2022 article and an inn’s page give three different sets of prices, and none states whether tax is included. So I give none.
- Guests of Daimaru Ryokan can use it free, and there is an art museum on the site, the guide says. It does not mention tattoos.

The tourism guide lists many other bathing facilities with prices. Their tax status is not confirmed, so none are given here.

## Bathing there

Official sources do not give prices for baths in Nagayu with a stated tax status, official tattoo guidance, or a comparison of Nagayu’s water with city baths.

- **Tattoos.** There is no official guidance. Taketa city’s English site gives a short introduction to carbonated springs without mentioning Nagayu’s bathing rules or tattoos, its foreign-language page (updated 1 April 2021) only links to machine translation, and the tourism guide’s Q&A says nothing on tattoos or manners. JNTO’s general guide (undated) says some onsen refuse people with tattoos, that you should check in advance, that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option, and that small cover sheets only suit very small tattoos. The wider picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Manners.** JNTO’s guide says to leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room, wash before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water, tie up long hair, not swim or splash, and not take phones or cameras in.
- **Compared with city baths.** The plan states the declaration and leaves it there. Artificial carbonated baths in the cities have no official definition or concentration standard; what is known is on the [carbon dioxide springs page](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/).

## What it is eaten with

Official sources do not tie any dish to Nagayu, and none links one with its springs.

- **Dangojiru.** The farm ministry places *dangojiru* across the whole of Oita, and its page does not mention Taketa, Nagayu or hot springs.
- **Taketa and the sea.** The ministry’s Oita page says Taketa is surrounded by the Kujū mountains, and that in the Edo period sea fish were prized there because the sea was far away. It mentions hot springs across the prefecture but not Nagayu.
- **A cooking class.** Taketa city’s English site (article of 6 March 2025) describes a class of about two hours, booked in advance, making *dangojiru*, *yaseuma* and rolled sushi. It does not mention the hot springs, and I give no price.
- **Atama ryōri.** Taketa’s "head cooking", from the days when sea fish were precious, is described on the [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/) page, without a link to Nagayu’s springs.

## Getting there

As given by the Taketa tourism association’s guide, which shows no update date, checked on 12 October 2026. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. The guide says the Nagayu bus times it shows are those revised on 1 April 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Bungo-Taketa station.** Local buses are infrequent. To the "Michi-no-eki Nagayu Onsen" stop, **30 to 35 minutes** via Takabushi, or about **45 minutes** via Kujū. About **20 minutes** by taxi.
- **From Ōita station.** JR Kyūdai line to Mukainoharu station, then a community bus, three a day.
- **To Bungo-Taketa.** From Oita Airport, the Airliner bus to Ōita station, about 65 minutes, then the JR Hōhi line, 60 to 70 minutes.
- **From Kumamoto Airport.** The access page gives about 130 minutes by the limited express *Yamabiko*; the guide’s home page gives about 1 hour 20 minutes by bus. The two do not agree.
- **By car.** **36 km** (about 45 minutes) from the Yufuin interchange, or **29 km** (about 40 minutes) from the Mitsuyoshi interchange. The guide says the Yufuin interchange is convenient from Fukuoka and Oita Airport. To Taketa, about 2 hours 30 minutes from Fukuoka and about 2 hours 40 minutes from Miyazaki.

Official sources do not give public-transport times from Fukuoka or Hakata, Kagoshima-Chūō or Nagasaki.

## What people get wrong

- **"Nagayu is officially Japan’s number one carbonated spring."** The ministry’s plan says one of Japan’s leading; "Japan’s best" is a 1991 "said to be".
- **"The sign will say carbonated."** Many of the plan’s source names are hydrogen-carbonate springs with "carbon dioxide-containing" at the front.
- **"Carbonated springs are always lukewarm."** Nagayu’s sources run from 25 to 52°C.
- **"Nagayu has 36 sources."** The plan says 36 in one place and 38 in another.
- **"The gas comes from the volcanoes."** Official sources do not say where it comes from.
- **"Dangojiru is Nagayu’s dish."** The farm ministry places it across all of Oita, without mentioning Nagayu.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so, and the plan’s verse is a verse. The official list of indications by spring type is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
