Kinugawa Onsen grew up along the upper Kinugawa river, in Nikko city, Tochigi, and Nikko city calls it one of the leading hot spring towns of the Kantō region. It is a different kind of place from the towns where you walk between shared baths. Large inns and hotels line the gorge, the water is a mild alkaline simple spring, and the railway from Tokyo shaped the town. This page explains how it works using Nikko city, Tochigi prefecture, the Nikko tourism association, the Kinugawa ryokan association, Tobu Railway, the farm ministry and JNTO. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn.

Kinugawa has no environment ministry plan as a national health resort onsen. The ministry’s list shows only two plans for Tochigi, Okunikkō-Yumoto and Itamuro. That, and the pages I could open, leave Kinugawa with fewer official figures than most towns in this series. For what the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### Discovery

The sources agree on the Edo period and differ on the year.

- **Nikko city** says the spring was found in the Edo period, and that there is a theory that it was in 1691 (Genroku 4). It was found on the west bank of the Kinugawa, in the Taki district, and was used under the name Taki no Yu as a *tōji* place, where people stayed for days or weeks to bathe, by people with burns and skin complaints.
- **Tochigi prefecture** and **the Nikko tourism association** say it was found in the Edo period.
- **The ryokan association** also says the Edo period.

The city itself calls 1691 a theory, and this page does not treat it as the founding year.

### For pilgrims of rank

The ryokan association says that because the land belonged to Nikko’s temples and shrines, the spring could be used only by feudal lords and monks on their way back from the pilgrimage to Nikko. The prefecture and the tourism association say the same, and that it was opened to everyone after the Meiji era.

### Two springs, one name

The ryokan association says the spring was long called Taki Onsen and lay only on the west bank. Later Fujiwara Onsen was found on the east bank, and the two together became today’s Kinugawa Onsen. It also quotes an old saying, "wounds at Kawaji, burns at Taki". That is a saying, and this page does not claim the water treats either.

### The railway

Nikko city gives the modern steps:

- **1927** — the name changed to Kinugawa Onsen.
- **1929** — after Tobu Railway’s Nikko line opened, large hot spring hotels and inns were built one after another.
- **18 March 2006** — JR and Tobu limited expresses began running through onto each other’s lines, so you could come from Shinjuku without changing.

The city’s page was last updated on 1 February 2024.

## The water

### Alkaline simple

Every official source I read gives the same type.

- **Nikko city** — a natural alkaline simple spring.
- **Tochigi prefecture** — an alkaline simple spring, soft and gentle on the skin, in its words.
- **The Nikko tourism association** — an alkaline simple spring, without taste or smell, with nothing harsh about it.
- **The ryokan association** — a simple spring, weakly alkaline, colourless, clear, without taste or smell. It writes the name as "alkaline simple spring".

What "simple" means, and why it is often misread as "weak", is explained on the [page on simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). The prefecture also gives the neighbouring Kawaji Onsen as an alkaline simple spring.

### No figures

I found no official count of sources, flow, temperature or pH for Kinugawa, on the city’s, the prefecture’s, the tourism association’s or the ryokan association’s pages that I opened. Figures that appear in travel guides did not come from any of these, and I have not used them.

Nor did I find an official source that explains where the water comes from or whether it is volcanic. "Alkaline" and "simple" describe what is in the water, not its origin, and this page does not say where it comes from.

The ryokan association lists indications and contraindications. This page does not repeat them.

## How the town works

### A gorge of inns

The prefecture and the tourism association describe Kinugawa as one of the Kantō region’s large hot spring towns, with inns and hotels lined up along the Kinugawa gorge. The tourism association says the World Heritage site at Nikko is about 30 minutes away by car, which makes Kinugawa a base for sightseeing there. JNTO describes it as a place to stay after seeing Nikko, with hiking along the gorge, boat trips down the river and theme parks nearby.

### The station footbath

Kinuta no Yu is a footbath in the square in front of Tobu’s Kinugawa-Onsen station. The Nikko tourism association gives it as free, 9:00 to 18:00, with a hand bath as well. Nikko city’s page for the Kinugawa and Kawaji area lists two footbaths, Kinuta no Yu and Kinuko no Yu.

### Day bathing at the inns

Day bathing in Kinugawa happens at inns and hotels. The Nikko tourism association’s feature on nine day-bathing spots presents inns by area, and its notes say that reception hours differ by place and that some days there is no day bathing because of group bookings, so you should check in advance. The association also mentions a leaflet map of day-bathing places in Kinugawa and Kawaji. The feature lists fees for individual inns, but I have not given them, because it does not say whether they include tax and this page does not cover individual inns.

### No shared bath I could confirm

The city once ran a bathing centre, Kinugawa Kōen Iwaburo, but the tourism association’s page now marks it "closed". I could not confirm any public shared bath in Kinugawa today.

From the official pages, then, Kinugawa is a town of large inns along a gorge, with day bathing inn by inn and a free footbath at the station. It is not a town built around walking from one shared bath to the next, like [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/) or [Zao](/en/soak/zao/).

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The Nikko tourism association has a foreign-language section, but the pages I opened had no English guidance on bathing in Kinugawa.
- **Tattoos.** I found no official guidance for Kinugawa on tattoos, on the city’s, the tourism association’s or the ryokan association’s pages, or on JNTO’s Kinugawa page. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn in advance.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Kinugawa source that compares its water with baths in the cities.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish that official sources tie to Kinugawa Onsen.

The obvious candidate is Nikko’s *yuba*, the skin lifted from heated soy milk. The farm ministry lists simmered *agemaki yuba* as a traditional dish of Nikko city and says that in Nikko it is written with different characters (湯波). It says *yuba* is used in the offerings at the spring and autumn grand festivals of Nikko Tōshōgū, that mountain ascetics who avoided meat have long eaten it, and that the Nikko tourism association presents factory tours and *yuba*-making as one of Nikko’s typical products. But the ministry’s page does not mention Kinugawa, and I did not find *yuba* on the tourism association’s Kinugawa page. So it is a Nikko city speciality, and this page does not call it Kinugawa’s.

The ministry’s other Tochigi dishes are not tied to the hot spring town either. Its *ayu-meshi*, rice with sweetfish, uses fish from the Naka and Kinugawa rivers, but the page does not mention Kinugawa Onsen.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026. Fares and limited-express charges are not on this page.

- **Tobu limited express from Asakusa.** I read these times from Tobu’s limited-express timetable, revised on 14 March 2026. The times are drawn as images in the PDF, so I may have misread them. *SPACIA X* No. 7 leaves Asakusa at 12:30 and reaches Kinugawa-Onsen at 14:32, about 2 hours 2 minutes. *Revaty Kinu* No. 109 leaves Asakusa at 8:30 and arrives at 10:36, about 2 hours 6 minutes. Times differ by train and by day, and some limited expresses, such as *SPACIA X* No. 1, end at Nikko and do not go to Kinugawa.
- **From JR Shinjuku.** The same timetable has a column for JR Shinjuku, with notes that the *Spacia Nikkō* and *Kinugawa* services start there; as I read it, the *Kinugawa* runs on weekends and holidays. I could not confirm the current running of every train.
- **Local trains.** JNTO gives about 2 hours 30 minutes from Asakusa on the Tobu Nikko line by local train, and says direct trains also run. That is a figure for a different kind of train, not a contradiction of the timetable. JNTO’s page carries a COVID-19 note and is evidently not recent.
- **Car.** Nikko city gives the Tōhoku expressway to Utsunomiya interchange, then the Nikko-Utsunomiya road to Imaichi interchange, then about 20 minutes on Route 121 towards Kinugawa.

Get off at Kinugawa-Onsen station on the Tobu Kinugawa line, says the city. I could not find an official journey time from Utsunomiya by rail.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kinugawa was founded in 1691."** Nikko city calls 1691 a theory. The other official sources say only the Edo period.
- **"Yuba is Kinugawa’s dish."** The farm ministry ties it to Nikko city, not to the hot spring town.
- **"Kinugawa is a town of shared baths."** I could not confirm any public shared bath there now. Day bathing is at inns, and there is a free footbath at the station.
- **"A simple spring is weak water."** The page on simple springs explains why that is a misreading.
- **"Kinugawa’s water temperature is well known."** I found no official figure for it, or for pH, flow or the number of sources.
- **"It is always about two hours from Asakusa."** That holds for the limited expresses I checked. Local trains take longer, some limited expresses stop short at Nikko, and times change by day.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
