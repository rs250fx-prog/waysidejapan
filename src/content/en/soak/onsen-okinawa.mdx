Okinawa is not where most people go for an onsen, and the figures say why it is unusual. On the environment ministry's table it has fewer hot springs than any other prefecture. Its own government says those springs are not volcanic in the way mainland ones are: most are wells drilled more than a kilometre down. And yet the springs it has are used by a lot of people, mostly in resort hotels, city hotels and seaside baths. This page sets out what is known and, as plainly, what I could not find. For how to read a spring's name and the sign on the wall, start from the onsen overview.

## Where the hot springs are

The environment ministry's yearly table of hot spring use, for fiscal 2024 (as of the end of March 2025), gives Okinawa:

- **15 onsen areas.** The ministry counts a place only where there is somewhere to stay.
- **22 springs**, 2 flowing naturally and 20 pumped, none unused.
- **19 places to stay**, with room for 12,696 guests.
- **2,391,987 overnight stays** in the year, a figure the ministry marks as a reference value.
- **30 public baths** using hot spring water.

The table has no ranking column. Comparing its figures across the 47 prefectures: 22 springs is the fewest in Japan (next come Nara with 77 and Tokushima with 82), about 0.08% of the national 27,899. Fifteen onsen areas ties with Tottori for the fewest. But overnight stays come 18th of 47, between Ishikawa and Osaka, about 1.9% of the national total. The table cannot tell you why; I have not guessed.

The prefecture keeps its own list of springs and the facilities using them. As of 31 March 2026, I count 22 springs on it. That matches the ministry's number, but the two are a year apart and I could not confirm they count the same thing.

The springs run the length of the main island — in the north at Motobu and Nakijin, in the centre at Onna, Kin, Uruma, Chatan and Ginowan, in Naha, and in the south at Urasoe, Tomigusuku and Nanjo — with three on Miyako and one on Iriomote. There is none on Ishigaki on the list.

## Why here

**What the prefecture says.** Okinawa's nature conservation division writes that Okinawa has no volcanic hot springs like those of the mainland, and that almost all come from deep drilling, to depths of 1,000 m or more, pumping water up from far below. The minerals, it says, come from ancient seawater trapped in the strata (fossil seawater) or from seawater that has seeped underground along faults, and the composition depends on the strata at each well.

**What the list shows.** Going through the prefecture's list:

- 15 of the 22 springs are 1,000 m deep or more; the deepest, on Miyako, is 2,431 m. Seven are shallower, from 8 m to 800 m. So "almost all" means 15 of 22 on the list, not all.
- The temperature at the source runs from 24.0°C to 68.7°C. One spring, 8 m deep, is below 25°C; under the law, water below 25°C is still onsen if it meets the mineral standards.
- Seven springs are 42°C or hotter at the source.
- Flow ranges from 2.7 litres a minute on Iriomote to 1,000 on Ginowan.

**The volcanoes.** It is not true that Okinawa has no volcanoes. The Japan Meteorological Agency, which defines an active volcano as one that has erupted within roughly the last 10,000 years or is actively steaming, lists two in the Okinawa region: Iōtorishima and a submarine volcano north-north-east of Iriomote. Iōtorishima is a small island whose people left it repeatedly because of eruptions and which is now uninhabited; its last recorded eruption was a steam explosion around 18 July 1968. The springs on the prefecture's list are on the main island, Miyako and Iriomote, not on Iōtorishima. I found no official source that discusses the two volcanoes and the springs together.

**What I could not find.** No ministry, prefectural or AIST source I could open explains *why* Okinawa has so few springs. I also could not find an official source on how the islands' coral limestone and other strata relate to the water. Private sites discuss this, but I have not used them. What the figures show is that the springs are few; the reason is not on this page.

## The water

The prefecture says sodium chloride springs are "the mainstream". In the official classification that means the main negative ion is chloride and the main positive ion is sodium. I could not find the count behind the statement: neither the prefecture's list nor the ministry's table has a column for spring type. So I do not say what share of Okinawa's springs are chloride springs.

The Okinawa Convention & Visitors Bureau's site, Okinawa Story, gives the facilities' own descriptions, which are not official analyses:

- A Ginowan bath calls its water a natural chloride spring, pH 8.20, from about 1,300 m down.
- A bath in Makishi, Naha, calls its water a sodium chloride spring.
- Another Naha bath gives no spring type, but describes water from 800 m down, "fossil seawater from about 8 million years ago", containing salt.
- A bureau article describes a Nanjo spring as drawing from 2,119 m and from two strata about 5 million and 54 million years old, golden in colour, about 80% as salty as seawater. The prefecture's list gives that spring's depth as 2,100 m.

The ages differ from one description to the next, and they are the facilities' and the bureau's words. I have not found an official source for them.

Whether any Okinawa spring is strong enough to be called a *strong* salt spring under the official thresholds, I cannot say without the official analyses, which I did not have.

## How people use them

**Hotels and seaside baths.** The bureau's article sorts Okinawa's onsen into resort hotels (on Senaga island and at Shigira on Miyako), a bath inside a city hotel in Naha, and baths with a sea view (Senaga, Motobu, Nanjo). It describes Senaga's as next to a waterfront resort, Motobu's as looking out over the sea to Ie island, and Miyako's as a bath with a view of the sea. The prefecture's list shows the same mix among the operators: resort and hotel companies at Onna, Chatan, Motobu, Tomigusuku, Nanjo and Miyako; municipal facilities such as a welfare centre in Kin, a park health facility in Chatan and a facility on Miyako; and day-bathing facilities in Naha and Ginowan. I have not counted which kind is most common.

**On the outer islands.** Miyako has three springs on the list, but two are marked "not currently used for bathing". The one the bureau features is at the Shigira resort. Iriomote's spring has no note of closure on the list, but I could not confirm whether it is open now.

**No national health resort.** The environment ministry's list of *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the areas it designates for health and recuperation, does not include anywhere in Okinawa, and the ministry's table records zero overnight stays at such areas in the prefecture.

**History.** I could not find an official history of hot spring development in Okinawa. The prefecture's list gives the date each well was licensed for use under the Hot Springs Act: the earliest on it is 1988 (the shallow Yamada Onsen in Onna), then 1998 in Naha and 2003 on Miyako. A licence date is not the date a well was drilled, so I would say only that the licence dates on the list start in 1988.

**With food.** The bureau's article suggests places to eat near each onsen: seafood and tempura on Ōjima near Nanjo, Okinawa soba and the Makishi public market near Naha, a market and *agu* pork shabu-shabu near Motobu. Okinawa soba is among the farm ministry's 30 local dishes for Okinawa. But the bureau is pointing to nearby restaurants, not to an onsen-town speciality, and I found no official source that pairs an Okinawa onsen with a dish.

**Heated or not.** Many of the springs are below 42°C at the source, and a cooler spring may be heated in the bath. I could not find any official source on whether or how Okinawa's baths heat, dilute or recirculate their water, and the bureau's facility pages do not say. One Motobu page describes its bath as "100% source-flowing", a term I could not find an official definition for. As everywhere in Japan, a bath open to the public must post its water's analysis and say whether, and why, the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected. The sign on the wall is where to look.

## Getting there

From Naha Airport, as given on the bureau's facility pages, which were last updated in 2023 and 2024. Check the operators before you go. Fares are not on this page.

- **Naha (Mie Gusuku area)** — about 7 minutes by car; about 15 minutes' walk from Asahibashi monorail station.
- **Naha (Makishi)** — about 20 minutes by car on ordinary roads.
- **Ginowan** — about 40 minutes by car on ordinary roads.
- **Motobu** — about 100 minutes by car via the Okinawa Expressway and Route 58, or about 2 hours 30 minutes by the Yanbaru Express bus.

The bureau calls the Senaga island resort the closest resort with an onsen to the airport, but gives no time. I could not find official times for Nanjo or for Shigira from Miyako Airport.

## What people get wrong

- **"Okinawa has no hot springs."** It has 22 springs on the ministry's table, the fewest in Japan, but not none.
- **"Okinawa has no volcanoes."** The meteorological agency lists two active volcanoes in the Okinawa region.
- **"There are no volcanoes, so there are no springs."** I could not find an official source for that cause and effect. The prefecture says only that its springs are not volcanic like the mainland's.
- **"They are all from a kilometre down."** 15 of the 22 on the prefecture's list are.
- **"The water is millions of years old."** That is how some facilities and the bureau describe it, with different ages in different places. I have not found an official source for any of the figures.
- **"Every Okinawa onsen is heated."** I could not find out. Read the sign at the bath.
