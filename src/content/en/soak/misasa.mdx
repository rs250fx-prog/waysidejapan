Misasa Onsen is in the town of Misasa in central Tottori, a bus ride from Kurayoshi station. It is a radioactive spring, the official class for water with enough radon in it, and it is often called a "radium onsen". Its best-known bath is the Kawara-buro, a free stone pool on the riverbank below the town’s bridge. This page covers the water and what the figures for it actually are and how old they are, the founding legend, the public baths and radon steam rooms, food, and how to get there. It uses the Misasa tourism association, Tottori prefecture’s tourism federation, a 2015 review in the journal of the Japanese Society of Hot Spring Sciences and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn, and it does not judge whether radioactive springs are good or bad for you.

What a radioactive spring is, how the class is decided and what official bodies say about it are in the separate guide to [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). For how to read a spring’s name and the analysis sign, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### What it is called

The spring names differ by source.

- **Tottori’s tourism federation** gives the spring type as "radioactive" (*gan-hōshanō*), with an average temperature of 52.8°C. It says the town’s "radium" has one of the highest radon contents in the world, using both words in one sentence.
- **The Misasa tourism association**, in Japanese, gives two names: a radioactive sodium chloride spring and a radioactive simple spring. It calls Misasa one of the world’s leading radioactive springs, with a high concentration of radon. It gives no radon figure, number of sources, flow or temperature.
- **The association’s English page** lists three types "containing radiation": sodium chloride, sodium hydrogen carbonate and simple. It gives no figures. I could not tell whether the difference from the Japanese two is a matter of wording or of counting.

"One of the world’s leading" is the association’s own description. I do not turn it into "the world’s highest".

### Radium or radon

Under the environment ministry’s analysis guidelines, a radioactive spring is decided by its radon content alone; there is no "radium spring" class. The 2015 review notes that old radon springs in Japan are customarily called "radium onsen", and that Misasa is no exception. So "radium onsen" is a customary name, not an official type. The radioactive springs guide explains how radon and radium are related.

### The figures, and their dates

I could not find current figures for Misasa from the town, the prefecture or the environment ministry. The most detailed figures I found are in a review article, "A hundred years of radon research at Misasa Onsen" (Horiuchi and others), published in 2015 in *Onsen Kagaku*, the journal of the Japanese Society of Hot Spring Sciences. It is a review by a learned society, not an official register.

The review summarises a survey of **81 sources recorded in the *Misasa Onsen-shi* of 1983**:

- **Temperature**: 32.5 to 89.3°C.
- **Radon**: 18 to 3,389 becquerels a litre (Bq/L), with an average of 474 Bq/L.
- **Type**: radon-sodium-chloride or radon-sodium-chloride-hydrogen carbonate springs.

Citing earlier work (Mifune, 1987 and 1992), it estimates the flow of Misasa’s springs at 3.02 million litres a day, and the radon given off into the air at up to 1.4 billion becquerels a day.

**The review does not agree with itself on temperature.** Its English abstract says "the highest water temperature of Misasa is 76°C", while the body of the review, citing the 1983 survey, gives a range up to 89.3°C. The two may refer to different years or sources, but I could not confirm that from the text, so I give both. The abstract also says Misasa is the largest radon spring area in Japan, and that radon springs above 34°C are found only at Misasa, which it calls rare worldwide, using the 1978 classification of the analysis guidelines. These are the review’s statements, not an official designation.

For scale: the environment ministry’s threshold for a radioactive spring is 111 Bq per kilogram of water. The review’s figures are per litre, which is not quite the same measure, but its average of 474 Bq/L is clearly above that line. That is my comparison, not an official one.

### An older figure

The review also says a survey in 1914 reported a source at 71°C with a radon content of 142.1 Mache units (1,192 Bq/L), which at the time was the second-highest radon content found in the world.

Misasa is not on the environment ministry’s list of *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the designated health resort onsen areas; the Tottori entries are Shikano, Yoshioka, Sekigane and Iwai.

## Where it came from

### The white wolf

The tourism association, in Japanese and English, tells it like this. More than 850 years ago, in the Heian period, Ōkubo Samanosuke, a retainer of Minamoto no Yoshitomo, came upon an old white wolf. He drew his bow, then let it go. That night the bodhisattva Myōken appeared to him in a dream and, in thanks, showed him where hot water rose. The association says the spring was then handed down among the villagers as water for their illnesses. Its home page speaks of "more than 850 years since the spring opened" and of the white wolf legend.

### The year

The 2015 review says the oldest bath, the Kabuyu, is said to be where, in 1164, a white wolf showed the hot spring at the foot of an old camphor tree, and that Misasa marked its 850th year in 2014. This fits the association’s "more than 850 years ago". Both rest on the legend, so I say "by tradition, 1164", not that the year is established.

### The bridge

The association’s English page says the Misasa bridge was built in 1934.

I found no title of the "oldest" or "three great" kind, with a body named as choosing it, on the official pages.

## How the town works

### The Kawara-buro

The Kawara-buro is a free open-air bath on the riverbank of the Mitoku river, below the bridge. Its name means "riverbed bath".

- **Tottori’s tourism federation** says it is under the Misasa-ōhashi, built of large stones, free, open 24 hours and all year, with a simple changing area, and mixed bathing. It cannot be used from 7:00 to 12:00 on odd-numbered days, for cleaning.
- **The association’s English page** says it is free, open 24 hours all year, closed for cleaning on the mornings of odd-numbered days, and stands by the Misasa bridge on the Mitoku river.

The federation’s Kawara-buro page calls the bridge Misasa-ōhashi, while its Misasa page and the association call it Misasa-bashi, so you will see both names. The pages do not say who manages the bath, and neither gives any rule on swimwear or tattoos.

### Other public baths

The association’s English list of public baths names the Kabuyu (a public bath, a footbath and a drinking fountain), Su-Ha Onsen (a natural radon steam bath), the Kawara-buro, the footbath Kawara no Yu, Yakushi no Yu, Kajika no Yu, and the drinking fountain Kami no Yu at Misasa Shrine. Its list does not give the prices for the baths that charge, and I could not find them elsewhere.

### Radon steam rooms

The association’s English page describes radon steam bathing (*netsuki-yoku*): a room at 32 to 42°C and about 90% humidity, used for 15 to 30 minutes. It is by reservation, up to two months ahead, and closed on Tuesdays and from 30 December to 3 January. Prices differ by facility. One is given excluding tax and another without a tax note, so I do not give them. The same page makes claims about health effects without citing any source, and I do not repeat them. The association calls the experience rare in Japan; that is its own description.

### Long stays

The association describes a modern form of *tōji*, staying to bathe for days or weeks, run by a hospital, the town and the inns working together. Tottori’s tourism federation mentions long-stay inns where guests cook for themselves and stay three nights or more.

The association’s English page also gives bathing advice, which it attributes to a "radium sommelier". Bathe at 40 to 41°C for 5 to 7 minutes, rest for 3 minutes, then bathe again for 5 to 7 minutes; at a footbath, use 39 to 43°C for 10 to 30 minutes. Another of its pages says water above 42°C puts a strain on the body and suggests 2 to 3 minutes. That is the association’s advice, not an official rule.

## Bathing there

- **English and tattoos.** The association’s English pages give the bathing advice above but nothing on tattoos, swimwear or rinsing before you get in. Tottori’s federation page for the Kawara-buro does not mention tattoos either. I found no official rule. Ask each bath or inn.
- **Mixed bathing.** The Kawara-buro is mixed and in the open, beside a bridge.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Misasa source that compares its water with baths in the cities. The rules on posting the water’s analysis, and whether it is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected, are the same everywhere; see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

The farm ministry lists 27 local dishes for Tottori, and the word Misasa does not appear on the list. The nearest the list comes to snow crab is *oyagani no miso-shiru*, miso soup with the female crab. *Tōfu chikuwa*, tofu and fish paste steamed in roughly seven to three proportions, is handed down in the central and eastern areas; the ministry says the most widely held explanation is that it began with an order from Ikeda Mitsunaka, lord of Tottori. Misasa is in the central area, but the ministry does not name the town.

The Misasa association’s food pages list restaurants, cafés, snack bars and *izakaya* by name, without describing a local speciality. I found no dish that both the ministry and the town present as Misasa’s.

## Getting there

From the tourism association’s access pages, Japanese and English (no update date; bus timetable file revised 1 April 2025; checked 11 October 2026). Fares are not on this page.

- **From Osaka.** By the limited express Super Hakuto to Kurayoshi, then local bus about 20 minutes; about 3 hours 15 minutes in all. Highway bus about 3 hours 20 minutes; car about the same.
- **From Tokyo.** By shinkansen to Himeji, the Super Hakuto to Kurayoshi, then local bus about 20 minutes; about 5 hours.
- **By air from Tokyo.** About 2 hours 10 minutes via Tottori airport: about 1 hour 10 minutes on ANA from Haneda, about 45 minutes by limousine bus to Kurayoshi station, then the local bus. Via Yonago airport, about 3 hours by car or by train and bus.
- **From Okayama.** About 1 hour 50 minutes by car, or 2 hours 55 minutes by highway bus.
- **From Hiroshima.** About 3 hours 20 minutes by car, or 3 hours 55 minutes by highway bus, then the local bus from Kurayoshi station.

Tottori’s tourism federation gives about 20 minutes by car from JR Kurayoshi, about 60 minutes from Tottori station, about 70 minutes from Yonago station, and about 60 minutes from Innoshō interchange on the Chūgoku expressway. The association uploaded a walking map as a PDF in June 2026. Check the bus timetable before you travel. For Tamatsukuri, further west in Shimane, see the [Tamatsukuri guide](/en/soak/tamatsukuri/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Misasa is a radium spring."** Officially it is a radioactive spring, decided by radon. "Radium onsen" is the customary name, as the 2015 review notes.
- **"Misasa has the most radon in the world."** The association says "one of the world’s leading". The 1914 figure was the second-highest known at the time.
- **"Misasa’s radon is 474 Bq/L."** That is the average of 81 sources in a 1983 survey, reported in a 2015 review. I found no newer official figure.
- **"The hottest source is 89.3°C" or "76°C".** The same review gives both, in its body and its abstract.
- **"The Kawara-buro is open around the clock."** Except on the mornings of odd-numbered days, when it is cleaned.
- **"The spring opened in 1164, full stop."** That year comes from the white wolf legend.
- **"Radon bathing is proven to be good or bad for you."** This page takes no side. What official bodies say is in the [radioactive springs guide](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/).
