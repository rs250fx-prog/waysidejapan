Minakami is a town in Gunma on the upper reaches of the Tone river, below Mount Tanigawa. "Minakami Onsen" can mean two things: the onsen town by Minakami station, and, more loosely, the whole spread of springs in Minakami town, which the tourism association calls the "18 springs of Minakami". This page explains both, using the Minakami tourism association, the environment ministry, Gunma prefecture, JNTO, and the own sites of the inn at Takaragawa and the Tanigawadake ropeway where they are the only source for a fact. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn or bath.

For what the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). Minakami’s neighbours in this series are [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/), [Ikaho](/en/soak/ikaho/) and [Shima](/en/soak/shima/).

## Eighteen springs, six health resorts

### The 18 springs

The tourism association says the name "18 springs of Minakami" comes from a time when the town had eighteen onsen areas with lodgings. Things have changed since, it says, but it keeps the name as a symbol of how many springs there are. The water varies, among them simple, alkaline simple, sulphur and sodium-calcium sulphate springs.

The association groups the Minakami area as Minakami, Tanigawa, Unose, Yubiso, Mukaiyama, Takaragawa, Kaminohara and Yunokoya. Minakami Onsen, the town by the station, is the centre of that group.

### Not a health resort onsen

The environment ministry has a plan for Minakami town as a *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, a national health resort onsen, but it covers six other areas: Kamimoku, Namezawa, Yujuku, Kawafuru, Sarugakyō and Hōshi. The tourism association gives the same six. Minakami Onsen, Yubiso, Tanigawa and Takaragawa are not among them.

That matters for the figures. The ministry’s plans (May 2018 and March 2024) give 24 sources, ten of them flowing naturally, and a total of more than 1,600 litres a minute. Those are the six health resort areas together, not Minakami Onsen or Takaragawa. I found no official count of sources, flow or temperature for Minakami Onsen itself; the town’s own website linked only to the tourism association.

## Where it came from

### Yubara no Yu

The tourism association says Minakami Onsen was once called Yubara no Yu. In 1889 Yubara village took in Fujiwara and Yago to become Minakami village, which became a town in 1947, and "Minakami Onsen" still means the old Yubara no Yu.

Its founding story is a legend. In the Eiroku era (1558–1570), the association says, the monk Kaiō of Kenmyōji temple saw white steam rising from a cliff by the Tone river. To make a way for the water, he wrote sutras on pebbles, set them in clay and piled them up, and the water rose. I found no record behind it.

### The railway

The association credits the railway. The Jōetsu line reached Minakami station in October 1928, when there were only two or three inns at Yubara. In September 1931 the 9,702 m Shimizu tunnel was finished and the line opened throughout, and Minakami grew into a large onsen town ranked alongside Kusatsu and Ikaho.

### The neighbours

- **Yubiso.** An old spring on the Yubiso river, a tributary of the Tone. The association says a powerful clan of the north, destroyed by the Minamoto, is said to have reached it and found the spring, and that the name comes from *yu ni hisomu*, "hiding in the water". After the war it was busy as a trailhead for Mount Tanigawa; now, the association says, six inns remain in a quiet onsen town. I found no climber numbers.
- **Takaragawa.** The association tells of Yamato Takeru, guided by a white hawk, healing himself here. The inn’s own site gives the legend too, but adds that no clear record or remains show when the spring was first used. It says the name comes from the river, which had a copper mine until early Shōwa, that it became a *tōji* place in the Taishō era, and that its main building dates from 1955.
- **Tanigawa.** The association and Gunma prefecture’s English guide give a legend of a princess who was an incarnation of the deity of Mount Fuji, and the name Omosuso no Yu.

The ministry’s plan says the town’s name comes from *Minakami Kikō*, a travel book by the poet Wakayama Bokusui, and that the town was formed in 2005 by joining Tsukiyono, Minakami and Niiharu.

## The water

### Two names for Minakami Onsen

The tourism association names the water of each area:

| Area | Tourism association |
|---|---|
| Minakami | Calcium-sodium sulphate-chloride |
| Tanigawa | Simple |
| Unose | Simple |
| Yubiso | Simple |
| Mukaiyama | Alkaline simple |
| Takaragawa | Simple |
| Kaminohara | Alkaline simple sulphur |
| Yunokoya | Simple |

Gunma prefecture’s list of springs by type, as of 31 March 2026, places both Minakami Onsen and Takaragawa in the simple-spring column. So the association and the prefecture disagree about Minakami Onsen. I read the prefecture’s column from the order of text in a PDF, not by eye, so it needs checking against the list, and I do not choose. Simple and sulphate springs are explained on the [page on sulphate and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). JNTO calls the water at Minakami Onsen clear and odourless.

### Why it is like this

The ministry’s plan for the six health resort areas says their water rose through green tuff, a rock that took in seawater components when the seabed was lifted, and that it is mainly sulphate water, mostly colourless and clear. That describes the area of the six, not Minakami Onsen, Takaragawa or the Tanigawa side. I found no explanation of their geology from the weather agency, the geological survey or the prefecture. Gunma prefecture’s English guide says, in general, that the prefecture’s mountains were shaped by volcanic activity and that most of its springs are groundwater heated by the earth.

## How the town works

### Spread out

Unlike Kusatsu, built around one source in the middle of town, Minakami is spread over a wide area. The tourism association describes Minakami Onsen as hotels and inns along the Tone from the station, Yubiso as near the Tanigawa trailhead, Takaragawa as a single inn in the upper Tone, and Tanigawa as a quiet spring looking up at the Mount Tanigawa ridge. Gunma prefecture’s English guide says Minakami is widely scattered and linked by bus routes. JNTO adds rafting and other river sports.

The association’s page for Minakami Onsen lists inns and phone numbers but no day-bathing fees, and I found no official description of shared baths there.

### Takaragawa’s open-air baths

Takaragawa is one inn, Ōsenkaku, but the tourism association, on more than one of its pages, and JNTO both single out its baths, so I describe them from those and the inn’s own pages.

- **Size.** The tourism association says the open-air baths, fed by four sources, cover about 470 tatami mats in four large baths, all *kakenagashi*. The inn says the same: four baths, 470 mats in all, among them Maka no Yu, Hannya no Yu, Kodakara no Yu (200 mats) and Maya no Yu (100 mats), fed constantly from four wells.
- **"Biggest in Japan."** The association calls it "said to be the biggest in Japan" and JNTO "said to have the largest open-air bath in Japan". Neither names who measured or chose it, and I found no such body.
- **Bathing wear.** The inn says its day-bathing fee includes Takaragawa’s own bathing wear, and that you may not wear your own. The baths are shared by men and women in that wear.
- **Day bathing.** The inn’s day-bathing page gives the hours and a four-hour limit. It shows prices, but its note that all prices include tax sits with the food menu, not the bath fees, so I do not give them.

The association says the baths are highly rated by visitors from overseas.

### The ropeway

The Tanigawadake ropeway, run by Hoshino Resorts, climbs from near Yubiso to Tenjindaira, which the prefecture’s guide puts at 1,319 m. As checked on 12 October 2026, the operator gives:

- **Season.** 18 April to 15 November 2026 (planned).
- **Ride.** About 15 minutes one way, seven at the fastest.
- **Hours.** 8:00–17:00 on weekdays, 7:00–17:00 at weekends and on holidays, last ride up 16:30.

The site shows fares, revised on 1 December 2025, but does not say whether they include tax, so I do not give them.

## Bathing there

- **Tattoos.** I found no official guidance for Minakami. The tourism association’s FAQ, in Japanese, does not mention tattoos, and its other languages are machine translation through an outside service. Takaragawa’s English page does not mention them either. Gunma prefecture’s general English guide says many onsen do not allow tattoos, that a small one may be fine covered with a plaster, and to check before you go. Ask each inn or bath in advance; the general picture is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Manners.** The same prefectural guide says to wash before getting in, keep the small towel out of the water, and not use cameras or phones in changing rooms or baths.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Minakami source that compares its water with baths in the cities.

## What it is eaten with

I found no official source that names a dish together with Minakami Onsen. The farm ministry’s list of Gunma dishes does not mention Minakami, Takaragawa or Tanigawa.

The tourism association’s "recommended food" lists soba, craft beer, fresh *dorayaki*, pork dishes, dam curry and apple pie. Its local brand, *Minakami Shugyoku*, includes Mizutsukiyo rice from the Tone headwaters, *gensen udon*, soba pasta, *amazake* and other local products. I found only the name *gensen udon*, "source udon", and no page explaining what it has to do with the hot springs, so I say nothing more about it. For Gunma’s dishes generally, see [Kantō’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/).

## Getting there

As checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Shinkansen.** Gunma prefecture’s access guide (March 2025) gives about 70 minutes from Tokyo to Jōmō-Kōgen by the Jōetsu shinkansen; JNTO gives 75 minutes from Tokyo or Takasaki; the tourism association says 62 minutes from Tokyo. They may measure from different places or trains, and I do not pick one.
- **Jōmō-Kōgen to Minakami station.** About 20 minutes by bus from stop 1, says the prefecture; 25 minutes, say JNTO and Takaragawa’s English page.
- **Local trains.** JNTO gives two hours on the Takasaki line to Takasaki, then an hour on the Jōetsu line to Minakami.
- **Takaragawa.** The prefecture gives about 30 minutes by bus from stop 4 at Minakami station to the Takaragawa-iriguchi stop, under three hours in all from Tokyo. The inn’s English page gives 30 minutes to Takaragawa-iriguchi or 35 to Takaragawa Onsen.
- **Tanigawa ropeway.** The prefecture gives about 45 minutes by bus from Jōmō-Kōgen to the ropeway station, under two hours from Tokyo.

On the pick-up from Takaragawa-iriguchi, the sources disagree. The prefecture’s guide says the inn’s free shuttle can be used by day visitors too. The inn’s Japanese day-bathing page says it does not pick up day visitors. Its English page mentions a shuttle that must be booked, and may be old. Check with the inn before you go.

The tourism association’s access page lists bus, taxi and car-hire phone numbers and links to a bus map and timetables, and says the town bus on the Hōshi line runs four times a day; I could not read times or fares on it.

## What people get wrong

- **"Minakami Onsen is a national health resort onsen."** The six designated areas are elsewhere in the town.
- **"The ministry’s figures describe Minakami Onsen."** The 24 sources and 1,600 litres a minute are the six health resort areas together.
- **"Takaragawa officially has Japan’s biggest open-air bath."** It is "said to be". The size both sides agree on is about 470 mats.
- **"Minakami grew because of its legend."** The tourism association credits the Jōetsu line, opened to Minakami in 1928 and through the Shimizu tunnel in 1931.
- **"There are eighteen onsen areas now."** The association says the name comes from an earlier count and things have changed.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
