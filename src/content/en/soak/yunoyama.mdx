Yunoyama Onsen is a hot-spring town in Komono, in the north of Mie prefecture, on the eastern foot of **Mt Gozaisho**, the main peak of the Suzuka range. A ropeway climbs the mountain, and the old temple of Sangaku-ji, burned in the 16th century, gave the place a history of warrior monks that still names its festival and its hotpot. This page uses Komono town, the Yunoyama Onsen association, Mie prefecture's research institute, Mie's tourism federation, Kintetsu, the ropeway company and JNTO. Where they disagree, both are given. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the spring types named below, see [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) and [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). For reading the analysis sign, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### Where it is

Komono town's page on the spring (updated 8 June 2026) places Yunoyama inside the **Suzuka Quasi-National Park**, on the eastern foot of Mt Gozaisho, 1,212 m high. Yunoyama is not on the environment ministry's list of national health resort hot springs; no area of Mie prefecture is.

### "Radium spring" and the official name

The town describes the water as an **alkaline radium spring (radioactive spring)**, colourless and clear, citing the Japan Onsen Association and its own page. Mie's tourism federation calls it an alkaline radium spring containing a large amount of emanation, also colourless.

"Radium spring" is a common name, not one of the environment ministry's spring types. Officially, a spring with enough radon is a **radioactive spring**, from 111 Bq per kilogram (8.25 Mache units). Both bodies' wording is the everyday name; why there is no official radium spring is explained in the [radioactive springs guide](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/).

Official sources do not give the number of sources, flow, temperature or pH for Yunoyama.

### Why it counts as onsen

A 2006 paper in the annual report of Mie prefecture's Health and Environment Research Institute says Yunoyama's water is treated as onsen because its **temperature, radon concentration and fluoride-ion concentration** are above the standards in the Hot Springs Act. It notes that the area has many sources, including relatively new deep-drilled ones, without giving a number.

### How the water rises

The same paper reports seven months of continuous monitoring, from July 2005, of three old sources that flow by themselves. Its authors suggest this model:

- The water is groundwater **under pressure**, travelling through **cracks in weathered Suzuka granite**.
- Deep down, a fluid rich in chloride and fluoride ions mixes in; near the surface, a fluid rich in nitrate and sulphate ions. The two mix as the water rises.
- A type of Suzuka granite is widespread around Yunoyama, and the authors **presume** the radon dissolves out of the weathered granite.

They found no decline in the water's quality at the time, and wrote that there was not yet enough data to extend the model to other springs. The paper does not explain why the water is warm. This page gives it as the authors' model and goes no further.

### One source, many inns

Komono town's history walk (updated 16 December 2025) includes the **Yumoto**, "the source of Yunoyama Onsen", and says the water rising there is **distributed to the ryokan and hotels**. Beside it stand a monument to the priest Jōkun, who is said to have found the spring about 1,300 years ago through a revelation in a dream, and a small hall with a statue of Yakushi Nyorai, honoured as the guardian of the source. The town does not say whether every facility takes its water from the Yumoto.

## Where it came from

### Founding: the dates

The accounts agree on a legend and differ slightly on the date:

- **The Yunoyama Onsen association's guide**: the town has continued "since the discovery of the spring in the Yōrō era (717–723)".
- **The association's page for the 1,300th anniversary** (April 2018 to March 2019): the spring **is said to have been** found in **Yōrō 2 (718)** by the priest **Jōkun**, guided by a revelation from Yakushi Nyorai. From a legend that a wounded deer bathed there, it is also called **Shika-no-yu**, "the deer's bath".
- **Komono town** (updated 8 June 2026): around Yōrō 2 (718), the founding tradition, citing Mie's prefectural history, the association and the history walk.

These are traditions, and this page gives them as such.

### Revival, war and the ropeway

The association says Sangaku-ji was burned in Oda Nobunaga's invasion of Ise and the spring went into decline, then revived as an onsen inn town in the **Edo period**. Komono town gives the year: in **Jōkyō 4 (1687)** inns were set up and the town revived as a spring resort.

Both then give a similar story. Around **1877**, during the Satsuma Rebellion, Yunoyama served as a **temporary sanatorium for wounded soldiers** (the town's list; the association names the war). The association adds that in **1950** the mountain-climbing events of the Aichi National Sports Festival were held here and made the name known across Japan, and that the opening of the **Gozaisho Ropeway in 1959** brought it popularity. The town lists **2018** as the 1,300th anniversary of the founding.

### Sangaku-ji and the warrior monks

A report on Mie's tourism federation site (undated) says Sangaku-ji is said to have had **about 300 armed warrior monks** (*sōhei*), and that it was burned in **1568** during Oda Nobunaga's invasion of Ise. The association's site has a story set in the Edo period, the "legend of the love-knot paper crane", in which warrior monks appear. It is presented as a tale.

## How the town works

### A mountain resort near the city

The association describes Yunoyama as a spring town close to the cities that has kept its natural setting, also called the **"Kansai no oku-zashiki"**, the inner parlour of Kansai. It says that from Mt Gozaisho you can see Lake Biwa and Ise Bay, and on clear days Mt Fuji in the distance.

Walking paths lead through the town to **Sangaku-ji**, the **Sōtaki falls** and the **Ōishi** boulder (association). Komono town's **history walk** links 10 points over about 3 km, about 2 hours on foot.

### The Gozaisho Ropeway

The ropeway company describes its line as "a 15-minute walk in the air" over a wide panorama. At **9:00 on 13 October 2026**, its site said the ropeway was running at one-minute intervals and the sightseeing lift was also running. Komono town refers visitors to the company for operating times and fares. JNTO says the ropeway runs from the foot of the 1,212 m mountain to the summit park. Fares are not given here; check the company's site.

### The Sōhei Matsuri

The association announced the **55th Sōhei Matsuri**, the warrior-monk festival, for **4 October 2026**, and the ropeway company's news gave the same date. As of 13 October 2026 it has passed; check the association's site for the next one.

### Day bathing

The association keeps a list of inns and baths that take day visitors, with a price table. The prices are published without saying whether they include tax, so they are not given here. Individual inns are outside this page.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos and English

The association's English page covers the spring, a list of inns and access, but not tattoos or how to bathe. Komono town's foreign-language pages are external links such as machine translation, and its Japanese pages say nothing on tattoos either. Official sources do not give a tattoo rule for Yunoyama. Ask each inn or bath before you go.

### Compared with city baths

No official source compares Yunoyama's water with baths elsewhere. What you can compare anywhere is the posted analysis sign; see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/) and [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Food

**Sōhei-nabe.** Mie's tourism federation describes a hotpot that the warrior monks of Sangaku-ji at Yunoyama **are said to have** eaten for stamina: mountain food and meat such as boar, simmered in several kinds of miso, served at inns in Yunoyama Onsen, among other places. A report on the same site (undated) describes local pork, boar, venison and other game, mushrooms and vegetables cooked in a broth of miso and sake lees, and prints the "nine rules of sōhei-nabe".

The description comes from the tourism federation alone. Komono town's own pages do not describe the dish, and it is not in the farm ministry's list of local dishes. What ties it to the town is the warrior-monk story it shares with the association's Sōhei Matsuri. More in the [Kinki local dishes guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kinki/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Komono town.** From Kintetsu's **Yunoyama-Onsen station**, about 10 minutes by local bus. By car, about 10 minutes from the Komono interchange on the Shin-Meishin expressway, or about 20 to 30 minutes from the Yokkaichi interchange on the Higashi-Meihan. The town asks you to check each operator's latest information.
- **The association's English page** (undated):
  - Kintetsu-Nagoya to Kintetsu-Yokkaichi, limited express, about 30 minutes.
  - Kintetsu Osaka-Namba to Kintetsu-Yokkaichi, limited express, about 120 minutes.
  - Kintetsu-Yokkaichi to Yunoyama-Onsen, local train, about 30 minutes.
  - Yunoyama-Onsen station to Yunoyama Onsen and the Gozaisho Ropeway, Mie Kōtsū bus or taxi, about 8 minutes.
  - From the Meitetsu Bus Centre in Nagoya, a direct Mie Kōtsū bus, about 60 minutes.
- **Kintetsu's timetable** (shown as of 22 February 2025) has, for example, a weekday local train leaving Kintetsu-Yokkaichi at 13:02 and reaching Yunoyama-Onsen at 13:28, 26 minutes.
- **JNTO** (undated) gives about 1 hour by Kintetsu from Nagoya and about 2.5 hours from Osaka-Namba or Kyoto, and about 1 hour 20 minutes from Nagoya to the ropeway by train, bus and walking.

Times from Osaka and Kyoto come only from the association's English page and JNTO. Check times with the operators before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Radium spring is the official type."** It is a common name. The official type is the radioactive spring, which the town gives in brackets.
- **"The founding date is fixed."** The association gives the Yōrō era (717–723) and 718; the town says "around 718". Both present it as a tradition.
- **"The town has run without a break since 718."** The association says it declined after Sangaku-ji was burned and revived in the Edo period; the town dates the revival to 1687.
- **"It must be volcanic."** The prefecture's research model has groundwater rising through cracks in weathered granite, and does not explain the heat.
- **"Sōhei-nabe is an official local dish."** The tourism federation describes it; the town's pages and the farm ministry's list do not.
