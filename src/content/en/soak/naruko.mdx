Naruko, in Miyagi, is not one town but five hot spring districts that share a name. Between them they have most of the official spring types, a volcano with an acidic crater lake, a long history of *tōji* (staying at a spring for days or weeks), and the squeaking wooden dolls called kokeshi. This page explains how it works using the tourism associations, the city, the prefecture, the environment ministry, the weather agency and JNTO. The fullest single source is the environment ministry’s plan for Naruko as a national health resort onsen, dated March 2025. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what the spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the posted notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on *kakenagashi*. The Honshu hot springs guide covers Naruko’s designation as a national health resort onsen.

## Where it came from

### The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Naruko as a volcano made of an indistinct caldera about 7 km across and a group of lava domes. Inside the domes lies Katanuma, a crater lake about 400 m across that is acidic, with active steaming around it. The only activity on record is a steam-driven (phreatic) eruption around Katanuma on 23 May 837 (the fourth year of Jōwa). The agency’s page does not mention hot springs.

### The springs and 837

Several sources tie the springs to that year, and they name the mountain differently.

- **The environment ministry’s plan** says that, according to the *Shoku Nihon Kōki*, Toyagamori behind Naruko Onsen exploded in 837, and hot water then welled up in each place except Onikobe.
- **The Naruko Onsen district’s tourism association** says the same chronicle records a great explosion of Katayama in 837 that brought out hot water.
- **Miyagi’s tourism federation**, on its page for Onsen Shrine, calls the spot the place where boiling water burst out in an eruption of Toyayama.
- **JNTO** says the springs were first discovered during a volcanic eruption in 837.

I have not read the chronicle itself, so this is what these bodies say it records. I found no official statement that Toyagamori, Katayama, Toyayama and the weather agency’s "around Katanuma" are the same place, and this site does not choose.

**Onsen Shrine.** The district association says the shrine was built to calm the hot water that burst out in 837. Miyagi’s tourism federation gives its founding as 835. The two do not agree, and I do not pick one.

**How old.** The ministry’s plan and the Naruko Onsenkyo association speak of a thousand years. The association’s walking-course page says 1,200 years. The district association dates the first inns to around the middle of the Edo period.

### When each district began

The ministry’s plan gives the districts’ beginnings, mostly as "it is said" traditions:

- **Naruko Onsen** — the 837 eruption. Its Taki no Yu is the shrine’s sacred water, with a thousand years of history.
- **Higashi-Naruko** — born in the same eruption as Naruko Onsen.
- **Kawatabi** — on one page, the first district to open.
- **Nakayamadaira** — about 300 years ago.
- **Onikobe** — said to have been known by the 270s, with a bath opened by the Fujiwara of Hiraizumi in 1182 to 1189 and later used by the Date family.

The same plan, on another page, says Onikobe is the oldest, followed by Kawatabi, Naruko, Higashi-Naruko and Nakayamadaira. The plan contradicts itself on which came first, and I leave both.

### Onikobe’s geysers

The Agency for Cultural Affairs lists the "Mekama and Okama intermittent hot springs of Onikobe" as a national natural monument, designated on 13 April 1933. Its description, written at that time, says Mekama spouted every 15 to 30 minutes and Okama every 10 to 15. Miyagi prefecture’s page heads it "special natural monument", which does not match the agency’s listing, so I use the agency’s term. The prefecture says both vents were buried by a landslide in Typhoon Ione in 1948, that restoration work steadied the flow, and that it has now stopped completely, for reasons not well understood.

The **Onikobe Kanketsusen** that visitors go to see is a separate attraction. Miyagi’s tourism federation says its geyser Benten spouts about 15 m every 10 minutes or so. The pages I read do not say whether its eruptions are natural or managed, or how it relates to the national monument. The federation lists 10:00 to 15:00, closed Tuesday to Thursday, open April to November; Osaki city says to check the attraction’s own site. Its admission is not given here because the tax status is not stated.

### The "three famous springs of Ōshū"

The tourism association and Osaki city say Naruko has been counted among the *Ōshū sanmeitō*, the three famous springs of the old north-east. Neither says who chose them or names the other two, and I could not confirm any official selection.

### Bashō and the post towns

The ministry’s plan says Naruko is known from Matsuo Bashō’s *Oku no Hosomichi*, with a stone carved with his verse and the site of the Shitomae barrier. In the Edo period the area was a junction of roads into Dewa, with post stations at Shitomae, Kajiyazawa, Nakayama and Onikobe. About 5.2 km from the Shitomae barrier to Sakaida in Yamagata has been laid out as a walking trail. The association says the barrier was a checkpoint on the Sendai domain’s border and has a statue of Bashō. The official pages I read do not give the year he passed or quote the poem, so I do not either.

### Long stays

The ministry’s plan says the five districts were old *tōji* places where farmers came in the slack season for stays of several nights, and that visitors rose when the railway arrived in the Taishō era, with leisure trips growing after the war. Higashi-Naruko is still the district where most self-catering *tōji* remains, but the plan says long self-catering stays have been declining with changes in how people live. It proposes a "modern *tōji*": shorter stays, with meals provided, and time spent on local nature, history, farming and crafts. The association says Higashi-Naruko is thought to have had a *goten-yu*, a bath for the Date family of the Sendai domain; the plan places it at the Akaume no Yu.

The plan’s figures show the change. Visitors peaked at about 4 million in 1991 and were about 1.75 million in 2019; overnight guests peaked at about 1.35 million in 1989 and were about 530,000 in 2019. Inns fell from 130 in 1996 to 63 in 2020.

## The water

### Seven of the ten types

Osaki city lists the seven modern spring types found in Naruko: simple, hydrogen carbonate, chloride, sulphate, iron-bearing, sulphur and acidic. The missing three are carbon dioxide, iodine-bearing and radioactive. The ministry’s plan and the tourism association say the same.

Miyagi’s tourism federation says eight of eleven. That is out of the old classification of eleven, not the modern ten, and it does not list which eight, so I could not match it to the seven.

### How many sources

The figure depends on who gives it, and I do not pick one.

- **Osaki city** says about 370.
- **The ministry’s plan** says about 380, used and unused. Its table, from Miyagi prefecture’s survey as of 31 March 2021, totals 377: 232 in use and 145 unused.
- **Miyagi’s tourism federation** and **JNTO** say about 400.

Only the plan says when and how it counted.

### District by district

The plan’s table, as of 31 March 2021, gives one main type per district. That does not mean a district has no other types, and I found no official list of every type in each district.

- **Naruko Onsen** — 158 sources, 23.8 to 98.2°C; mainly sulphur-bearing sodium sulphate-chloride springs.
- **Higashi-Naruko** — 50 sources, 16.7 to 72.7°C; mainly sodium hydrogen carbonate springs.
- **Kawatabi** — 32 sources, 29.2 to 56.8°C; mainly sulphur-bearing sodium hydrogen carbonate springs.
- **Nakayamadaira** — 82 sources, 40.4 to 74.1°C; mainly sulphur-bearing sodium hydrogen carbonate springs.
- **Onikobe** — 55 sources, 27.0 to 98.1°C; mainly simple springs.

The plan says some steam sources and sources that are hard to measure have not been measured.

### Steam and scale

The plan says more than half the sources are steam, and that hot water of 90 to 100°C is made from them as needed and used at inns, public baths, hospitals and footbaths. Some baths take one source on its own; others mix several. It does not say how this relates to the notices about added water or heating.

It also says Naruko Onsen and Nakayamadaira have many high-temperature and steam sources and a lot of scale, which has to be removed repeatedly, with changes in flow. Kawatabi to Higashi-Naruko has no scale and little change through the year. Measurable sources are checked for temperature and flow once a year.

## How the town works

### Five districts, five associations

The five districts are Naruko Onsen, Higashi-Naruko, Kawatabi, Nakayamadaira and Onikobe. Each has its own tourism association, and the Naruko Onsenkyo association brings them together and runs the website. The association describes them like this:

- **Naruko Onsen** — the largest of the five and the centre.
- **Higashi-Naruko** — the district of *tōji* inns, along a street of about 300 m.
- **Kawatabi** — on the Arao river, at the entrance to the area.
- **Nakayamadaira** — its alkaline water is known as *unagi-yu*, "eel water".
- **Onikobe** — upstream of the Naruko dam, inside a quasi-national park.

The ministry’s plan says the first four lie in the Naruko caldera along the JR Rikuu East line and Route 47, while Onikobe is scattered in the separate Onikobe caldera north of the dam. The area is inside Kurikoma Quasi-National Park. Osaki city’s Onikobe bus line runs from Naruko-Onsen station to Tanohara, six round trips on weekdays (update of 14 August 2026).

### The shared baths

The tourism association lists seven baths open to day visitors in the five districts. The hours below are as the page shows them, checked on 11 October 2026; the page has no date.

- **Taki no Yu** (Naruko Onsen) — 7:30 to 21:00.
- **Waseda Sajiki-yu** (Naruko Onsen) — 9:00 to 21:30.
- **Hare Onsen** (Higashi-Naruko) — Monday to Wednesday 9:00 to 17:00, Friday to Sunday and holidays 9:00 to 19:00; closed Thursday.
- **Kawatabi Onsen communal bath** — 9:00 to 17:00, from 13:30 on Monday and Friday.
- **Kami no Yu Hygge Kawatabi** — 10:00 to 17:00; closed Monday and Thursday (open on holidays, closed the next day).
- **Shintoro no Yu** (Nakayamadaira) — 9:00 to 20:30.
- **Spa Onikobe no Yu** — 10:00 to 18:00; closed in winter, mid-November to late April.

Entry ends 30 minutes before closing. The same list also has Akakura Yukemuri-kan, across the border in Yamagata. The ministry’s plan, from March 2025, speaks of five public baths; I do not know whether it counts differently or the number has since grown. The plan lists Taki no Yu as a city-owned day bathing facility and Waseda Sajiki-yu as privately owned. The association’s page lists fees, but I have not given them because it does not say they include tax, and it gives no bathing manners.

The ministry’s plan and JNTO both say most inns and hotels take day bathers. Osaki city lists a hand bath at the Yumeguri plaza in front of Naruko-Onsen station and the Shimojigoku footbath; the plan also names the Poppo footbath. The district association says the footbaths and hand baths by the station are free.

### The bath-hopping ticket, on hold

The association has sold a sticker ticket, *yumeguri chiketto*. It comes as seven kinds of sticker, covers the bath only (not rest areas), and is valid for six months from the day of sale. It can be used in seven districts: the price list names Naruko, Higashi-Naruko, Kawatabi, Nakayamadaira and Onikobe, plus Akakura and Semi in Yamagata. The ministry’s plan says the scheme is run with the Mogami town tourism association in Yamagata. The page notes that you can pay cash instead and that at some baths cash is cheaper. The price is shown only in images, and the page does not say whether it includes tax.

From 30 April 2026 the association stopped selling the ticket "for the time being" to review the scheme, and says a restart will be announced. Stickers already sold can be used within their six months. The home page still carried a "sales suspended" notice on 11 October 2026.

### Kokeshi

The sources tell the origin three ways, and I give each.

- **The tourism association and Osaki city** trace them to the Bunka and Bunsei years (1804 to 1830), when woodturners in the mountains made them as toys for children. The city says the start cannot be dated and is estimated at the late Edo period from records and tradition; a record of 1862 calls them *kofukeshi*.
- **Miyagi prefecture** says traditional kokeshi are said to have been born around the end of the Edo period at hot spring towns in Tōhoku, as souvenirs for children.
- **The ministry’s plan** says demand for souvenirs from *tōji* visitors brought up woodturners and lacquerers, forming Naruko kokeshi and Naruko lacquerware. It is the only official source I found linking kokeshi to *tōji*.

The association says about 50 craftspeople still make Naruko kokeshi by hand. The squeak when you turn the head comes from the way the head is fitted into the body, and the typical patterns are layered chrysanthemum (*kasane-giku*) and diamond chrysanthemum (*hishi-giku*). Osaki city says the name "kokeshi" was settled in 1940 at a meeting of the Tokyo Kokeshi Society. Miyagi’s traditional kokeshi, Naruko among the prefecture’s five lines, were designated a national traditional craft on 22 June 1981.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The association’s site offers English, Chinese, Cantonese and Korean, but the links go through Google Translate. There is no separately written English version.
- **Manners and tattoos.** I could not find official guidance for Naruko on bathing manners or tattoos, on the association’s, the city’s or JNTO’s Naruko pages, or in the ministry’s plan. JNTO’s general onsen guide (20 June 2024) says tattoos are very much case by case, that a private bath booked in advance is the surest option, and that you should ask each place directly. Do that for each inn or bath.
- **Winter.** There is no district-wide winter closure notice. The association’s sightseeing page lists Naruko gorge and Katanuma as closed in winter and the Japan Kokeshi Museum as closed for the winter; Spa Onikobe no Yu closes from mid-November to late April.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish that both the town side and the farm ministry tie to Naruko. The ministry’s list of Miyagi’s 29 local dishes has no entry naming Naruko, Osaki or onsen, and I could not find *suzuko-meshi* on any official page I opened. On the town side, Osaki city calls *kuri dango*, a chestnut rice cake in a sweet soy glaze, a standard souvenir of Naruko Onsen; the ministry does not list it. The ministry’s plan mentions *doburoku* (unfiltered sake) brewed under a special zone licence and served at inns and restaurants.

## Getting there

Checked on 11 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Tokyo to Furukawa.** About 2 hours by Tohoku shinkansen, says Osaki city. The tourism association gives Tokyo to Sendai about 120 minutes and Sendai to Furukawa about 15 minutes.
- **Furukawa to Naruko-Onsen.** By the JR Rikuu East line, the tourism association says about 45 minutes (its page has no date); Miyagi’s tourism federation says about 50 minutes (updated 28 March 2023). I could not open JR East’s timetable, so I do not choose.
- **By bus from Sendai.** Miyagi’s tourism federation gives the Miyako Bus highway service at about 1 hour 30 minutes, and JNTO about 90 minutes from stop 24 in front of Sendai station. The tourism association’s current access page lists no direct bus from Sendai, only local buses to Furukawa and Onikobe. I could not confirm whether the direct bus runs now; check with the operator.
- **Car.** The association gives about 370 km from Urawa interchange to Furukawa interchange on the Tohoku expressway, then about 28.5 km on Route 47. It gives no total time.

**The line to Shinjō is suspended.** JR East’s service information (19:15, 11 October 2026) says the Rikuu East line between Naruko-Onsen and Shinjō has been suspended "for the time being" since heavy rain in July 2024, with replacement buses. It did not show the Furukawa to Naruko-Onsen section as suspended. The association’s access page still gives Shinjō to Naruko-Onsen as about 60 minutes by train, and a route from Yamagata airport via Shinjō; those train times do not apply while the section is closed. I found no date for reopening.

**Autumn.** The association’s temporary *Kōyō-gō* bus runs from Naruko-Onsen station via Naruko gorge to Nakayamadaira-Onsen station, from 26 October to 8 November 2026. Timetables are on its Japanese and English PDFs.

## What people get wrong

- **"Naruko is one hot spring."** It is five districts, each with its own association.
- **"Only JNTO says the springs began in 837."** The environment ministry’s plan and the Naruko Onsen district association say so too, citing an old chronicle. The weather agency records an eruption at Katanuma in 837 but does not mention hot springs.
- **"There are 400 sources."** JNTO and Miyagi’s tourism federation say about 400; the ministry’s plan about 380 (377 in its table); Osaki city about 370.
- **"Seven types or eight — one of them is wrong."** Seven is out of the modern ten; eight is out of the old eleven.
- **"The Onikobe geysers are still erupting."** The national monument, Mekama and Okama, has stopped, the prefecture says. The geyser visitors see is a separate attraction.
- **"Naruko is officially one of the three famous springs."** The association and the city use the phrase without naming who chose them.
- **"The ticket’s seven districts are a mistake."** They are the five Naruko districts plus Akakura and Semi in Yamagata. Sales have been suspended since 30 April 2026.
- **"You can take the train on to Shinjō."** That section has been suspended since July 2024, with replacement buses.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
