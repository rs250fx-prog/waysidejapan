Naruko, in Miyagi, is not one town but five hot spring districts that share a name. Between them they have most of the official spring types, a volcano with an acidic crater lake, a long history of *tōji* (staying at a spring for days or weeks), and the squeaking wooden dolls called kokeshi. This page explains how it works using the tourism association, the city, the weather agency and JNTO. Where they disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what the spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the posted notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on *kakenagashi*. The Honshu hot springs guide covers Naruko’s designation as a national health resort onsen and the two different counts of its spring types.

## Where it came from

### The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Naruko as a volcano made of an indistinct caldera about 7 km across and a group of lava domes. Inside the domes lies Katanuma, a crater lake about 400 m across that is acidic, with active steaming around it. The only activity on record is a steam-driven (phreatic) eruption around Katanuma on 23 May 837 (the fourth year of Jōwa). The agency’s page does not mention hot springs and gives no cause for the eruption.

JNTO’s English page says the hot springs were first found at Katanuma during that eruption in 837. JNTO is the only source I found that connects the eruption to the discovery of the water. The tourism association gives no founding year for any district, and I could not find the 837 date in the association’s or the city’s pages, nor whether other accounts exist. JNTO also speaks of a thousand years of history.

### The "three famous springs of Ōshū"

The tourism association says Naruko has been counted among the *Ōshū sanmeitō*, the three famous springs of the old north-east. It does not say who chose them, and I could not confirm any official selection.

### Long stays

The association says Higashi-Naruko has long been valued as a place for *tōji* and still has traditional *tōji* inns. Osaki city, which Naruko belongs to, says you can choose a self-catering inn for a long, quiet stay. The association also says Higashi-Naruko is thought to have had a *goten-yu*, a bath for the Date family of the Sendai domain, in the Edo period.

## The water

The tourism association says seven of the ten modern spring types rise in Naruko. The three missing are carbon dioxide, iodine-bearing and radioactive springs. It does not say which types are found in which district.

How many sources there are depends on the source of the figure:

- **Osaki city** says about 370.
- **JNTO** says about 400.

Neither says how or when it counted, and the tourism association’s spring page gives no number. I do not pick one.

## How the town works

### Five districts, five associations

The five districts are Naruko Onsen, Higashi-Naruko, Kawatabi, Nakayamadaira and Onikobe. Each has its own tourism association, and the Naruko Onsenkyo association brings them together and runs the website. The association describes them like this:

- **Naruko Onsen** — the largest of the five and the centre.
- **Higashi-Naruko** — the district of *tōji* inns, along a street of about 300 m.
- **Kawatabi** — on the Arao river, at the entrance to the area.
- **Nakayamadaira** — its alkaline water is known as *unagi-yu*, "eel water".
- **Onikobe** — upstream of the Naruko dam, inside a quasi-national park.

### The shared baths

The tourism association lists seven baths open to day visitors, at least one in each district: Taki no Yu and Waseda Sajiki-yu in Naruko Onsen, Hare Onsen in Higashi-Naruko, two in Kawatabi, Shintoro no Yu in Nakayamadaira, and Spa Onikobe no Yu in Onikobe. It calls Taki no Yu the sacred water of Naruko Onsen Shrine. Entry ends 30 minutes before closing. The list marks Spa Onikobe no Yu as closed in winter; that is one facility’s notice, not a district-wide closure.

The page does not explain who owns or runs these baths, and it gives no bathing manners. It does list fees, but I have not given them, because I could not confirm they include tax.

JNTO adds that free footbaths are dotted around the town and that many hotels take day bathers.

### The bath-hopping ticket, on hold

The association has sold a sticker ticket, *yumeguri chiketto*. It comes as seven kinds of sticker, covers the bath only (not rest areas), and is valid for six months from the day of sale. The page says it can be used in "seven districts", which I could not square with the five districts above. It also notes that you can pay cash instead and that at some baths cash is cheaper. The price is shown only in an image I could not read as text.

From 30 April 2026 the association stopped selling the ticket for a review of the scheme, and says a restart will be announced. The home page also carried a "sales suspended" notice when I checked on 10 October 2026.

### Kokeshi

The association’s crafts page says about 50 craftspeople still make Naruko kokeshi by hand. The squeak when you turn the head comes from the way the head is fitted into the body, and the typical patterns are layered chrysanthemum (*kasane-giku*) and diamond chrysanthemum (*hishi-giku*). It traces them to the Bunka and Bunsei years, about 200 years ago, when woodturners in the mountains made them as toys for children; later they became souvenirs and then collectors’ pieces. Miyagi’s traditional kokeshi, Naruko among them, were designated a national traditional craft in 1981. The page does not link kokeshi to *tōji*, and I have not either.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The association’s site offers English, Chinese, Cantonese and Korean, but the links go through Google Translate. There is no separately written English version.
- **Manners and tattoos.** I could not find official guidance for Naruko on bathing manners, tattoos or winter closures. JNTO’s Naruko page does not cover them either. Ask each inn or bath in advance about tattoos, and consider a private bath where there is one.
- **Autumn.** The association’s home page announced a temporary "Kōyō-gō" bus for the Naruko gorge in the autumn leaf season. I did not read the details.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish that both the town side and the farm ministry tie to Naruko. The ministry’s list of Miyagi’s local dishes has no entry naming Naruko, Osaki or onsen, and the tourism association and JNTO pages I read do not pair a dish with the springs.

## Getting there

As checked on 10 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Rail, by the tourism association.** From Tokyo by Tohoku shinkansen to Sendai about 120 minutes; Sendai to Furukawa about 15 minutes; then the JR Rikuu East line to Naruko-Onsen station about 45 minutes. The page has no update date.
- **Rail and bus, by JNTO.** Tokyo to Sendai about 120 minutes by shinkansen; Sendai to Naruko Onsen about an hour by train, or about 90 minutes by highway bus from stop 24 in front of Sendai station.
- **Car.** The association gives about 370 km from Urawa interchange to Furukawa interchange on the Tohoku expressway, then about 28.5 km on Route 47. It gives no total time.

The association’s home page carried a notice about a suspension of the Rikuu East line between Naruko-Onsen and Shinjō, with replacement buses. That is not the Furukawa to Naruko-Onsen section described above, but check the notices before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Naruko is one hot spring."** It is five districts, each with its own association.
- **"The springs were found in 837."** That is JNTO’s account. The weather agency records an eruption at Katanuma in 837 but does not mention hot springs, and the tourism association gives no founding year.
- **"There are 400 sources."** JNTO says about 400; Osaki city says about 370.
- **"Naruko is officially one of the three famous springs."** The association uses the phrase without naming who chose them.
- **"You can buy the bath-hopping ticket."** Sales have been suspended since 30 April 2026.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
