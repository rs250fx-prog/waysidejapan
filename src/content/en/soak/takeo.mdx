Takeo is a hot spring town in Saga prefecture whose best-known building is its gate: a two-storey vermilion structure from 1914, designated with the bathhouse behind it as an Important Cultural Property. Behind the gate is a long written history, from an 8th-century gazetteer to a set of bathing rules from the time of Toyotomi Hideyoshi. This page uses Takeo city’s own guide and its 2015 exhibition pages, the Cultural Agency’s heritage database, the Takeo tourism association and JNTO. Where sources disagree, each is given. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how Takeo compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the [Kyushu hot springs overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/). For what a simple spring is, and why "simple" does not mean weak, see the page on [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/); for the notices about added water and recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). The neighbouring town of [Ureshino](/en/soak/ureshino/) is about 30 minutes away by JR bus.

## Where it came from

### The Fudoki

Takeo city’s guide, *Takeo Kenbunroku* (fiscal 2025 edition), says the *Hizen no Kuni Fudoki* records a place to the west of the district where hot water comes out, but where few people go because the rocks are very steep. The sources date the *Fudoki* differently:

- **The city’s guide**: the first half of the 8th century.
- **The city’s 2015 exhibition page**: around 730, adding that the spring is thought not to have been used much at the time.
- **The tourism association**: "1,300 years of history", written "about 1,300 years ago".

This page has not checked the *Fudoki* itself.

### Empress Jingū and a sword hilt

The city tells a legend in which Empress Jingū struck a rock with the hilt of her sword and clear water sprang up, so that the place came to be called Tsukasaki Onsen. The tourism association says only that she is said to have bathed here in old times. Ureshino has a different legend about the same empress, with a white crane; they are separate stories.

### Hideyoshi’s rules

During Hideyoshi’s invasions of Korea in the Bunroku and Keichō eras (1592–1598), soldiers came to Takeo to bathe. The city’s guide says Hideyoshi issued rules so that they would not trouble the people already there for a cure, and that the document is preserved. The sources name it differently:

- **The city’s guide**: *Toyotomi Hideyoshi Tsukasaki Onsen okitegaki*, Hideyoshi’s regulations for Tsukasaki Onsen.
- **The tourism association**: a vermilion-sealed letter, *nyūyoku kokoroe*, "bathing rules".

The city’s 2015 exhibition page adds a later layer: an old document of 1686 sets out rules for local residents in dealing with bathers, and bathing regulations were posted at the gate of the bathhouse.

### Siebold, and the lords’ baths

The city’s guide says Siebold’s bath in Takeo, in **1826**, is described in his account of his journey to Edo, and that there are bathrooms named *Tonosama-yu* and *Karō-yu*, the lord’s bath and the senior retainer’s bath, in memory of the time. The exhibition page gives no year. Ureshino’s tourism association, citing the same journey, gives **1812** for Ureshino; the two do not agree.

The tourism association also says the swordsman Miyamoto Musashi, Siebold, the lord Date Masamune and the surveyor Inō Tadataka are all said to have bathed here.

For the lord’s bath itself, the sources differ in detail:

- **The tourism association**: an all-marble bath said to have been built in the mid-Edo period for the exclusive use of the Takeo Nabeshima family, the local lords.
- **The city**: in the Edo period the spring came under the Saga Nabeshima domain, and a bath for its exclusive use was built.

## The gate and the new hall

### What the Cultural Agency records

The Cultural Agency’s heritage database and its Bunka Isan Online give the following.

- **Name and status.** "Takeo Onsen new hall and gate", designated an **Important Cultural Property** (buildings) on **22 July 2005**.
- **The gate** is a two-storey wooden gate with a hip-and-gable roof of tiles, in the *ryūgū-mon* style, the style of the Dragon Palace gate.
- **Dates.** Built in **1914** (Taishō 3). The gate’s ridge was raised on 20 November 1914 and its opening ceremony held on 12 April 1915. The new hall’s ridge was raised on 6 August 1914.
- **Design.** The database gives "designed by the Tatsuno and Kasai office, built by Shimizu Mannosuke", and its commentary calls it valuable as one of the few Japanese-style buildings in which Tatsuno Kingo was involved. Its detailed notes say the new hall’s drawings carry a seal reading "planned by Dr Tatsuno Kingo and Kasai Manji".

The other sources word the design differently. Takeo city says Tatsuno Kingo "was involved in" the design; the tourism association calls it his design. Because the agency names the office, this page says he was involved rather than that he designed it alone.

### Details of the gate

- **Four zodiac animals.** The city and the tourism association say the four corners of the gate’s upper ceiling are carved with the rat, the rabbit, the horse and the rooster.
- **Tokyo station.** The tourism association says that together with the zodiac animals at Tokyo station, all twelve are complete. That is the association’s story; it is not confirmed by an official source.
- **Colour.** The city says the vermilion was restored in a 2013 repair.
- **Nails.** The tourism association says the gate is built without nails.

### The new hall

The city and the tourism association say the new hall’s conservation repair was completed in 2003 and that it has exhibits inside. The city says the repair uncovered a long-lost bathroom built for the Taishō emperor, which can now be seen.

### Visiting the gate

The tourism association says the gate can be visited from 9:00 to 10:00, last entry 9:30, taking about 20 minutes, closed on Tuesdays, with the visiting period extended to 31 March 2027. It says the visit includes a ticket for the Motoyu bath. It gives a fee, but not whether it includes tax, so this page does not give it.

## The water

### What kind

- **Takeo city**: a **simple spring** (hypotonic, weakly alkaline, high temperature), clear and soft to the touch.
- **The tourism association**: a "weakly alkaline simple spring" on its hot spring page, and an "alkaline simple spring" on its page about the city.

Official sources give no pH figure. The two wordings from the association differ, so this page does not choose between "weakly alkaline" and "alkaline".

### The city’s own sources

The city’s guide has a table for three **city-owned sources**, Ikenoue A, B and C, which supply water through a scheme that began in 1970. They are not the sources of Motoyu or the town’s other baths, and the figures should not be read as Takeo Onsen’s.

| Source | Temperature | Natural flow |
|---|---|---|
| A | 25.5°C | None |
| B | 30–36°C (43°C at the bottom of the well) | 120 tonnes a day |
| C | 22–28°C | 68 tonnes a day |

The city gives all three as alkaline simple springs. Its description of Takeo’s water as a whole, a high-temperature spring, does not match these temperatures, which is another reason not to treat them as the town’s. Official sources do not give the number of sources, the flow or the temperature for Takeo Onsen as a whole.

### Why here

Official sources, national, prefectural or city, do not explain how Takeo’s or Ureshino’s water forms. So this page does not call it a volcanic spring.

### "Bijin no yu"

The tourism association calls Takeo a *bijin no yu*, a "beauty’s spring", and describes the water as keeping you warm and suiting the skin. It gives no basis. This page does not claim the water does anything for your skin.

## How the town works

### Motoyu

The tourism association’s page for Motoyu, the old public bath, gives its hours as 6:30 to 23:45, last entry 23:00, and no closing day. It gives fees but not whether they include tax, so this page does not give them. It says people with tattoos, drunk people and people who break the rules are refused. That notice is for Motoyu only, not for the town.

### Other baths

The tourism association lists the town’s baths and inns under headings for public baths, drop-in baths and private baths. It gives prices for the private baths without a tax status, so this page does not give them.

## Bathing there

Official sources give no prices with a stated tax status for any bath in Takeo, no tattoo statement other than Motoyu’s, no English guidance and no comparison of the water with baths in the cities.

- **Tattoos.** Motoyu refuses people with tattoos, as above. The tourism association’s front page shows no English link or tattoo information. JNTO’s general guide (undated) says some onsen refuse people with tattoos, that you should check in advance, that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option, and that small cover sheets only suit very small tattoos. The wider picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Manners.** JNTO’s guide says to leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room, wash before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water, tie up long hair, not swim or splash, and not take phones or cameras in.

## What it is eaten with

Official sources tie no dish to the hot springs. The farm ministry’s material on Saga names Takeo and Ureshino as spa towns but does not link either to a dish, as the [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/) page notes.

The tourism association lists the city’s specialities: Saga beef, Wakakusu pork, wild boar, lemongrass, high-oleic soybeans, sweets such as Taihaku yuri yōkan and Rōmon senbei, and "black" products such as black rice. It says nothing about onsen eggs or hot-spring tofu. Its page about the city adds that the cooks of the inns compete with one another, and names a Saga beef sukiyaki *bentō* and Takeo Kitagata champon.

The association also describes Takeo as a pottery area, with stories of Korean potters, about 90 kilns, and recognition in April 2016 as part of the Japan Heritage story "Hizen, the home of Japanese porcelain". It does not connect the pottery to the springs.

## Getting there

As given on the tourism association’s access page, which shows no date, checked on 12 October 2026. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Hakata.** Limited express, **70 minutes** at the fastest. Ureshino’s tourism association gives the same train as about 60 minutes.
- **From Fukuoka Airport.** Subway to Hakata, then limited express, about **1 hour 22 minutes**.
- **From Nagasaki.** Nishi-Kyushu shinkansen, **23 minutes** at the fastest. The association says the line opened between Takeo-Onsen and Nagasaki on 23 September 2022.
- **From Saga.** Limited express, **24 minutes**.
- **From Nagasaki Airport.** Bus to Shin-Ōmura station, then the shinkansen. The page gives about 30 to 35 minutes; from the layout it is not clear whether that covers the whole journey, so check before relying on it.
- **From Saga Airport.** About 55 minutes by car.
- **From Huis Ten Bosch station.** Limited express, **42 minutes**.
- **By car.** About 5 minutes from the Takeo-Kitagata interchange to the town.

Official sources give no times from Kumamoto, Kagoshima-Chūō, Miyazaki or Oita.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tatsuno Kingo designed the gate."** The Cultural Agency gives the design as the Tatsuno and Kasai office and says he was involved. The city and the tourism association put it in different words.
- **"The Fudoki says Takeo was a busy spa."** The city’s reading is the opposite: a spring few people visited because the rocks were steep.
- **"The city’s source temperatures are Takeo’s water."** Those are three city-owned supply wells, not the town’s baths.
- **"Takeo and Ureshino share a legend."** Both tell a story of Empress Jingū, but they are different stories.
- **"No one in Takeo accepts tattoos," or "everyone does."** The only official statement is Motoyu’s refusal. Ask each bath.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications by spring type is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
