  - q: Is an acidic spring the same as water below pH 3?
    a: Not quite. An acidic spring is defined by hydrogen ion, 1 mg or more per kilogram. "Acidic, under pH 3" is a separate scale on the same sign. The two usually point the same way, but the spring name follows the hydrogen-ion figure.
  - q: Is there an official radium spring?
    a: No. In the environment ministry's analysis guidelines a radioactive spring is decided by radon alone, 111 Bq per kilogram or more, and none of the ministry's naming documents has a radium-spring type. "Radium spring" is a common name.
  - q: Is it safe to bathe in a radioactive spring?
    a: This site does not give a verdict. A database run for Japan's nuclear regulator says such springs are considered not to harm health in ordinary use, and a 2004 page by the Japan Atomic Energy Agency says there seems to be no need to worry about ordinary spa use.

Three of Japan's ten official spring types cause more confusion than the rest: the acidic spring, the sulphur spring and the radioactive spring. Each has a name that sounds alarming in English, each has a popular label that does not match the official one, and two of them come with real safety rules. This page goes deeper on those three, using the environment ministry's documents and a handful of public bodies. The ten types as a whole, and how to read the analysis sign, are in [the guide to onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). This page does not rank any spring and does not say what any water will do for you.

## What it is

### Three thresholds, two tiers

Japanese law has two tiers. The Hot Springs Act decides what counts as an onsen at all. Inside that, the ministry's *Guidelines for Mineral Spring Analysis* (revised 2014) define the therapeutic spring (*ryōyōsen*), and only a therapeutic spring gets one of the ten type names. For these three types the figures are as follows, per kilogram of water.

| Component | Onsen under the Act | Therapeutic spring (the type) |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen ion (H⁺) | 1 mg | 1 mg — acidic spring |
| Total sulphur | 1 mg | 2 mg — sulphur spring |
| Radon | 20 × 10⁻¹⁰ curie (74 Bq, 5.5 Mache units) | 30 × 10⁻¹⁰ curie (111 Bq, 8.25 Mache units) — radioactive spring |
| Radium salt (as Ra) | 1 × 10⁻⁸ mg | No entry |

So water with 1.5 mg of total sulphur is an onsen but not a sulphur spring. The 74 Bq figure you sometimes see quoted for radioactive springs is the onsen threshold, not the radioactive-spring one.

"Total sulphur" has a specific meaning in the guidelines: the sulphur in hydrogen sulphide ion (HS⁻), thiosulphate ion (S₂O₃²⁻) and free hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), added together.

### Acidic is decided by hydrogen ion, not pH

The acidic spring (*sansei-sen*) is defined by the amount of hydrogen ion. The sign also carries a pH band, set by the same guidelines: acidic under pH 3, weakly acidic 3 to under 6, neutral 6 to under 7.5, weakly alkaline 7.5 to under 8.5, alkaline 8.5 and over. These are two different measures. By my own arithmetic, 1 mg of hydrogen ion per kilogram works out at roughly pH 3, so they usually agree; that is not an official explanation, and the spring name follows the hydrogen-ion figure.

The Japan Hot Spring Association, a private body, adds a caution about the pH number itself: pH measured in a laboratory can differ from pH at the source, and in water containing hydrogen sulphide, oxidation to sulphate can change it.

### Sulphur, and the "hydrogen sulphide type"

The guidelines separate sulphur springs where the sulphur is mainly free hydrogen sulphide from those where it is not. The first get the label **"hydrogen sulphide type"** (*ryūka-suiso-gata*). The test is a comparison of molar concentrations: if free H₂S is greater than HS⁻ plus S₂O₃²⁻ combined, it is the hydrogen sulphide type. The rest carry no label.

Under the old names these were two separate types, the sulphur spring (*iō-sen*) and the hydrogen sulphide spring (*ryūka-suiso-sen*). The ministry's old-and-new name table folds both into today's sulphur spring, with "(hydrogen sulphide type)" added where it applies. The association's page describes sulphur springs as split into a "sulphur type" and a hydrogen sulphide type; that is the association's wording, and "sulphur type" is not a label in the guidelines.

### Radioactive means radon, and there is no radium spring

In the therapeutic classification only radon appears. Radium salt is in the Act's onsen table but has no line in the guidelines' therapeutic tables, and none of the guidelines, the old-and-new name table or the posting standard has a type called "radium spring". Radon of 8.25 to under 50 Mache units makes a spring "weakly radioactive"; 50 or more, "radioactive".

The guidelines even work out radium from radon. Their method measures radon, treats it as being in equilibrium with radium, and converts it to a radium-salt figure (radium salt in mg/L equals radon in Ci/L multiplied by 1,000).

"Radium spring" is still in descriptive use. Aomori prefecture explains the radioactive spring as onsen containing the natural radioactive substances radon and radium. A 2004 page by the Japan Atomic Energy Agency (JAEA) says many so-called radium springs mainly contain radon, with little radium. I could not find any official grouping of "Japan's three great radium springs".

## Reading the names

A long spring name is built in a fixed order, and these three types follow two rules.

- **Acidic gets no "containing".** In a salt spring, acidity goes at the front as *sansei-* (acidic-), as in "acidic-sodium-sulphate spring". The guidelines never write "containing acid".
- **Sulphur and radon get *gan-* (containing).** *Gan'iō-* (sulphur-containing) and *gan-hōshanō-* (radioactivity-containing). A water under 1,000 mg/kg of dissolved substances is a "simple sulphur spring" or "simple acidic spring" instead.
- **The order is fixed.** Hydrogen ion, then sulphur, then carbon dioxide, then radon, then iron, then iodide. The guidelines' own examples are "acidic, sulphur-containing sodium-sulphate spring" and "sulphur-containing, weakly radioactive alkaline simple onsen".

Put together, a name such as the one given for Kusatsu's Yubatake source, "acidic, sulphur-containing aluminium-sulphate·chloride onsen (hydrogen sulphide type) (acidic, hypotonic, high-temperature spring)", reads as: acidic by hydrogen ion, sulphur above 2 mg/kg and mainly as hydrogen sulphide, aluminium the main positive ion, sulphate then chloride the main negative ions, followed by the pH, tonicity and temperature bands.

## Where the water comes from

The public material I found is thinner than the folklore, and mostly specific to one place.

- **Acidic water and volcanic gas.** The Geological Survey of Japan explains the strongly acidic springs on the south slope of Satsuma-Iōjima as acidic fluid, formed when volcanic gas mixes with groundwater, dissolving volcanic rock as it flows underground. The tourism agency's text for Tamagawa says its strongly acidic water comes from volcanic gas, exists only near active volcanoes, and owes its acidity to hydrochloric and sulphuric acid from the gas. Both are single cases, not a general account of every acidic spring in Japan.
- **Sulphur.** The ministry's 2017 hydrogen sulphide guideline says the gas is released mainly in volcanic fumarole areas and onsen around volcanoes, but also from building sewage tanks and drains. It also notes that alkaline and weakly alkaline sulphur springs contain almost no hydrogen sulphide, carrying hydrogen sulphide ion and thiosulphate instead. I could not find an official general explanation of how sulphur springs away from volcanoes form.
- **Radon.** The ministry's radiation primer says radon is a radioactive noble gas produced by the alpha decay of radium, which is widespread underground; radon-222 comes from radium-226 in the uranium series and has a half-life of about 3.8 days. Granite contains relatively more uranium, thorium and potassium, and western Japan, where granite is often exposed, tends to have natural radiation about 1.5 times that of eastern Japan. That supports the chain "uranium series in granite, then radium, then radon". It does not support "granite areas always have radioactive springs", and I found no official explanation of how the radon gets into the spring water.

## The official indications and contraindications

The ministry's posting standard of 1 July 2014 lists these. They are reproduced here as what the standard lists, not as a promise; the same standard describes spa therapy as easing symptoms and restoring health over weeks, best under a doctor.

| Type | Bathing (official list) | Drinking (official list) | Type-specific contraindications (bathing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic | Atopic dermatitis, psoriasis vulgaris, impaired glucose tolerance (diabetes), superficial skin infections | None listed | Sensitive skin or mucous membranes; dry skin in older people |
| Sulphur | Atopic dermatitis, psoriasis vulgaris, chronic eczema, superficial skin infections (hydrogen sulphide type adds peripheral circulation disorders) | Impaired glucose tolerance (diabetes), high cholesterol | As acidic |
| Radioactive | Hyperuricaemia (gout), rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and others | None listed | None listed |

The standard defines a contraindication as a condition where even one bath or drink could cause harm, and says therapy under a doctor with specialist knowledge is not ruled out. The ministry's 2019 booklet adds that acidic and sulphur springs can cause *yu-tadare*, a skin inflammation from bathing, and that strongly acidic water stings the skin and tastes sour.

## Bathing in them

### Acidic springs, skin and metal

Aomori prefecture describes the acidic spring as strongly irritating to the skin. For drinking, the posting standard says water below pH 3 is diluted with fresh water to pH 3 or above, with no more than 100 mL a time.

On metal, the only official line I found is local. The tourism agency's text on Zao Onsen says the water is strongly acidic, pH 1.6 to 1.25, can oxidise and discolour metal, and that bathers should be careful with jewellery and piercings. Its Tamagawa text says the strongly acidic water slowly dissolves iron, aluminium and even the cement in concrete. I could not find a ministry or prefectural document telling bathers to remove jewellery, or explaining silver turning black in sulphur water.

### Sulphur springs and hydrogen sulphide

The ministry's 2017 guideline says almost all poisoning accidents caused by onsen are hydrogen sulphide. The gas is colourless, heavier than air and tends to collect low down, and smells of rotten eggs. Above 50 ppm it is said to be acutely toxic, potentially life-threatening. The guideline quotes this table from a ministry field office.

| ppm | Effect |
|---|---|
| 0.025 | Lowest level the nose can detect |
| 0.3 | Clearly smelt |
| 5–10 | Strong unpleasant smell |
| 20–50 | Eye inflammation |
| 50–150 | Headache, dizziness, nausea |
| 150–200 | Sense of smell paralysed; odour no longer noticed |
| 300 | Subacute poisoning (unconsciousness) |
| 700–800 | Unconsciousness without noticing the smell; life at risk within 30 minutes |
| 1,000–2,000 | Fainting, convulsions, breathing stops, death |

The guideline's key sentence for a visitor: even at low concentrations, breathing it for a long time paralyses the sense of smell, so **do not rely on smell** to judge whether hydrogen sulphide is present. 
Inside the bathhouse, the rules are the operator's. Since 1 October 2017, for water with total sulphur of 2 mg/kg or more, the facility must keep hydrogen sulphide in the bathroom air at or below 20 ppm 10 cm above the water and 10 ppm 70 cm above the floor, with at least two vents (one at floor level), the water surface above the floor and the inlet above the water. These are duties on the facility, not instructions to bathers. The guideline also notes that even an alkaline sulphur spring can give off hydrogen sulphide on its way to the bath; it cites a pH 8.8 spring where none was detected at the source but over 100 ppm was found in a storage tank and over 10 ppm at a bath inlet.

Outside, the guideline is blunter. Gas vents can release over 1,000 ppm, and digging into or stepping onto snow-covered ground can be fatal; in December 2005 a family of four died beside a car park next to an onsen inn. Managers of sources must fence and sign them, and geothermal ground also carries a risk of breaking through and being scalded. A fence or a sign is there for that reason.

For bathers in general, the posting standard says that if you feel dizzy or unwell, call for help, keep your head low and get out slowly.

### Radioactive springs, in the official words

No body I checked calls these springs either safe or dangerous outright, so this page does not either.

- The environmental radioactivity database run by the Japan Chemical Analysis Center for the Nuclear Regulation Authority says radioactive springs are considered not to harm health in ordinary use. It gives Tamagawa and Misasa as examples and no dose figures.
- JAEA's 2004 page says there seems to be no need to worry about health effects from ordinary radioactive-spring therapy. It also calls radon inhalation therapy an empirical, traditional folk remedy whose efficacy lacks sufficient scientific grounds, and says a survey of Misasa residents found no increase in lung cancer deaths but that more detailed study is needed. I did not find a newer official statement.
- A 2013 study of one inn using a radioactive spring in Komono, Mie, found 5.3–18.0% of the tank's radon left in the bath just after filling, and estimated 2.8–12.0 nanosieverts from breathing radon while bathing, excluding radon that had escaped into the air. It is one facility.
- As general background, not specific to onsen, the ministry's primer says radon and its decay products account for the largest share of exposure to natural radiation, and that inhaled radon is an issue for internal exposure of the lungs.

The posting standard has no bathing precaution specific to radioactive springs.

### Rinsing afterwards

The posting standard's general advice is not to rinse off the onsen components, but to towel dry, dress, keep warm and rest about 30 minutes. It adds that people with sensitive skin are better off rinsing with warm water after strong water, for example acidic or sulphur springs, or where chlorine disinfection is used. The ministry's booklet says the same. That is the official position in full; it does not say rinsing is better or worse for everyone.

## Places named in official sources

Only what an official or association source states; no facility is assessed here.

- **Tamagawa, Akita.** The association names it, with Sukawa in Iwate, as an example of an acidic spring, and its page on reading analyses gives pH 1.2. The tourism agency says the Ōbuki source runs at around pH 1. Downstream sits hokutolite, a mineral of barium and lead sulphate containing radium and so mildly radioactive. It is a Special Natural Monument (designated 1952), and taking it is illegal.
- **Zao, Yamagata.** pH 1.6 to 1.25 per the tourism agency text, with the jewellery note above. I could not find its spring names.
- **[Sukayu](/en/soak/sukayu/), Aomori.** The environment ministry's plan for the area (March 2025) names the water an acidic, sulphur-bearing sodium sulphate spring of the hydrogen sulphide type, and the operator and the prefecture's tourism site also call it acidic. The association lists it as a sulphur spring, at 925 m. None of them gives a pH. The ministry's list of national health resort onsen dates its designation to 11 October 1954, the same day as Shima and Okunikkō Yumoto. The operator and the prefecture's tourism site call it the first; the ministry's list does not rank the three, and I did not see the word "first" on the association's page.
- **Sulphur examples.** The association names Kowakudani in Hakone and Nikkō Yumoto.
- **Misasa, Tottori.** The prefecture's tourism federation lists the water as radioactivity-containing, averaging 52.8°C, and its text mixes "radium" and "radon". The ministry's primer says soil there contains much radium and the air dose rate is slightly higher.
- **Masutomi, Yamanashi.** The association's example of a radioactive spring. A prefectural tourism page calls it a radium onsen without giving a spring name; I could not confirm the official one.

## Local and elsewhere

These types are associated with mountain onsen towns, but the legal system is the same everywhere.

- **City onsen are legal onsen.** The Tokyo metropolitan government explains that in the wards and eastern Tama, drilling a few hundred metres reaches water above 25°C that is also "fossil water" carrying sodium chloride from ancient seawater, so it qualifies under the Act. The dark brown *kuro-yu* around Ōta ward has long been used in public baths. Whether water is an onsen depends on the Act's criteria, not on where it is.
- **The posting rules are the same.** Components, contraindications, precautions, and any added water, heating, recirculation, additives or disinfection, apply to any facility offering onsen water.
- **Labels are policed.** The consumer agency's casebook includes a 21 July 2011 order against a sports club advertising a "natural ore radium onsen" that was heated tap, well and industrial water with a quasi-drug added; the problem was that it was not onsen at all.
- **Bath salts are a different regime.** They are quasi-drugs under the health ministry. The 2015 approval standard lists effects such as heat rash, stiff shoulders and sensitivity to cold, and does not mention onsen. A 1988 voluntary standard said bath salts should not use expressions indicating a spring type, such as "alkaline spring" or "○○ spring type"; I could not confirm whether it is still in force, or find a public document stating outright that bath salts are not onsen.

## What people get wrong

- **"Acidic spring means under pH 3."** It means 1 mg/kg or more of hydrogen ion. pH is a separate band on the sign.
- **"74 Bq makes a radioactive spring."** That is the onsen threshold. The radioactive spring starts at 111 Bq (8.25 Mache units).
- **"Radium spring is an official type."** It is radon that counts, and the guidelines calculate radium from radon.
- **"Sulphur type" is an official label.** Only "hydrogen sulphide type" is; the rest carry no label.
- **"If I can't smell it, there's no gas."** The ministry's guideline says not to judge by smell, because the gas dulls it.
- **"The bathroom gas limits are bather rules."** They are duties on the operator.
- **"Onsen only exist in onsen towns."** Tokyo has legal onsen, under the same posting rules.
- **"A spring-type bath salt is the same water."** Bath salts sit under a separate regime, and a 1988 standard told makers not to name spring types.
