Tsuetate is a small hot spring town in a gorge of the Tsuetate river, on the border between Kumamoto and Ōita. It is in **Oguni town**, not in Minamioguni, the neighbouring town where [Kurokawa](/en/soak/kurokawa/) is. What sets it apart is steam: water that comes out at close to 100°C, steam rooms in many inns, and steaming boxes in the street where eggs and sweet potatoes are cooked. This page uses the Tsuetate Onsen tourism association (which runs tsuetate-onsen.com), the Tsuetate Onsen inns’ association, Oguni town, Kumamoto prefecture’s hot spring site, the Aso Geopark and the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA). Much of the detail comes from the tourism association, and the page says so where it does. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the salt-type spring named below, see [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/). For reading the sign at any bath, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### Hot, and from many sources

The tourism association says the water is **about 98°C** and that **26 sources** are scattered through the town; its spring details give 98 to 100°C. The inns’ association says **about 20** sources in the small spa town, at about 98°C. Neither says why the counts differ, and neither gives a year. Oguni town gives no count. Official sources do not give flow or pH.

### Three ways of naming it

- **The tourism association**: colourless and clear, a weakly alkaline *jaku-shokuen-sen* (weak salt spring) close to neutral. It says the water is rich in metasilicic acid.
- **Oguni town** (page updated 12 March 2025): a colourless, clear weak salt spring.
- **Kumamoto prefecture’s hot spring site**: simple spring (weakly alkaline), chloride spring and hydrogen-carbonate spring.

"Weak salt spring" is an old-style name. The environment ministry’s table of old and new names gives its current equivalent as **sodium chloride spring**.

The Aso Geopark adds that Kurokawa and Tsuetate are accompanied by many springs high in carbon dioxide. None of the sources explains how that fits with the "weak salt spring" of the town and the association, so this page does not call Tsuetate a carbonated spring.

### Why here

The Aso Geopark describes Tsuetate as a spring of the Kujū volcanic system rising along the Tsuetate river, in the northernmost part of the geopark. Like Kurokawa, it says Tsuetate is "thought to be" related to the Kujū volcano, not Aso. It also says that in volcanic areas most hot spring water is groundwater, with only a small share of volcanic origin.

The JMA lists Kujūsan as a volcano under constant watch: a group of more than 20 volcanoes spread over 15 km east to west, from the south of Kokonoe town in Ōita to the north of Taketa city, with Nakadake at 1,791 m and Hosshōzan at 1,762 m. As checked on 13 October 2026, it was at **alert level 1** (a reminder that it is an active volcano), and the agency’s regular report for September 2026 was issued on 8 October. Tsuetate is on the Kumamoto side of the border.

Tsuetate is not on the environment ministry’s list of national health resort hot springs; the Kumamoto entries are Amakusa Shimoda, the Minamioguni onsen district and Yunoko–Yunotsuru. That means only that Tsuetate has no such designation.

## Where it came from

### The birth-bath legend

The tourism association tells the founding story as a legend. More than 1,800 years ago, in the time of Emperor Chūai, Empress Jingū went into labour at Munakata in Chikuzen. A white-haired old man told her that up a river to the south-east was a sacred spring, and that a prince bathed in it at birth would live a thousand years. Her attendants found a thread of steam and brought the water back for the birth bath. That spring, the association says, was Tsuetate, and the child was the later Emperor Ōjin. Oguni town says Tsuetate "is said to" have 1,800 years of history, and the inns’ association that it "is said to" be reaching 1,800 years since it opened. No record supports the date; every source tells it as a tradition.

### Two stories for the name

*Tsuetate* means "standing staff". The tourism association’s English page gives two explanations and this page does not choose:

- Early in the Heian period, the monk Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi), moved by the spring, stood his bamboo staff in the ground, and leaves and branches grew from its joints.
- Sick and elderly people came on sticks for long-stay bathing, and left their sticks behind when they went home.

The association’s Japanese page gives only the Kūkai story, and the Aso Geopark also mentions it.

### Shōwa and after

The tourism association says that in the early Shōwa period Tsuetate was busy as an entertainment district known as the "inner parlour of Kyushu" (*Kyūshū no oku-zashiki*), and that it declined from the Heisei era, which began in 1989. Its narrow back lanes, called *sedoya*, took shape as buildings were added to and rebuilt in the Shōwa years.

## How the town works

### Steam rooms

The tourism association says many inns have a *mushi-buro*, a steam bath it likens to a sauna. In the traditional type you open a small door and, past the steam, find a small cave-like room where you lie down. Other types have you sit with only your head outside, or lie on a bed. The association gives five rules:

1. Wash your whole body with hot spring water before going in.
2. If the room is too hot, splash hot spring water or cold water about.
3. Five to ten minutes is about right.
4. Drink a glass of water first.
5. Afterwards, rinse off the sweat with hot spring water.

These are in Japanese only. The association’s notes on benefits are not repeated here. The inns’ association marks which inns have a steam bath and which take drop-in bathers.

### Steaming boxes in the street

The tourism association says there are shared baths and *mushiba*, steaming places, around the town, where people steam eggs and sweet potatoes, and where eggs and sweet potatoes are sold. It says steaming food with spring vapour was part of daily life.

### Shared baths

The tourism association’s list of baths describes *kyōdō-yu*, communal baths, as the baths local people use every day, and *kazoku-buro*, family baths, as private rooms hired by the hour. It gives prices for both but does not say whether tax is included, so none are given here. It lists these communal baths:

- **Motoyu**, at the foot of Momiji bridge, a half-open-air rock bath, linked by legend to Empress Jingū’s birth bath.
- **Yakushi-yu**.
- **Gozen-yu**, a bath with ties to the lords of the Higo domain.
- The footbath **Oyu no Eki**.

### Bridges, lanes and fire

The association also points to the *sedoya* lanes; the painted carp, *e-goi*, on Momiji bridge, where a wish is made on a pun between *koi* (carp) and *koi* (love); and the Dondoya fire festival on 14 January.

### Carp streamers

Each spring, carp streamers (*koinobori*) are strung across the river. The tourism association says the festival runs **from late March to late April, not including Golden Week**; in 2026 it ran from **28 March to 23 April**. Oguni town says that in April about **3,500** carp streamers fly above the Tsuetate river.

The association says Tsuetate is where the carp streamer festival, now seen all over Japan, began, with streamers hung from ropes tied to balls thrown across the river. That is the association’s own account; no other source for it could be found.

On 15 September 2026 the association said it would announce the 2027 festival once restoration work is finished.

### Rain damage in July 2026

The tourism association reported on 3 July 2026 that heavy rain early on 2 July had flooded one inn above floor level; the others had no major damage and were open, there were no closures on the national road, and the car park on the left-bank flood-defence road could not be used. On 15 September it said the July rain had damaged that road, that parking there would be unavailable for a long time, and that repair work is expected to finish **around the end of February 2027**. Inns, restaurants and attractions were open as usual. The association attributes this damage to the July rain; its pages do not describe damage from the July 2026 earthquake. A notice on its site says 50% of sales of the *Yuge no Mori* bath salts and related goods from 1 August to 31 October 2026 goes to support for the earthquake.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos and English

The tourism association’s English page covers history only. Oguni town’s foreign-language page links to machine translation. Official sources do not give a tattoo rule for Tsuetate, in English or Japanese. Ask each inn or bath in advance; a family bath is one option. More in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

JNTO’s general advice: leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room; wash and rinse before getting in; keep your small towel out of the water; tie up long hair; do not swim or splash; leave your phone outside. It says some traditional baths refuse tattoos but this is starting to change, and suggests tattoo-friendly places or a private bath.

### Compared with city baths

No official Tsuetate source compares its water with baths in the city. *Yuge no Mori* bath salts and body cream are sold in Tsuetate as souvenirs, but no source says how they compare with the spring itself. For what the notice at the bath tells you, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

The farm ministry’s list of Kumamoto dishes and its Kumamoto overview do not mention Oguni or Tsuetate, the same result as for neighbouring Kurokawa. The tourism association has its own example: *ama-tamago*, a local sweet like *chawanmushi* with sugar, served to welcome guests and close to what is now called pudding. Its Tsuetate Pudding Project is named after it. That is the association’s project, not a dish listed by the ministry, the prefecture or the town. For Kumamoto’s food as a whole, see [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026, from the tourism association’s access page, which has no date. Timetables, fares and any suspensions are not given; check the bus companies before you travel. Official Tsuetate sources say nothing about recovery from the 2016 earthquakes.

- **From Fukuoka by car.** About 1 hour 30 minutes via Fukuoka interchange, Tosu junction, Hita interchange and Route 212.
- **From Fukuoka by bus.** Nishitetsu bus bound for Tsuetate from Hakata bus terminal, via Fukuoka airport (international terminal), about 2 hours 30 minutes.
- **From Hakata by train and bus.** JR to Hita, then the Hita bus bound for Tsuetate, about 2 hours 10 minutes.
- **From Kumamoto by car.** About 1 hour 40 minutes by Route 57 and Route 212.
- **From Kumamoto by bus.** From Kumamoto Kōtsū Center, the Yamabiko limited express bus via Kumamoto airport to Aso station, then the Sankō bus bound for Tsuetate, about 3 hours 10 minutes.
- **From Aso.** About 40 minutes by car on Route 212 from the former Aso town. See also [Aso-Uchinomaki](/en/soak/aso-uchinomaki/).
- **From Beppu.** About 2 hours 30 minutes by car via the Yamanami Highway.

## What people get wrong

- **"Tsuetate is part of Kurokawa."** Tsuetate is in Oguni town; Kurokawa is in Minamioguni. They are neighbours and the geopark groups them, but they are different towns.
- **"The carp streamer festival is in February and March."** The tourism association says late March to late April; Oguni town says April.
- **"Tsuetate has 26 sources."** That is the tourism association’s count. The inns’ association says about 20.
- **"Tsuetate is 1,800 years old."** That figure comes from the Empress Jingū legend, told by every source as a tradition.
- **"The water or the steam cures things."** This page does not say so. The official indications by spring type are in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
