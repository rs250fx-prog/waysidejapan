Yamashiro Onsen is one of the hot spring towns of Kaga, in the south-west of Ishikawa prefecture, a bus ride from Kaga Onsen station on the Hokuriku shinkansen. It is built in the old Hokuriku way, around a shared public bath, and it now has two: the Sōyu, the working bath, and the Kosōyu, a reconstruction of the Meiji-era bathhouse with no showers and no soap. This page covers what the town says about how it began, what is known about the water, how the two baths work, and how to get there. It uses the Yamashiro Onsen tourism association, Ishikawa prefecture’s Japanese and English tourism sites and, for food, the farm ministry. Where they disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how to read a spring’s name and the analysis sign, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for what "no water added" and *kakenagashi* mean on that sign, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Where it came from

### Two stories on one page

The tourism association’s page about the town tells two versions of the founding, and they are not the same story.

- **The crow.** In 725, the second year of the Jinki era, the monk Gyōki, on his way to train on Mt Hakusan, saw a crow healing a wounded wing in a pool of water. The water turned out to be hot, and the place came to be called *karasu no yu*, "the crow’s bath". The association says the crow is held to be Yatagarasu, the three-legged crow of the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* chronicles.
- **The temple.** The same page, describing the temple Yakuō-in Onsen-ji, says Gyōki found the spring while climbing Hakusan and carved images, among them the healing Buddha Yakushi Nyorai, to guard it.

Both put Gyōki at the start, but one has a crow and the other does not. I give both and do not choose.

### "1,300 years"

The association’s page on the public baths says Yamashiro is "said to have opened 1,300 years ago". Counting from 725 gives about 1,300 years; that sum is mine, not the association’s. Ishikawa prefecture’s English page says the spring was "first discovered over 1,300 years ago" and does not tell either legend.

On the association’s pages I found no title of the "oldest" or "three great" kind with anyone named as having chosen it, so I do not use one. Yamashiro is not on the environment ministry’s list of *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the hot spring areas designated for health and recreation; the only Ishikawa entry on that list is the Hakusan onsen district.

### Writers

The association says Yamashiro was loved by writers and artists, naming Yosano Akiko and Izumi Kyōka among them. It also lists, among its sights, Iroha Sōan, the house where Kitaōji Rosanjin stayed in 1915. The association says the house is open, with his workroom, study, hearth room, tea room and an exhibition room in the storehouse. Its page gives no opening hours, closing days or admission. I could not find, on the association’s pages, how Rosanjin came to stay or for how long, so I leave it there.

## The water

### What is on the sign

The association’s page for the two public baths gives their spring type as a sodium-calcium sulphate-chloride spring (*natorium-karushiumu ryūsan’en-enkabutsu sen*). Its general page about the town says there are "three slightly different spring types in the same place", but does not name them. So I cannot give one spring type for Yamashiro as a whole; what I can give is the type for the Sōyu and the Kosōyu.

### How it reaches the baths

- **Sōyu.** The association says it uses a heat-exchange system so that no water is added, and calls it 100% source water. Ishikawa prefecture’s page for the Sōyu also says 100% source water with no water added, and adds that it uses free-flowing and filtered, recirculated water together. The prefecture’s page carries no update date.
- **Kosōyu.** The prefecture describes it as *gensen kakenagashi*, water running straight from the source.

How these fit together inside the building is not explained. The sign in each bath is the place to read which bath gets what.

### What I could not find

I could not find the number of sources, the flow or the temperature for Yamashiro. They are not on the association’s pages, I could not open a Kaga city or prefectural document that gives them, and because Yamashiro is not a designated health resort there is no environment ministry plan to read either.

## How the town works

### The *yunogawa*

The association explains that in the Edo period a spa town grew up around its shared bath. The bath was called the *sōyu*, and the streets around it the *yunogawa*, a name it says is particular to Hokuriku. The association’s English site gives the same: *soyu* for the shared bath in the Hokuriku dialect, *Yunogawa* for the district around it.

### The Sōyu

The present Sōyu, the association says, reuses the gate of the former Yoshinoya inn so that it sits in with the streets of the *yunogawa*.

### The Kosōyu

The Kosōyu, "old sōyu", is a reconstruction of the Meiji-era public bath. The association says it recreates the outside and inside of the building and also the way people bathed then, called *yuami*, and that the upstairs rest area and the Kutani-ware tiles were restored as they were. It calls the building a hands-on hot spring museum.

The practical points, from the association and the prefecture:

- There are no taps and no showers.
- Soap and shampoo cannot be used.
- The prefecture says to pour water over yourself before getting in, and that the tiles reproduce the original patterns in Kutani ware.
- The prefecture’s English page says there is one bath each for men and women.

The association’s Kutani-ware tiles are the only link with the porcelain that I found on its pages. It does not describe Yamashiro as a place where Kutani ware is made, so I do not either.

### Hours and closing days

From the association’s public bath page, which carries no update date:

| Bath | Hours | Closed |
|---|---|---|
| Sōyu | 6:00–22:00 | 4th Wednesday of the month, 6:00–12:00 |
| Kosōyu | 6:00–22:00 (December–February 7:00–21:00) | 4th Wednesday of the month, 6:00–12:00 |

Both open normally from noon on the closing day. The association’s English page gives the same closing time. Ishikawa prefecture’s pages add that on 1 to 3 January both baths open 7:00 to 21:00.

## Bathing there

### Prices

The association lists prices for each bath and a combined ticket, with a note that they were revised from 1 April 2026, and the prefecture gives the same revision date. Neither says whether tax is included, so I do not give the figures. The association’s English site gives a different, lower figure for the Sōyu, also without a tax note or a date.

### Manners

The association’s English page asks you to rinse your body with hot water before getting in, says swimsuits are not allowed, and says no running, swimming or diving in the Sōyu. For the Kosōyu it repeats that there are no showers and that soap cannot be used. The general steps for a Japanese public bath are in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

### Tattoos

The association’s English pages do not mention tattoos, and I found no rule for the Sōyu or the Kosōyu.

visitkaga.jp, which calls itself the official travel guide in its footer but does not name who runs it, has an undated FAQ. It says that people with tattoos were not allowed in the past, that many public baths have begun to accept tattooed visitors from overseas, and that a cover sticker can make things more comfortable. It says nothing about Yamashiro’s baths in particular. Ask before you go in.

### Compared with city baths

I found no official Yamashiro source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What the association does say is that the Sōyu adds no water. In any town or city, a bath using hot spring water has to post whether the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected, which is how to compare one bath with another.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish that both the farm ministry and Yamashiro’s own pages present as Yamashiro’s. The ministry lists thirty local dishes for Ishikawa, including *jibuni*, a thickened duck stew, and *kabura-zushi*, turnip with yellowtail fermented with rice malt. One of the ministry’s explanations for *kabura-zushi* is that it was served to a Maeda lord at a local hot spring inn where he had come to bathe, but the page does not name the spring, so I do not tie it to Yamashiro. The association’s pages that I read do not mention local dishes.

Ishikawa prefecture’s tourism site describes *Kaga kani gohan*, a crab rice dish created in Kaga city in 2015 ahead of the Hokuriku shinkansen. Its rules include using a whole *kōbako* crab, the female snow crab, and serving it in Yamanaka lacquerware or Kutani ware. That is a Kaga city dish, not one the association names for Yamashiro.

## Getting there

From the association’s access page, which carries no update date (checked 11 October 2026). Fares are not on this page.

- **By shinkansen to Kaga Onsen.** From Kanazawa 16 minutes at the fastest; from Tokyo 2 hours 43 minutes at the fastest; from Osaka or Nagoya about 2 hours, changing at Tsuruga; from Kyoto about 1 hour 40 minutes. JR West says the Hokuriku shinkansen opened from Kanazawa to Tsuruga on 16 March 2024.
- **Kaga Onsen station to the town.** Ishikawa prefecture gives about 4.4 km. Take the CAN BUS, Kaga’s sightseeing loop bus, on its mountain line to the "Yamashiro Onsen Sōyu/Kosōyu" stop, about 100 m from the baths, or the Hokutetsu Kaga bus on its Onsen–Yamanaka line to "Yamashiro Onsen", about 250 m away. The prefecture does not give the journey time.
- **By air.** Komatsu airport, about 1 hour from Haneda or Narita, says the association.
- **By car.** The association gives times from Kaga and Katayamazu interchanges.

The association’s English site gives about 3.5 hours from Tokyo and about 2.5 hours from Osaka by shinkansen. These do not match the Japanese page, and the English page has no date, so they may be counted differently or be older.

The association’s access page also carries notices about bus changes: a timetable change on the CAN BUS airport line, the Kaga Yunosato express suspended from 1 August 2023, and planned suspensions of other services. I could not confirm the current timetables, so check before you travel.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yamashiro has one spring type."** The association says there are three slightly different ones and names only the one used at the two public baths.
- **"The Sōyu and the Kosōyu are the same bath."** The Kosōyu is a reconstruction of the Meiji bathhouse, with no showers and no soap.
- **"The founding is a single story."** The association’s own page has Gyōki with a crow in one place and Gyōki with temple images in another.
- **"Yamashiro is 1,300 years old, officially."** It is the association’s phrase, "said to have opened 1,300 years ago", counted from a legend.
- **"The baths are open every day."** Both close on the morning of the fourth Wednesday.
- **"Rosanjin made Yamashiro famous."** The association says only that the house where he stayed in 1915 is open to visitors; it does not tell the story of his stay.
- **"Tattoos are fine in Kaga."** visitkaga.jp says many baths have begun to accept them, in general; nothing official covers Yamashiro’s two baths.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide.
