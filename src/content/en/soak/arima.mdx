Arima Onsen sits in Kita ward of Kobe, in a valley at the foot of the northern side of the Rokko mountains, as its tourism association puts it. It is known for two kinds of water — a brown *kinsen*, "gold spring", and a clear *ginsen*, "silver spring" — and, among geologists, for being hot with no volcano nearby. This page covers where the water comes from, what the town’s sources say about its age, and how its two public bathhouses work. The ten official spring types and how to read the analysis sign are explained in the separate guide to onsen water types, and what free-flowing water means in the piece on *kakenagashi*; the Honshu overview puts Arima alongside the rest of Kansai.

## Where it came from

The tourism association’s history page sets out several stories side by side and treats them as tradition — "it is said", "it is told" — not as settled history. I do the same, and this site does not choose a starting point.

- **Myth.** In the age of the gods, Ōnamuchi and Sukunahikona are said to have first found the spring, according to the shrine history of Tōsen Jinja. Another story tells of three injured crows that bathed in a puddle and healed within days; the puddle was hot spring water.
- **Written record.** The association cites the *Nihon Shoki* for Emperor Jomei bathing at Arima for 86 days, from the 19th day of the 9th month to the 13th day of the 12th month of 631, and a later text for Emperor Kōtoku staying 82 days in 647. A University of Tsukuba release says only that, according to the *Nihon Shoki*, the spring was known by the first half of the 7th century.
- **Gyōki.** The monk Gyōki, trusted by Emperor Shōmu, is said to have revived Arima and carved a life-size statue of the healing Buddha, after which the town is said to have thrived for about 370 years. Kobe city’s page also credits Gyōki with reviving it and founding Onsen-ji temple.
- **Flood and Ninsai.** According to the Onsen-ji temple chronicle, a flood in 1097 destroyed the spring and it lay waste for about 95 years. The association adds that "there are various theories". The monk Ninsai, following a dream from the Kumano deity, is said to have restored it, and in 1191 to have renovated Onsen-ji and run twelve lodging temples.
- **Hideyoshi.** The association calls 1583 the first recorded visit of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and says he began a large repair of the springs in 1597, after an earthquake the year before had made the water suddenly much hotter. He died the following year. The association also writes that no repair of the springs was made for about 350 years after his. Kobe city says remains of his bath from the Yuyama palace are shown at the Taikō no Yudono-kan museum.

**How old, then?** The association’s 631 date is about 1,400 years ago, and Kobe city speaks of a 1,400-year history. JNTO says "nearly 1,500 years". These are different ways of counting, and I give all of them.

**"Three ancient springs."** The association’s spring page calls Arima Japan’s oldest onsen; its "About Arima" page calls it one of Japan’s three ancient springs; a 2022 guide page on its site says one of the "three ancient and three famous springs". Kobe city says it is renowned as both. The history page quotes Sei Shōnagon’s *The Pillow Book*, which names Arima among four hot springs, with Nanakuri and Nasu, and reads that as Arima already ranking among the three great springs, alongside Sakakibara. None of these pages says who chose any of the groupings, and I could not confirm that any was officially chosen.

## The water

### Gold and silver

The association says Arima’s water contains seven of the nine main components the environment ministry’s guideline uses for therapeutic springs — all but sulphur and acidity — and is drawn from shallow depths, down to 300 m. *Kinsen* is "the water Arima has had since ancient times, containing iron and salt". *Ginsen* is two things: water with carbonic acid, and water that, in the association’s words, contains radon. The official classification treats radioactive springs separately, so "contains radon" here is the association’s wording, not a spring type.

The association also lists indications for each kind on that page. This site does not reproduce them as claims; the official indications by spring type, and the ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance, are in the water types guide.

### The sources, and how many there are

Kobe city’s Arima page has a table of five sources. I read it through a summary, not figure by figure against the original, and the page gives no year for the measurements.

| Source | Kind | Spring type (city’s wording) | Temperature | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tenjin | kinsen | iron-bearing sodium chloride, strong salt, high temperature | 99.4°C | 206 m |
| Uwanari | kinsen | same type as Tenjin | 100.5°C | 187 m |
| Tansan (carbonic) | ginsen | simple cold mineral spring with carbon dioxide and iron | 18.3°C | 13 m |
| Gokuraku | kinsen | sodium chloride, high temperature | 98.1°C | 240 m |
| Gosho | kinsen | sodium chloride, high temperature | 63.9°C | 165 m |

The 99.4°C at Tenjin also appears in a 2022 guide page on the association’s site and in Hyogo prefecture’s October 2024 newsletter.

The number of sources depends on who is counting, and this site does not choose:

- Kobe city’s table lists **five**.
- Kobe city’s sightseeing map names **six** on the map: Tansan, Uwanari, Gokuraku, Tenjin, Gosho and Ariake.
- A 2015 signed article on the association’s English site lists **seven** — Gosho, Tenjin, Jōryū, Gokuraku, Ariake No. 1, Ariake No. 2 and Tansan — drilled 200–300 m. That page is old and I could not match its figures against the association’s Japanese pages.
- The University of Tsukuba study says it measured "Arima’s seven sources".

I could not find an official figure for the total, or for the flow. Kobe city’s records say the water is supplied to inns and hotels by a company set up by what is now Kobe Electric Railway and the former town of Arima, which maintains the city’s sources and its own Ariake source. So some sources are city-owned and one is company-owned. I could not find how individual inns draw their water.

### What the sign at the carbonic source says

Kobe city publishes the legally required notice for the Tansan source, in Japanese, English, Chinese and Korean. The water was analysed on 25 October 2019 by the Japan Hot Springs Research Center, and the city fixed the notice on 10 March 2021. It reads: simple cold mineral spring containing carbon dioxide and iron, hypotonic, weakly acidic, 18.3°C (air 21°C), pH 4.3, radon 23.3 Bq/kg. The notice lists poor stomach and bowel function and iron-deficiency anaemia as the indications for the type, and drinking precautions, including a contraindication for people with an overactive thyroid if more than 250 mL is drunk a day, and that children aged 15 and under should generally avoid it.

### Salt, and how salty

The same 2015 English article says kinsen is 1.5 to 2 times as salty as seawater. The University of Tsukuba’s 2024 release says "more than twice". I give both.

## Why here: hot water without a volcano

A 2024 newsletter from the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST) says most springs are rain or seawater warmed by the earth’s heat or by volcanic gas and magma, but that some, like Arima, have no volcano nearby and became hot because deep fluid mixed in. Its diagram puts Arima on the non-volcanic side, as "Arima-type hot water". A 2012 conference abstract by researchers from AIST and three universities estimated that six places in Kansai, Arima among them, release a combined 8.5 kg a second of this deep fluid naturally, and said that fits with it coming from the sinking plate. Neither defines exactly where "Arima-type" begins and ends.

The University of Tsukuba’s release of 29 August 2024 explains what was new. Arima’s unusual water had long been **thought** to contain water from the Philippine Sea Plate, sunk more than 60 km down, but there was no direct evidence. The team measured hydrogen and oxygen isotopes at seven sources and found that the non-rainwater part matched a model of the plate’s water at 67.4 km below Arima. It also found that the share of plate water fell after deep wells were drilled from the 1940s, rose for a time around 1995, and that dilution by rainwater continues while the falling salt levels are levelling off. On the Great Hanshin earthquake of 17 January 1995, the release only **suggests** that deep water may have weakened the fault and helped trigger it. That is the team’s interpretation, not a finding I can state as fact.

Hyogo prefecture’s October 2024 newsletter quotes the head of a volunteer guide group saying the gold water took 6 million years to form from the plate, and that it is rare enough worldwide to be called "Arima-type". That figure is his, not the prefecture’s or the university’s.

## How the town works

The association lists two public day bathhouses: **Kin no Yu** and **Gin no Yu**. The company that runs them says on its own site that Kin no Yu’s water is kinsen — iron-bearing sodium chloride, strong salt, high temperature, clear when it emerges and turning brown soon after, with a strong salty, bitter, metallic taste — and that Gin no Yu’s is carbonic and radium water. The association’s own pages for the two do not give the water type.

The same operator publishes how each bath is run. Treat this as the operator’s own statement:

- **Kin no Yu**: free-flowing (*kakenagashi*). Water is added because the source is too hot to bathe in; it is then heated with steam to make up for the cooling; no bath additives; chlorine disinfection; the bath is fully drained and cleaned every day. Drinking the water is not allowed.
- **Gin no Yu**: recirculated and filtered, partly free-flowing, to protect the source and for hygiene. Water is added only now and then, when the source runs low; heated by heat exchanger; no additives; chlorine disinfection.

Neither way is officially better; the *kakenagashi* piece explains why operators add water, heat or disinfect.

Around them, the association’s 2022 guide describes Yumoto-zaka street as two-storey wooden buildings that were once shops below and inns above, and Taikō-bashi bridge as named after Hideyoshi. Kobe city’s map also marks Nene-bashi bridge, the Yukemuri plaza, the Taikō no Yudono-kan, Tōsen Jinja and Onsen-ji. The same 2022 page said the water at the Tansan Source Park had gone quiet after work uphill; the city’s analysis shows it was flowing in 2019, and I could not confirm its state now. The association’s English site calls *tōji*, staying to bathe as a cure, the original way to enjoy the spring.

## Bathing there

As listed on the association’s pages dated 2 September 2026, which match the operator’s site (checked 11 October 2026):

- **Kin no Yu** — 8:00 to 22:00, last entry 21:30. Closed the 2nd and 4th Tuesday (open if a public holiday, closed the next day instead) and 1 January.
- **Gin no Yu** — 9:00 to 21:00, last entry 20:30. Closed the 1st and 3rd Tuesday (same holiday rule) and 1 January.

There is a ticket for both; the weekday version comes with a towel. Admission prices are listed, but none of the pages says whether they include tax, so I do not give them. The operator says body soap, rinse-in shampoo and hair dryers are provided and towels are on sale, that weekday prices exclude busy periods such as New Year, Golden Week and Obon, and that entry may be limited when crowded. There is a free footbath next to Kin no Yu, open the same hours.

**Tattoos.** I could not find a tattoo policy from the association, on its English site, or on the bathhouses’ pages. JNTO’s national page says policies differ by facility and suggests checking in advance. The water types guide covers what the tourism agency and JNTO say more generally, including private baths.

## Food

The farm ministry’s list of Hyogo’s local dishes has nothing for Arima. On the town’s side:

- **Tansan senbei**, carbonated crackers — the association says they are said to have been created in 1907 (Meiji 40), using the carbonated water that was welling up.
- **Arima sanshō** — the association lists a shop selling sanshō pepper products under that name. The 1559 date on the page is the founding of the shop, Kawakami Shōten, not of Arima sanshō. I could not find an official account of where the pepper comes from.
- **Arima Cider** — the bathhouse operator calls it a local specialty. Its history, as told in a 2019 Kobe Shimbun newspaper series, is that the carbonated water was once called "poison water", was sent for analysis in 1875, was bottled from 1901, sold sweetened as Arima Cider from 1908, and revived by local shopkeepers in 2001. That is a newspaper’s account, and I did not confirm it in public records.

## Getting there

From the association’s Japanese access pages, which carry no update date (checked 11 October 2026):

- **JR Osaka** — about 1 hour: JR Kobe Line to Sannomiya, Kobe municipal subway to Tanigami, Kobe Electric Railway via Arima-guchi.
- **Shin-Osaka** — about 1 hour 10 minutes. **Hankyu/Hanshin Umeda** — about 1 hour 20 minutes via Shinkaichi; direct trains from Shinkaichi run only morning and evening.
- **Kansai Airport** — about 2 hours 10 minutes by train; about 2 hours 30 minutes by train and the Arima Express bus; about 1 hour 50 minutes by airport bus and train via Sannomiya; about 2 hours by airport bus via Sannomiya and Shin-Kobe.
- **Itami Airport** — about 30 minutes by bus; about 50 minutes by train and bus; about 1 hour 30 minutes by train.
- **Kyoto** — about 70 minutes by Hankyu or Keihan bus from the Hachijō exit of Kyoto station.
- **Sannomiya or Shin-Kobe** — about 30 minutes by train, or by the Arima Express or Shinki express bus.

The association’s English site gives different, rounder figures: 90 minutes from Kansai Airport and 60 from Osaka and Kyoto on its transport page, and about 1 hour from Kyoto, Osaka and Himeji and about 2 hours from the airport elsewhere. JNTO describes a route from Kobe-Sannomiya via Hankyu to Rokko, then the Rokko cable car, a bus and the Rokko-Arima ropeway down to Arima, without times. I did not check the rail or bus operators’ own pages, and fares are not on this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Onsen need a volcano."** Arima is the standard example of one that does not.
- **"The plate-water origin was proven long ago."** The 2024 study says it had been assumed without direct evidence; the earthquake link is still only suggested.
- **"Arima has seven springs."** Seven is one count. Kobe city’s table has five and its map names six.
- **"Ginsen is a radium spring."** The association calls one ginsen water radon-bearing, and the operator says "radium"; the official classification has no "radium spring" type.
- **"The three ancient springs is an official title."** No source I found names who chose it.
- **"Kakenagashi means nothing is added."** Kin no Yu says it is free-flowing and also adds water, heats it and uses chlorine.
