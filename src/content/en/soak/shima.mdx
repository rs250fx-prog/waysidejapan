Shima Onsen lies in the north-west of Gunma, in the town of Nakanojō, strung out along the Shima river between 600 and 700 m up. It has no central square or source to gather round; it is one long valley of inns, with a reservoir above it whose colour the prefecture calls Shima blue. This page explains how it works using the environment ministry, Nakanojō town, Gunma prefecture, the Agency for Cultural Affairs and JNTO. The fullest single source is the ministry’s plan for Shima as a *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, a national health resort onsen, dated March 2024, which I read from an image PDF. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn or bath.

One gap matters: the Shima Onsen association’s own website refused to open, so its English pages, fees, access details and anything it says about tattoos are missing here. For what the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). Shima’s neighbours in this series are [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/), [Ikaho](/en/soak/ikaho/) and [Minakami](/en/soak/minakami/).

## Where it came from

### The legend and the dates

The ministry’s plan says Shima’s history goes back to around 989 (Eien 3), with the Hinatami district as its birthplace. Its 2014 edition tells the founding legend: the spring was found by Usui Sadamitsu, a retainer of Minamoto no Raikō. I could not find the same passage in the 2024 edition. It is a legend, and I found no record that dates it.

The plan then sketches what came after: inns from the 16th century, the outline of the present town in the 17th, and crowds of *tōji* guests, people staying for days or weeks to bathe, in the Meiji era.

### The name

Nakanojō town says Shima was known from the Kamakura period as a legendary spring that cures "40,000 illnesses", the characters for Shima also being read *yonman*, forty thousand. That is the only explanation of the name I found on official pages, and the town gives it as a legend.

### "Three famous springs of Jōmō"

The town says Shima is counted, with Kusatsu and Ikaho, among the "three famous springs of Jōmō" (*Jōmō san-meitō*). It does not say who grouped them, and I found no official selection. Kusatsu is also called one of a different three, with Gero and Arima; see the [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/) page.

### The first health resort onsen

The environment ministry began designating national health resort onsen in 1954, and says there were 79 as of October 2022. The ministry’s plan for Shima says Shima was designated first, in 1954; Nakanojō town and JNTO say the same.

I could not confirm that on the ministry’s own list. Its PDF did not display Japanese text properly, so I could not match the Shima row to a date, and the date 11 October 1954 seemed to sit against more than one row. Several places may have been designated on the same day. So this page says that the ministry’s plan calls Shima the first.

## The water

### Forty-two sources

The ministry’s plan (March 2024) gives these figures:

| | March 2024 plan | May 2014 plan |
|---|---|---|
| Sources | 42 | 42 |
| In regular use | 38 | 37 |
| Temperature | 27–83°C | 26–83°C |
| Total flow | 3,000–4,000 L/min | 3,000–4,000 L/min |

Most sources flow naturally, the plan says. The 2014 edition’s table of individual sources gives temperatures from 26.5 to 79.9°C. I read the 2024 figures by eye from an image, so check them against the plan if you need them exactly.

### Two kinds of water

The plan names two waters: an alkaline simple spring, and a sodium-calcium chloride-sulphate spring. Simple springs are explained on the [page on sulphate and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/).

Other official pages name it differently. Nakanojō town says the water is plentiful, a chloride-sulphate spring, and hot, at 40 to 80°C. The town’s day bath, Shima Seiryū no Yu, gives sodium-calcium chloride-sulphate. Gunma prefecture’s list of springs by type, as of 31 March 2026, places Shima in the chloride column; I read that from the order of the text in a PDF, so treat it with care.

### Why it is like this

The ministry’s plan does not explain how Shima’s water forms, and I found no explanation from the weather agency, the geological survey or the prefecture.

## How the town works

### One river, five districts

The plan describes Shima as a long, narrow town along the Shima river, inside Jōshin’etsu Kōgen National Park, covering 171.6 hectares. Its five districts, from upstream, are Hinatami, Yuzuriha, Arayu, Yamaguchi and Onsenguchi. Traditional inns and modern hotels stand side by side, and the 2014 edition says a shopping street grew up to supply *tōji* guests with daily needs.

The 2014 edition counted 36 places to stay, three local shared baths — Kawara no Yu, Gomusō no Yu and Kami no Yu — and two day baths, Shima Seiryū no Yu and Koshiki no Yu. Those are 2014 numbers, and I could not find current ones. The same edition says Gomusō no Yu is regarded as the bath where Shima began, and that a *yutate* hot-water ritual was revived in front of it in 2006.

I could not find fees or opening days for any of these. The town’s page for Shima Seiryū no Yu gives no fee, and neither the plan nor the town lists the shared baths’ fees or closing days.

### Hinatami Yakushidō

Gunma prefecture calls the Hinatami Yakushidō, a Buddhist hall, a rare example of Chinese-style building and a national Important Cultural Property that keeps the building style of the Muromachi period. The ministry’s plan also calls it a nationally designated Important Cultural Property.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs’ online heritage record has a hall called simply "Yakushidō" in Ōaza Shima, Nakanojō, owned by the temple Sōhonji: three bays by three, single-storey, hipped and thatched, dated to the Momoyama period and designated an Important Cultural Property on 8 February 1912. The record does not use the name Hinatami, so I do not state outright that it is the same building, though its place, type and structure fit the prefecture’s description. The prefecture says Muromachi style; the record says Momoyama period. I do not choose.

### Sekizenkan

The same heritage records list three parts of the inn Sekizenkan:

- **Sekizenkan Sansō**, a wooden two-storey building of 1936, a Registered Tangible Cultural Property since 12 June 1997.
- **Maeshin**, from 1930, with a reinforced-concrete bath on the ground floor and arched windows in a style the record calls Taishō Romanesque, designated by Nakanojō town on 23 July 2014.
- **Mukaishin**, from 1936, said to have been built from timber left over from the Sansō, designated by the town on the same day.

I found no national designation or registration for Sekizenkan’s main building. JNTO calls it "centuries-old". This page does not review it.

### Lake Okushima and Shima blue

Above the town, the Shima river is held back by Shimagawa dam, a concrete gravity dam 89.5 m high built for flood control, water supply and power, directly upstream of the town, according to the prefecture. The ministry’s 2014 plan says construction began in 1980 and finished in 1999, and gives the dam as the background to monitoring the sources.

The lake behind it is Lake Okushima. The prefecture says its colour changes with the light and the time of day. Its English guide (December 2020) calls the colour "Shima blue", says the vivid blue comes from the water’s great clarity, and that it is deepest in spring, until about May, when snowmelt from the mountains flows into the river. It puts the lake about 5 km from the town. JNTO mentions its cobalt colour and canoe tours. I found no scientific explanation of the colour from a government body or university, so I go no further than the prefecture’s.

The prefecture also lists the falls of Maya, Ogura and Koizumi, the Fusenkyō gorge and the Shima potholes, a prefectural natural monument.

## Bathing there

- **Tattoos.** I could not open the Shima Onsen association’s site, and the town’s English pages explain only how to use the website. I found nothing on tattoos for Shima. Gunma prefecture’s general English guide says many onsen do not allow tattoos, that a small one may be fine covered with a plaster, and to check before you go. It also says to wash before getting in and keep the small towel out of the water. Ask each inn or bath in advance; the general picture is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Signs.** The ministry’s 2014 plan mentions signs in English, Chinese and Korean for overseas visitors, but not bathing instructions.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Shima source that compares its water with baths in the cities.

## What it is eaten with

I found no official source that names a dish together with Shima Onsen. The nearest is *hana-ingen*, large runner beans. The farm ministry lists simmered *hana-ingen* as a local dish handed down mainly in Nakanojō, Tsumagoi, Kusatsu and Naganohara, grown at 900 to 1,300 m, and says that in Nakanojō they are especially enjoyed as tempura. Shima is in Nakanojō, but the ministry’s page does not mention Shima or the spa, and the town’s specialities page did not link the two either. So I do not call it a Shima dish. For the bean at Kusatsu, see the [Kusatsu](/en/soak/kusatsu/) page; for Gunma’s dishes generally, [Kantō’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kanto/).

## Getting there

As checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, and I could not open the Shima Onsen association’s access page.

- **Limited express and bus.** Gunma prefecture’s access guide (updated March 2024) gives about two hours from Ueno to Nakanojō by the limited express Kusatsu-Shima, then about 40 minutes by Kan-etsu Kōtsū bus from stop 1, roughly one an hour, to the Shima Onsen terminus, two minutes’ walk from Ochiai-dōri. The prefecture’s dam access page (July 2021) gives about 2 hours 10 minutes to Nakanojō, then about 40 minutes by bus or about 25 by taxi.
- **Shinkansen.** JNTO gives 50 minutes from Tokyo to Takasaki, 55 minutes on the Agatsuma line to Nakanojō, and 40 minutes by bus.
- **Direct bus.** The prefecture’s access guide gives about four hours by the Shima Onsen bus from Tokyo station (Yaesu south exit) and about 4.5 hours from Haneda. Its Shima destination page says one or two a day from Tokyo station, taking about 3.5 hours. The two pages disagree, and I do not pick one. The Shibukawa Ikaho tourism association says the Ikaho–Shima Onsen bus from Haneda, Tokyo station and Kawagoe to Ikaho is currently suspended. I could not tell whether that affects the service to Shima, so check before you rely on it.

## Shima beside Kusatsu

Kusatsu gathers round the Yubatake, one open source in the middle of town. Shima has no single centre: the ministry describes five districts along one river. Beyond that, the official pages do not compare them.

## What people get wrong

- **"Shima means forty thousand illnesses, officially."** The town gives that as a legend, not as the origin of the name.
- **"Shima was certainly the first health resort onsen."** The ministry’s plan, the town and JNTO say so; I could not match it to a date on the ministry’s list.
- **"Sekizenkan’s main building is a national Important Cultural Property."** I found no record of that. The Sansō is registered, Maeshin and Mukaishin are town-designated.
- **"Shima blue is a chemical colour."** The prefecture’s guide puts it down to clear water; I found no scientific explanation.
- **"There is one Shima water."** The ministry names two, alkaline simple and chloride-sulphate, and the town and prefecture word it differently again.
- **"The water cures 40,000 illnesses."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
