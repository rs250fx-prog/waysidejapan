Kurokawa is a small hot spring town in a river valley in the highlands of north-eastern Kumamoto. What makes it different is not one famous bath but an arrangement: the open-air baths of thirty inns can be toured with a single pass. This page explains how that works using the environment ministry’s plan for the Minamioguni onsen district, Minamioguni town and JNTO. The inns’ association’s own site would not open for me (it returned an error on all three pages I tried), so some details are missing, and I say which. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how Kurokawa compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the Kyushu hot springs overview. For what the spring names on the sign mean, see the guide to onsen water types; for the notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on *kakenagashi*.

## Where it came from

### The district

Kurokawa is in Minamioguni, a town the ministry’s plan (dated March 2020) places at the north-eastern tip of Kumamoto prefecture, on a plateau at the foot of the Aso outer rim and the Kujū mountains, 430 to 945 m above sea level. It is in the headwaters of the Chikugo river, and 85% of the town is forest and open land; part of it lies in the Aso-Kujū national park. Three hot spring towns, Kurokawa, Tanohara and Manganji, were designated a national health resort onsen (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*) in 1964, and the plan later grew to cover the whole town, adding Oda, Shirakawa and others.

The plan also says the Aso outer rim and its surroundings, including the Oguni area, joined the Global Geoparks Network in 2014, became the Aso UNESCO Global Geopark in 2015, and that the Minamioguni springs are part of its Oguni-gō onsen geosite.

### Founding stories

The plan gives two accounts that do not agree, and this page does not choose.

- In one place it says Manganji’s spring is "said" to have been opened by the monk Kōbō Daishi in 848.
- In another it says Tanohara and Manganji are said to have opened in the Kamakura period, and that Kurokawa is said to have had inns by the Edo period.

For Tanohara, the plan says the water once rose from the riverbed, and the river was diverted to secure the source. From around 1996 the natural springs began to dry up, and the communal bath closed; the building is still there.

### 1986

The plan dates Kurokawa’s rise to the open-air bath tour, *rotenburo meguri*, which began in 1986. Because the valley has little flat land, hot spring development spread to the plateau away from the old town, and the area of the onsen grew with it.

## The water

The plan, as of when it was written, says 15 sources in the Kurokawa area are used by 30 inns and two public baths. The main types are sodium chloride-hydrogen carbonate springs and simple springs, and almost all the sources are 42°C or hotter. Its table lists each source’s temperature, flow and type; in the first seven rows, which are all I have read closely, temperatures run from 29.4 to 80.0°C and flows from 52 to 236 litres a minute. I have not checked the whole table, and I could not find a more recent count.

The town’s own Kurokawa page gives 30 inns, but no spring type, number of sources or history.

**Why here.** The plan describes where Kurokawa is, along the Tanohara river, a tributary of the Chikugo, but says nothing about how the water forms. The Japan Meteorological Agency lists Asosan (1,592 m, last erupted 2020) and Kujūsan (1,791 m, 1996) as active volcanoes under constant watch. I found no official source linking Kurokawa’s water to either, so this page does not.

## How the town works

### The entry pass

The plan describes Kurokawa’s streets as wooden inns and shops along the Tanohara river, in a Japanese style. Each of the 30 inns has set up its own distinctive open-air bath, and the inns’ association issues an entry pass, the *nyūtō tegata*, that lets you bathe in any three of them. The plan says visitors buying the pass to tour the baths has proved popular. Some passes are made of wood, using local Oguni cedar.

Minamioguni town says the pass has been sold for more than 30 years and that sales passed three million in 2019. JNTO says it is sold at the tourist information office, where there is an English pamphlet.

Both the town and JNTO give the price, and their figures match, but neither says whether it includes tax or gives a date, and I could not open the inns’ association’s site. So I have not given it. For the same reason I could not confirm how long a pass is valid, how many inns accept it now, or where else it is sold.

### Yukata in the street

The plan says day visitors can also rent colourful yukata, so many bathers walk through the town in them between baths. In winter, lanterns made of local bamboo, *yu-akari*, are set along the river and elsewhere.

### Public baths, and water in daily life

The plan counts two public baths in Kurokawa. At Manganji nearby, it describes the riverside *kawa-yu* bath with a washing place beside it, fed by hot spring water and used for washing vegetables and dishes, as a sign that the springs are still part of daily life.

## Bathing there

I found no official prices for baths in Kurokawa whose tax status I could confirm, so none are given.

JNTO gives this advice for Kurokawa:

- Most baths are separated by sex, and you bathe naked; swimwear, clothing and jewellery are not allowed.
- Wash well before getting in, and tie up hair that might touch the water.
- Guests with tattoos are often refused at public baths unless the tattoos are covered. Check first with the tourist information office or your inn; a private bath, which can be hired for a fee, is an option.

That is JNTO’s guidance, not a policy of the town or the inns’ association. The plan says foreign visitors have been increasing in recent years, and that the town intends to post bathing instructions at the baths and hand out leaflets.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find one. The farm ministry’s list of Kumamoto dishes includes nothing named for Kurokawa, Minamioguni, Oguni or hot springs. ## Getting there

As checked on 10 October 2026. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel.

- **By bus.** JNTO says there is no railway to Kurokawa, and that direct highway buses run from Fukuoka Airport and Hakata station. It gives no journey time, and I could not find one.
- **By train and local transport.** From Kumamoto station, JNTO says the nearest station is Aso, then a taxi or bus.
- **By car.** JNTO says the roads are narrow and winding, inn car parks are for staying guests first, and day visitors should use the public car parks. Minamioguni town gives driving times to the town, not to Kurokawa itself: about 1 hour from Kumamoto Airport by the Milk Road, about 1 hour 45 minutes from Fukuoka Airport and about 1 hour 30 minutes from Oita Airport, both by the Ōita expressway. The page has no update date.

Fares are not on this page.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kurokawa is one big bathhouse."** It is 30 inns, each with its own open-air bath, and two public baths. The pass is what ties them together.
- **"The pass covers every bath."** It covers three of your choice. I could not confirm how many inns take part now.
- **"Kurokawa’s water comes from Aso."** No official source I read says so.
- **"Kurokawa is an ancient hot spring town."** The plan says Kurokawa had inns by the Edo period, and dates its growth to the 1986 bath tour. The older dates, 848 or the Kamakura period, are told of neighbouring Manganji and Tanohara.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications by spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
