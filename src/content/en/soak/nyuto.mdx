Nyūtō, in Akita, is not a single town. It is seven inns spread around the foot of a mountain, each drawing its own water, joined by a bus that runs between them. Most of what a visitor needs to know — who can buy the bath-hopping book, what to pay in, what closes in winter — follows from that. One caveat as of 11 October 2026: one of the seven, Ōgama, has been closed for the time being since 1 January 2026, according to its own page on the cooperative’s site. This page uses the inns’ own cooperative, the environment ministry, Semboku city, the Tazawako-Kakunodate tourism association, the Tohoku tourism promotion body, the bus operator and JNTO. Where they disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn.

For what the spring types and the long chemical names on the sign mean, see the separate guide to onsen water types; for the posted notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on *kakenagashi*. The Honshu hot springs guide covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

The Nyūtō Onsen cooperative calls the area "a hidden spring of the Akita domain, continuing since the Edo period", and says it was once loved as a *tōji* spot, a place to stay for days or weeks for the sake of the water. Its history page is mostly photographs with captions. Among them:

- At Tsuru no Yu, the thatched row house called the *honjin* is "said to be" where guarding samurai waited.
- At Kuroyu, people stayed for long *tōji* in the farming off-season.
- Magoroku was busy with *tōji* guests and mountain climbers.
- The thatched hut at Ganiba dates from the Meiji era.

### When Tsuru no Yu began: two ways of counting

The cooperative also publishes a chronology, which it says was put together from surviving material. For Tsuru no Yu, the inn usually described as the oldest, it gives:

- 1615: the spring opens, first called "Tazawa no Yu".
- 1638: the second lord of the Akita domain, Satake Yoshitaka, is "said to have" come for *tōji*.
- 1681: the inn opens, with Hanekawa Rokuemon running it as keeper of the spring.
- 1708: the name changes to Tsuru no Yu, after a hunter, Kansuke, saw a crane (*tsuru*) healing its wounds in the water.

The environment ministry’s 2017 plan for the area tells it differently. It says the oldest spring in Nyūtō is "said to have" begun in 1708, when Kansuke named it after the wounded crane, and that a document called the *Tsuru no Yu Yuraiki* records the lord’s visit in 1638. So the cooperative counts from 1615 and the ministry from the naming in 1708. This site does not choose. Semboku city calls Tsuru no Yu the oldest and says it was the Akita lord’s *tōji* place, without a year; JNTO says more than 300 years. I could not find prefectural or national cultural-heritage material that settles it.

### The other inns, by the cooperative’s chronology

These are the cooperative’s dates; several entries are given as "said to have been found by" someone.

- Kuroyu: opened 1674. The chronology says records survive of the Ashina lords of Kakunodate and their retainers staying there, and that until the mid-Meiji era it was also called "Kame no Yu".
- Ganiba: found 1846, said to be by two doctors; opened as a self-catering inn in 1850.
- Magoroku: found 1902, said to be by a forestry engineer; opened 1906.
- Taenoyu: found 1952, opened 1955.
- Ōgama: opened 1962. The cooperative says it was the first in Nyūtō to pipe its water to a site on the prefectural road, rather than build at the source.
- Kyūkamura: opened 1965, and a bus route opened with it. The cooperative calls it the fifteenth national vacation village; the ministry’s plan says it was designated the twelfth in 1964 and opened the next year. I could not find out why the numbers differ.

### How the area became a set of inns linked by a bus

The cooperative’s chronology traces the change from long *tōji* stays to the bath-hopping system:

- After the railway reached Obonai (now Tazawako station) in 1923, it was still about 20 km and six hours on foot to Nyūtō.
- 1956: Towada-Hachimantai National Park is established, and Tsuru no Yu, Kuroyu, Ganiba, Magoroku and Taenoyu are licensed as lodgings within it.
- 1959: the road becomes a prefectural road, and a forestry road to Tsuru no Yu opens, so cars can reach the area. In 1963 a bus line to Tsuru no Yu starts (it was withdrawn in 1978).
- Around 1973, it says, bus tours spread and the weeks-long farm-season stay came to an end.
- 1979: the cooperative is formed and the "bath-hopping stamp book" begins.
- 1997: the Akita shinkansen opens, with direct trains from Tokyo to Tazawako.
- 2009: the Yumeguri-gō, a shuttle bus round the seven inns, begins.
- 2023: the other member inns take shares in Magoroku to keep it running, and the cooperative becomes a cooperative association approved by the prefecture. Its website still uses the old name.

### Why "hidden spring"

*Hitō*, "hidden spring", is a word you will see attached to Nyūtō. The Japan Association for the Preservation of Hidden Springs (*Nihon Hitō wo Mamoru Kai*), a private incorporated association, says the word was coined by its founder, Iwaki Hifumi of the travel company Asahi Ryokōkai, and that the association began in April 1975 with 33 inns: small mountain inns, hard to reach and with no bus, that joined to advertise together. It says it does not require its members to be *kakenagashi*. I did not check whether any Nyūtō inn is a member. The environment ministry’s 2017 plan also uses the word, calling Nyūtō one of the most popular *hitō* in the country, without defining it.

### The national health resort designation

The cooperative’s site says Nyūtō is a national health resort onsen (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*) designated by the environment ministry. The ministry’s own documents explain the name. The designated area is called the Tazawako Kōgen Onsenkyō, in Semboku city, designated on 19 October 1967 (in the ministry’s list as of October 2022). The ministry’s 2017 plan for it says the area "consists of Nyūtō Onsen and Tazawako Kōgen Onsen". The cooperative’s chronology says Nyūtō and Tazawako Kōgen were designated in 1967. So Nyūtō is part of a designated area under a different name, not designated under its own.

## The water

The cooperative says the seven inns each have their own source, and that there are more than ten kinds of spring in all.

**The number of sources.** The environment ministry’s 2017 plan gives a count: the Tazawako Kōgen Onsenkyō has 18 sources, all of which rise in the Nyūtō district, between 30°C and 86°C, mostly sulphur springs, used for bathing and in part for drinking. Three of the 18 are city-owned bored wells whose water is piped about 6 km down to Tazawako Kōgen. For the seven Nyūtō inns it gives flows of 10 to 650 litres a minute. The plan gives no total; the flows in its table add up to roughly 2,200 litres a minute, of which the three city wells are about 650. These are figures from 2017, not a current analysis, and no prefectural or city page I read gives a current total. "More than ten kinds" (the cooperative) and "18 sources" (the ministry) count different things, so they do not contradict each other.

**The spring names, inn by inn.** The sources do not use the same names. Since the inns post their own analyses, take these as what each source printed, not as the current analysis:

- **Tsuru no Yu.** Cooperative: sulphur-bearing sodium-calcium-chloride-hydrogen-carbonate spring, and three others. Tourism association: sulphur-bearing sodium-chloride-hydrogen-carbonate spring, pH 6.6 to 7.4. Semboku city’s page uses an older name.
- **Taenoyu.** Cooperative: calcium-magnesium sulphate spring; simple spring. Tourism association: acidic calcium-magnesium sulphate spring, pH 2.52; simple spring, pH 6.5.
- **Kuroyu.** Both: simple hydrogen-sulphide spring; acidic sulphur spring. The association’s English page gives pH 4.
- **Ganiba.** Both: simple sulphur spring. Association: pH 8.3.
- **Magoroku.** Cooperative: simple spring. Association: simple spring and simple sulphur spring, no pH.
- **Ōgama.** Three different names. Cooperative: acidic, arsenic-bearing sodium chloride sulphate spring. Tourism association: acidic (iron-bearing) aluminium acid spring, pH 2.51. The ministry’s 2017 plan: iron-bearing simple acidic spring.
- **Kyūkamura.** Cooperative: simple sulphur spring; sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring. Association: sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring, pH 6.7; simple sulphur spring, pH 5.4.

None of these pages gives the year of the analysis, so I could not tell which name is current. The list mixes current spring names with older ones such as "simple hydrogen-sulphide spring"; the water types guide explains how the old and new names relate. The cooperative also lists effects for each inn; I have not copied them.

**Why the water is here.** I could not find a public source that says where Nyūtō’s heat comes from. Two official sources sit side by side. The national geological survey (AIST) places Mount Nyūtō and Akita-Komagatake in the same north-east to south-west line of volcanoes, with Akita-Komagatake at its southern end, but does not mention the hot springs. The environment ministry’s plan describes the area as lying at the foot of Akita-Komagatake, which it calls an active volcano, and Mount Nyūtō, with springs rising all over the slopes. The cooperative’s chronology records an eruption of Komagatake (Medake) in 1970 with casualties; I did not confirm that in the weather agency’s or AIST’s material.

## How the area works

### Seven inns, not one town

The cooperative has seven members: Tsuru no Yu, Taenoyu, Ganiba, Ōgama, Magoroku, Kuroyu and Kyūkamura. Each is an inn with its own source. A visitor moves between them rather than walking a street of baths.

As checked on 11 October 2026, Ōgama’s page on the cooperative’s site says it is closed "for the time being" from 1 January 2026 and that rooms are not available. The cooperative’s home, spring and lodging pages still say bathing is possible at all seven, and the spring shuttle timetable still lists an Ōgama stop. I could not confirm when or whether it reopens.

The inns’ pages list Tsuru no Yu, Taenoyu, Ganiba, Magoroku and Kyūkamura as open all year. Kuroyu’s page gives its season as mid-April to mid-November, though its introduction on the same page says early November; the cooperative’s spring page says it closes from mid-November to early April.

### The bath-hopping book and the bus

There are two products, and they are easy to confuse.

- **Yumeguri-chō**, the bath-hopping book. Semboku city’s English leaflet says it covers rides on the Yumeguri-gō bus and one bath at each of the seven inns. It is for overnight guests only, sold at each inn’s front desk, valid for one year from purchase, and covers day bathing only, not a stay. Extra baths are paid in cash. The cooperative also says guests only, cash only. The ministry’s 2017 plan described it as sold to guests by reservation; the current cooperative and city pages do not use that word.
- **Yumeguri map**, a one-day pass for unlimited rides on the Yumeguri-gō. The city’s 2020 leaflet says any guest or visitor can buy it; the cooperative’s current pages (checked 11 October 2026) list it, with the bath-hopping book, as sold to overnight guests only. A day visitor should ask before relying on it. It does not include baths, which are paid in cash at each inn.

The prices do not agree. Semboku city’s 2020 leaflet and the Tohoku tourism body give one pair of figures, JNTO gives one figure, and the cooperative gives a higher pair. None says whether tax is included, and none is dated, so I have not given any.

**The shuttle timetable.** The cooperative publishes two timetables:

- **Spring, from 15 April 2026**: five runs a day each way between Tsuru no Yu and Magoroku, calling at the inns in between. Until the snow melts, the Magoroku stop is at the Kuroyu car park.
- **Winter, from 10 November 2025**: five runs a day each way between Tsuru no Yu and Ganiba. It does not stop at Kuroyu or Magoroku; for Magoroku it says to get off at Ōgama and walk about 15 minutes, depending on the snow.

Neither timetable gives fares or says whether it runs every day. I could not find a timetable for the winter starting November 2026. Semboku city’s leaflet adds that the bus leaves on time and does not wait for latecomers, and that times can change with the weather and road conditions.

### Fees and hours

I have not given the inns’ bath fees. On each inn’s page the room rates carry a note on tax, but the day-bathing fees do not, so I could not confirm that they include tax.

Day-bathing hours and weekly closing days differ from inn to inn. They also differ between pages: the cooperative gives different hours for Kuroyu on its inn page and on its spring page. I have not given times. Check with the inn.

## Bathing there

- **Cash.** The bath-hopping book, the bus pass and extra baths are paid in cash, according to the cooperative and the city.
- **Winter.** Semboku city says some facilities close or change their hours in winter, and marks Kuroyu as closed for winter. It warns that phones may not be answered in winter and suggests asking the Tazawako tourist information centre "Folake", which is open all year. The winter shuttle skips Kuroyu and Magoroku (see above).
- **Feeling unwell from bathing.** The cooperative asks bathers to take care against *yu-atari*, feeling ill from too much bathing, and says its member inns employ staff qualified as onsen bathing instructors.
- **Tattoos and English.** I could not find official guidance in English on tattoos or on how to bathe from Semboku city, the tourism association, the Tohoku tourism body or JNTO. The cooperative’s English site, "RYOKAN STORIES", was made by a private company, Glocal Promotion; I found nothing on tattoos or etiquette in the pages I read there. The ministry’s 2017 plan said signs and bus stops were not adequate for foreign visitors and set out to add English, Chinese and Korean; it does not say how far that has gone. Ask each inn in advance about tattoos, and consider a private bath where there is one.

## What it is eaten with

I could not find a dish that both the inns’ side and the farm ministry tie to Nyūtō. I went through all 29 of Akita’s entries in the ministry’s list of local dishes: none mentions Nyūtō or Tazawako. Semboku appears in two (*sasamaki* and *kakizuke*), neither linked to Nyūtō. The cooperative mentions mountain vegetables and mushrooms as gifts of the land, without naming a dish, and I found no Nyūtō dish on the tourism association’s site.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Bus from Tazawako station.** The public bus is Ugo Kōtsū’s Nyūtō line. Its timetable, revised on 1 April 2026, shows 11 departures a day from Tazawako station, and the journey takes 38 minutes to the Tsuru no Yu entrance stop, 45 minutes to the Kyūkamura and Taenoyu stops, and 48 minutes to the last stop at Ganiba. It warns of delays with road and weather conditions and says some buses do not connect with the shinkansen. Ugo Kōtsū says its buses take touch payment and QR codes. I could not find whether there is a separate winter timetable.
- **Other figures for the bus.** The cooperative gives about 50 minutes; the tourism association about 45, noting that the nearest stop depends on your inn; the Tohoku tourism body about 40; JNTO about 45, and another JNTO article 30. The difference comes mostly from which stop you get off at.
- **Tsuru no Yu.** The bus does not stop at the inn itself. The Ugo Kōtsū timetable says to contact the inn and use its shuttle from Alpa Komakusa, or walk about 50 minutes from the Tsuru no Yu entrance stop. Semboku city gives about 90 minutes from the station, including that walk.
- **Rail to Tazawako.** From Tokyo by the Akita shinkansen, the cooperative gives 2 hours 55 minutes, the tourism association about 3 hours 12 minutes (as the fastest), and Semboku city’s English page about 2 hours 50 minutes. They also differ for Morioka (40, about 44 and about 30 minutes), Sendai and Akita. I could not open JR East’s own page.
- **From Akita airport.** By the Akita Airport Liner, the cooperative gives 2 hours 10 minutes, the tourism association about 130 minutes, and Semboku city about 150 minutes to Tsuru no Yu.
- **Car.** The cooperative gives about an hour from Morioka interchange on the Tohoku expressway and about an hour and a half from Akita city; the Tohoku tourism body gives about 70 minutes, and Semboku city about 85 minutes, both via Routes 46 and 341.
- **Winter roads.** I found no official notice about winter closure of the road to Nyūtō, either way. The prefecture’s list of roads closed in the 2024–25 winter names a section of Route 341 towards Tamagawa, not the Nyūtō road. Ask Semboku city or your inn before a winter trip.

So plan on roughly 40 to 50 minutes by bus from Tazawako, depending on your stop, and check the current timetables, especially in winter.

## What people get wrong

- **"Nyūtō is an onsen town with a main street."** It is seven separate inns, each with its own source, linked by a bus.
- **"All seven inns are open."** Ōgama’s page says it has been closed since 1 January 2026, and Kuroyu closes in winter.
- **"Anyone can buy the bath-hopping book."** The city and the cooperative say it is for overnight guests. The cooperative's current pages say the same for the bus pass, though the city's 2020 leaflet says anyone can buy it.
- **"Cards are fine."** The bath-hopping book, bus pass and extra baths are cash.
- **"Nyūtō itself is a national health resort onsen."** The designated area is the Tazawako Kōgen Onsenkyō, designated in 1967, which the ministry says consists of Nyūtō and Tazawako Kōgen.
- **"Tsuru no Yu dates from a single known year."** The cooperative’s chronology gives 1615; the ministry’s plan starts the story with the naming in 1708.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
