Gero Onsen is in Gifu prefecture, on the Hida river, and is one of the three springs that writers of the past grouped as Japan’s most famous, with Kusatsu and Arima. Its water is an alkaline simple spring that the town has supplied through a central management system since the 1970s. Around Gero station, a riverside source, public baths, a hot spring museum and many footbaths sit within walking distance. This page covers the water, how the town tells its history, how the baths and footbaths work, and what I could not confirm. The ten official spring types and how to read the sign by the bath are in the guide to onsen water types; the Honshu overview places Gero alongside the rest of Chubu.

## Where it came from

The tourism association’s site (gero-spa.com, which gives the Gero Onsen Tourism Association as its contact) tells the story in stages. I give each as it does.

- **The mountain.** The spring is said to have first been found near the summit of Mt Yugamine, 1,067 m high, about 4 km from today’s town. The site says Yugamine is considered a volcano formed by an eruption about 100,000 years ago. Yugamine is not on the weather agency’s list of active volcanoes, and I found no public source explaining what heats Gero’s water, so I do not call the water volcanic.
- **When.** There are two accounts, and this site does not choose. One places the discovery in the Tenryaku era (947–957), citing the *Hishū-shi*; the other in the Engi era (901–923), citing the *Hida Hennen Shiyō*.
- **The river.** Before 1265, in the middle of the Kamakura period, the spring near the summit stopped, and it was found again on the bank of the Hida river. The town calls this the white heron legend.
- **The visitor.** In 1489 the monk Banri Shūku, of Kyoto’s Five Mountains temples, came to Gero and praised the spring.

**"Three famous springs".** The site says Banri Shūku named Kusatsu, Arima and Yushima (Gero) in his *Baika Mujinzō*, and that the Edo-period Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan named Yushima alongside Arima and Kusatsu in his collected poems. So the grouping comes from writers. I found no government body that chose it. The site also says Hayashi Razan wrote that Gero was little known far away but that those who bathed felt its effect; that is his view, not evidence of any effect.

## The water

### What the town says

The tourism association’s site gives the spring as a simple spring (alkaline simple spring), pH 9.2, and publishes the analysis and a diagram of the management system as PDFs. It gives no number of sources, flow or temperature on that page. I could not find those figures on the inn cooperative’s site, Gero city’s top page or the museum’s page either.

The temperature comes from two places. The Gero Onsen Ryokan Cooperative’s site gives "84°C" and "alkaline simple spring". An English page run by Centrip Japan, which the association’s site links to, says the source is 84°C and "finally" 55°C. That page does not say what the 55°C is measured at, and I could not tell, so read it with care.

### Central management

The association’s site says Gero began a project to manage its hot spring centrally in 1971, and that the system has been running since 1974. The Centrip Japan page gives the same years and says the water serves more than 50 inns and hotels. I could not find an official description of how the system distributes the water, or how many sources feed it; the PDFs on the association’s site may cover it, but I have not read them.

## How the town works

The pattern is a compact one: from Gero station, the sources say, you can walk to Funsenchi in about 3 minutes, and to the Sagi-no-Ashiyu footbath and the hot spring museum in about 8.

**Funsenchi, the riverside source.** The association’s site says Funsenchi is free, that bathing there has been banned since 1 December 2021, and that it is now an open space on the Hida river for looking at Gero’s source. The inn cooperative’s English page still describes it as a "free open-air bath". These are two official pages from the same town that say different things; the association’s page gives the date of the ban, so I follow it on that point, but I note both.

**Public baths.** The association’s site lists Funsenchi, Shirasagi-no-Yu, the Kur Garden open-air bath and Sachi-no-Yu under hot springs. The Centrip Japan page speaks of "three public baths and Funsenchi". The association describes Shirasagi-no-Yu as a bath long loved by local people, and Sachi-no-Yu as a public bath attached to an inn, with seven kinds of bath including a waterfall bath and an open-air bath. I could not find these described as *kyōdōyu*, community baths run by residents in the way some other towns run theirs.

**Footbaths.** The inn cooperative lists the town’s footbaths, many of them free, each with its own hours:

- **Sagi-no-Ashiyu** — 24 hours, free, next to the central car park. The association says it is made of hinoki cypress, seats five side by side, has a roof, and was the town’s first footbath.
- **Venus no Ashiyu** — in front of Shirasagi-no-Yu, 10:00 to 21:00.
- **Yuamiya no Ashiyu** — ground floor of the Ryokan Kaikan, 9:00 to 18:30, with changes.
- **Tanokami no Ashiyu** — in the park beside Gero city hall, 24 hours, free.
- **Gasshō no Ashiyu** — inside Gasshō-mura (Gasshō Village); the footbath is free but entry to the village is ¥800.
- **The museum footbath** — at the hot spring museum, which charges admission.

The list also includes Miyabi no Ashiyu and the Sarubobo Ōgon footbath. It gives no manners.

**The hot spring museum.** Gero Hakken Onsen Hakubutsukan presents hot springs from the side of chemistry and of culture, has a footbath, and is arranged in five sections: the science of hot springs, the culture of hot springs, welcome to Gero Onsen, the hot spring doctor’s room, and fun hot spring challenges. It lists an admission price but does not say whether tax is included, so I do not give it.

## Bathing there

**Yumeguri tegata.** The inn cooperative sells a *yumeguri tegata*, a pass for touring baths, at ¥2,500 including tax (checked 10 October 2026). It is valid for six months from purchase and lets you bathe at three of the member inns. The cooperative’s page links to the list of member inns, where it is sold, and notices of days when bathing is restricted, so check those before you go.

**Public bath prices.** Shirasagi-no-Yu and the Kur Garden open-air bath list prices, but neither says whether they include tax, so I do not give them.

**Tattoos and manners.** I could not find a tattoo policy or bathing instructions in English on the association’s site, the Centrip Japan page, the inn cooperative’s English site, or the Japanese pages for the public baths and footbaths. I do not say whether you can bathe; ask the facility in advance, and a private bath is one option. The water types guide has what the tourism agency and JNTO say in general.

**Things to check.** Funsenchi is no longer for bathing. Footbath hours differ from one to the next, and two of them are inside places that charge entry.

### Food

The farm ministry’s *Our Local Cuisines* lists *keichan* for Gifu: chicken and vegetables stir-fried in a special sauce, with Gujō, Gero and the southern part of Takayama as the main areas where it is handed down. The ministry says it is thought to have been modelled around 1950 on the *jingisukan* eaten in parts of Gifu, began as a way to eat household hens that had stopped laying as a valuable source of protein, and that the name has two explanations — one from a name for grilled pork offal, one from *keijan*, "chicken sauce". It does not mention the hot spring. On Gero’s side, the gourmet site run by the Gero chamber of commerce lists *keichan* among Gero Onsen’s specialities. So Gero city is in the ministry’s entry, and the dish is on the town’s list; the ministry does not call it a hot spring dish.

The same chamber of commerce site also lists Nattoku pork, Hida beef (including Hida beef *hitsumabushi*), seared meat sushi, local sake tasting, lunches and sweets. The ministry lists *hoba miso*, miso grilled on a *hoba* leaf, with Hida and Takayama as its main areas; it does not name Gero, and I could not find it on Gero’s official pages, so I do not link the two.

## Getting there

Times as given on the official pages, checked on 10 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Nagoya** — the association’s museum page says about 1 hour 30 minutes from Nagoya station on the JR Takayama line, and about 3 hours 30 minutes from Osaka. The association’s sample timetable has a train taking about 1 hour 34 minutes, and says every train, including the limited express *Hida*, stops at Gero.
- **Direct bus for inn guests** — the inn cooperative runs a bus between Nagoya and Gero Onsen, one round trip a day, taking about 2 hours 30 minutes. It is only for guests of member inns and must be booked in advance. Its timetable and boarding point change, so check the cooperative’s page.
- **From Gero station** — about 3 minutes on foot to Funsenchi, about 8 minutes to the Sagi-no-Ashiyu and the museum, about 6 minutes by bus to Gasshō-mura, and about 1 hour by train to Takayama.

I could not find the inn cooperative’s rail and road routes in text, and the JNTO page I tried for Gero did not open.

## What people get wrong

- **"You can bathe in the river pool for free."** You could once. The association’s site says bathing has been banned since 1 December 2021, though an English page still says otherwise.
- **"The three famous springs is an official title."** It comes from writers of the Muromachi and Edo periods.
- **"Gero’s water is volcanic."** The association says Mt Yugamine is considered a volcano. I found no public source on what heats the water.
- **"Gero was founded in 947."** That is one of two accounts; the other says the Engi era, 901–923.
- **"Every footbath is free."** Most are, but two are inside places that charge entry.
- **"Tattoos are banned in Gero."** I could not find an official policy either way.
