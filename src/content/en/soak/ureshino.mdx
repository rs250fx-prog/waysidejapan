Ureshino is a hot spring town in Saga prefecture, on the old Nagasaki road, and a tea-growing area. Its water is known for feeling smooth, and its best-known dish is tofu simmered in that water. This page explains how the town works using Ureshino city, the Japan Tourism Agency’s multilingual commentary, the Ureshino Onsen tourism association, the Cultural Agency, the Japan Hot Spring Association and JNTO. Much of the history comes from the tourism association, and I say so where it does. Where sources disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how Ureshino compares with the rest of Kyushu, start from the [Kyushu hot springs overview](/en/soak/onsen-kyushu/). For what its spring names mean, see the page on [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/); for the notices about added water and recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). The neighbouring town of [Takeo](/en/soak/takeo/) is about half an hour away by bus.

## Where it came from

### The crane legend

The tourism agency, the tourism association and the city all tell the same story of the name, and all tell it as a legend. Empress Jingū, who is said to have lived around the 3rd century, came this way after a battle. She saw a white crane dip its wings in the river and fly off refreshed, and told her wounded soldiers to bathe there. When they were healed, she said *ureshii no*, "how glad I am", and the place took its name from that.

Takeo has a separate story about the same empress, with a sword hilt and a rock. They are different legends, and I do not join them.

### A mention in the Fudoki

The tourism association’s history page says the *Hizen no Kuni Fudoki*, which it dates to 713, records a hot spring on the eastern side that "heals people’s illnesses". The page does not name the passage or cite an edition, and I have not seen the original or a modern translation. Another of the association’s own history pages does not mention the *Fudoki* at all, and neither do the city’s Chinese-language page nor the tourism agency’s commentary. So this is the association’s account, not a quotation I have checked.

### Siebold

The tourism agency says the German scientist Philipp Franz von Siebold (1796–1866) stayed in Ureshino to analyse the spring water, and that the public bathhouse Siebold no Yu is named after him. The city’s Chinese-language page says the same. The city’s own page for the bathhouse describes only the building.

The tourism association’s history page goes further. It quotes Siebold’s account of his journey to Edo, with the result of an analysis by his assistant Bürger: that the water held mainly sulphates and a little chloride. It gives that visit as **1812**. Takeo city, citing the same journey for a bath in Takeo, gives **1826**. The two years do not agree, and I do not give one for Ureshino.

### A post town, and tea

The tourism agency says Ureshino was a post town on the Nagasaki road in the Edo period, and that the road was also called the "Sugar Road" because sugar was carried along it.

For tea, the tourism association gives two dates, both in the "it is said" form. In 1440, Chinese who had come to Hirado settled and grew tea. In 1504, a man from Ming China brought a Nanjing-style iron pan and taught the method of pan-fired tea, *kamairi-cha*; the association treats that as the beginning of Ureshino tea. The city’s English page describes the Great Tea Tree, about 350 years old, said to have been planted by Yoshimura Shinbei and designated a national natural monument. I could not read the city’s own tea page beyond its headings.

## The water

### Three names

The sources do not give one spring name for the town, so I give each.

- **Ureshino city**, for Siebold no Yu: a **sodium-hydrogen carbonate·chloride spring**.
- **The tourism association**: a ***jūsō* spring**, "sodium bicarbonate", with the modern name sodium-hydrogen carbonate·chloride in brackets, colourless, clear and slippery.
- **The Japan Hot Spring Association**: a **hydrogen-carbonate spring**.

*Jūsō* is an old name; the ministry’s table of old and new names, explained on the [hydrogen-carbonate page](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/), maps it to today’s sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring. The city and the association agree on the full modern name, chloride included. The Japan Hot Spring Association’s shorter entry leaves chloride out.

### pH and temperature

- **pH.** The tourism association gives weakly alkaline, pH 7.5 to 8.5. The tourism agency gives weakly alkaline, pH 7 to 8. The city’s Chinese-language page gives pH 7 to 8 but calls it strongly alkaline, which does not match the city’s other wording. I do not choose a band.
- **Sodium.** The association gives 400 to 500 mg of sodium per kilogram of water.
- **Temperature.** The association gives 85 to 95°C. The city’s page for Siebold no Yu gives no temperature.

I could not find an official count of sources or a figure for flow. The Japan Hot Spring Association’s entry has the spring type only, and I could not confirm an environment ministry plan for Ureshino.

### Why here

I could not find an official explanation of how Ureshino’s water forms. The tourism agency’s commentary does not give one, and I found no geological survey, prefectural or city source on it. So I do not call Ureshino a volcanic spring.

### "Skin beauty"

The tourism association says Ureshino is called one of **Japan’s three great skin-beauty springs**, *Nihon sandai bihada no yu*, together with Hinokami Onsen in Shimane and Kitsuregawa Onsen in Tochigi. It does not say who made the selection, and I could not find an official one. The city lists skin beauty among the water’s effects without giving a basis. These are the association’s and the city’s words; this page does not claim the water does anything for your skin.

## How the town works

### Siebold no Yu

The city-run public bathhouse is the one place in Ureshino where I could confirm a price with its tax status. As checked on 12 October 2026, the city’s page says:

- **Adults (15 to 69): 450 yen.** The page states that its prices include consumption tax.
- **Hours:** 6:00 to 22:00, last entry 21:30.
- **Closed:** the third Wednesday of each month, or the next day if that Wednesday is a public holiday.
- **The building** is described as Gothic in style, with a Taishō-romantic feel.

The page says nothing about tattoos.

### Three free footbaths

The city lists three footbaths, all free. Each may be out of use on Wednesday mornings for cleaning.

- **Yuyado Hiroba:** 8:00 to 23:00. It also has a foot-steaming bath, which the city calls rare in Japan.
- **Yuyū Hiroba, "Siebold’s Footbath":** open 24 hours. The building’s lights go off at 3:00.
- **Yuttsura Hiroba:** 7:00 to 23:00.

The tourism agency’s commentary on a footbath here is quoted on the [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/) page; it is a local description, not a rule.

### Day bathing at the inns

The tourism association lists day-bathing facilities, including inns that take outside bathers. The list gives no prices, hours or tattoo policies, so I cannot give them here.

## Bathing there

I found no official prices for baths in Ureshino with a stated tax status other than Siebold no Yu’s, so no others are given.

- **Tattoos.** I found no official guidance for Ureshino. The city’s English page of sights says nothing about tattoos or bathing, the tourism association’s front page has no English link or tattoo information that I could find, and the Siebold no Yu page does not mention them. JNTO’s general guide (undated) says some onsen refuse people with tattoos, that you should check a facility’s policy in advance, that a private bath, *kashikiri-buro*, is an option, and that the small cover sheets on sale only suit very small tattoos. The wider picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
- **Manners.** JNTO’s guide says to leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room, wash before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water, tie up long hair, not swim or splash, and not take phones or cameras in.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Ureshino source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What the sources do say is the spring names above and the association’s description of the water as slippery.

## What it is eaten with

### Hot-spring tofu

***Ureshino Onsen yudōfu***, tofu simmered in hot spring water, is a Cultural Agency **100-year food**, certified in fiscal 2024 in the "traditional" category. The agency’s list names the group as the Ureshino Onsen Yudōfu Promotion Council; the tourism association writes it as the Ureshino Onsen Yudōfu Council. The agency’s page says tofu simmered in the spring water melts into a soft texture, and mentions the diary of the haiku poet Taneda Santōka. It is not in the farm ministry’s list of Saga’s local dishes, as the [Kyushu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-kyushu/) page notes.

The tourism association adds its own account:

- The balance of the water’s components acts on the protein in the tofu. That is the association’s explanation; I found no official test of it.
- The method is said to have been brought from Tang China by Japanese envoys.
- It is made by five tofu shops in the town and served at about 25 inns and hotels.

### Tea

The tea dates above are the tourism association’s. I found no official source that ties the tea to the hot springs themselves.

## Getting there

As given on the tourism association’s access page, which shows no update date, checked on 12 October 2026. Timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page; the association gives some taxi fares without saying whether tax is included, and no rail fares.

- **From Hakata by train and bus.** Limited express from Hakata to JR Takeo-Onsen station, then a JR bus to Ureshino Onsen, about **30 minutes**. The association gives the train as about **60 minutes**; Takeo’s tourism association gives the same train as **70 minutes at the fastest**. Allow about an hour either way. From Fukuoka Airport, take the subway to Hakata first.
- **From Fukuoka Airport by highway bus.** The *Kyūshū-gō* to the Ureshino interchange, about **70 minutes**. Another line on the same page gives Fukuoka Airport terminal to Ureshino bus centre as about 1 hour 20 minutes; the layout makes it unclear how the two lines relate.
- **From Nagasaki Airport.** A Saihi bus, about 25 minutes, then a JR bus, about 25 minutes, via Sonogi-honmachi. About 40 minutes by taxi.
- **From Nagasaki city.** By road to the Ōmura interchange and then the *Kyūshū-gō*. The association gives no time.
- **By car.** About 1 hour from Fukuoka city to the Ureshino interchange, about 2 hours from Kumamoto and about 1 hour from Nagasaki. From the interchange to the town, about 5 minutes.

The association’s page directs rail travellers to Takeo-Onsen station and does not mention Ureshino-Onsen station on the Nishi-Kyushu shinkansen. I found no official journey time from that station, so I do not give one.

## What people get wrong

- **"Ureshino is a volcanic spring."** I found no official source that explains how its water forms, volcanic or otherwise.
- **"There is one official spring name."** The city, the tourism association and the Japan Hot Spring Association word it differently, and the pH is given in two bands.
- **"The three great skin-beauty springs is an official ranking."** The tourism association uses the phrase without naming who chose them.
- **"Siebold came in 1812."** That is the tourism association’s year. Takeo city gives 1826 for the same journey, so I give neither for Ureshino.
- **"Hot-spring tofu is a farm ministry local dish."** It is a Cultural Agency 100-year food and is not in the farm ministry’s Saga list.
- **"Champon is Ureshino’s, or Unzen’s."** The farm ministry ties Obama champon to the inns’ association of Obama, a separate hot spring town in Unzen city; see the [Unzen](/en/soak/unzen/) guide and [champon](/en/eat/champon/).
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications by spring type is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).
