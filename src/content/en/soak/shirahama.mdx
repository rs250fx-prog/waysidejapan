Nanki-Shirahama Onsen is in the town of Shirahama, Wakayama prefecture, on the Pacific coast. Its tourism association counts 15 sources and 23 places to use the water — baths, footbaths and the like — and the town runs several public baths, one of them a rock pool facing the ocean. This page covers what the sources are, what the town and its bodies say about its age, how the public baths work for a visitor, and how to get there, using the town office, Wakayama prefecture, the tourism association, the inn cooperative and national agencies. Where they disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath.

The ten official spring types and how to read pH and tonicity on the sign are in the separate guide to onsen water types, and what free-flowing water means is in the *kakenagashi* piece; the Honshu overview places Shirahama among the other Kansai springs.

## Where it came from

### Muro and 657

Every official source agrees on one written record and disagrees on the count of years.

- **Shirahama town**, on its Saki no Yu page (updated 16 July 2026): Shirahama became known as a hot spring around the Asuka and Nara periods, about 1,400 years ago, and Saki no Yu was where it began. It was then called the hot spring of Muro (牟婁温湯) and appears in the *Nihon Shoki* and the *Man’yōshū*. Prince Arima is said to have recommended it to Empress Saimei in 657, and later visits by Emperors Jitō and Monmu are recorded. The town’s geosite page also says the *Nihon Shoki* records it under that name in the Asuka period.
- **The tourism association**: Prince Arima’s visit in 657 is in the *Nihon Shoki*, and Empress Saimei came the following year, then Jitō and Monmu. Its Saki no Yu description speaks of "Shirahama Onsen’s 1,350-year history" without saying where it counts from; a notice it posted in 2024 says the *Nihon Shoki* mentioned it "1,300 years ago". It calls the Gyōkō source the oldest in Shirahama.
- **The Shirahama Onsen Ryokan Cooperative**, an industry body for the town’s inns: the spring appears in the *Man’yōshū* and the *Nihon Shoki*, and Empress Saimei and Emperor Tenji among others came from the capital to bathe. Its top page says the *Man’yōshū* calls it "the old spring of Muro".
- **Wakayama prefecture’s English tourism site**: "more than 14 centuries".
- **JNTO**: a "1,300-year history" on one page and "over 1,350 years" on another.

So the official figures run from about 1,300 to more than 1,400 years, and the tourism association itself uses two. This site does not choose. The fixed point the town and the association share is Prince Arima’s visit in 657.

### The seven springs of Yuzaki

Saki no Yu stands in the Yuzaki district. The tourism association says that from the end of the Edo period into Meiji, seven places in Yuzaki where hot water rose naturally were called the "seven springs of Yuzaki", and it does not list their names. Its Saki no Yu page describes the bath as the only surviving basin of the seven, which it says go back to the time of the *Man’yōshū*. The two pages put the name in different periods; I do not decide between them. What both support is that, of the seven, Saki no Yu is the one basin left.

The town’s Saki no Yu page adds that the thatched building of the Edo period was given a tiled roof, that in the Meiji era the bathhouse layout was changed over the question of foreign visitors bathing, and that in 1879 a foreign adviser employed by the Home Ministry recorded high praise for the seven springs. The name is not clear in what I read, so I do not give it.

### What came later

The prefecture’s environmental health research centre, in its 2022 annual report, says development took off in the 1960s and 1970s, inns multiplied along the shore, and Shirahama is now popular as one of the leading seaside resorts in the Kansai region as well as for its springs. I could not find an official timeline of the railway, the inns or the town’s policies from Meiji onward, so I give none.

### "Three ancient springs"

The town says Shirahama "is said to be" one of Japan’s three oldest springs, with Dōgo and Arima. The tourism association and the inn cooperative say it is counted among Japan’s three ancient springs (*Nihon san-kotō*), the prefecture’s English site says the same with Dōgo and Arima, and the prefecture’s research report calls them the three ancient springs with a slightly different word (*san-kosen*). A site presenting itself as Dōgo Onsen’s official site, whose operator I could not confirm, says only five spring towns appear in the *Man’yōshū*, beginning with "the three ancient springs" of Dōgo, Arima and Shirahama.

So every source I read names the same three. None says who chose them or when, and I could not confirm that it is an official title.

### Not on the national health resort list

The environment ministry designates "national health resort hot springs" (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*), 79 nationwide as of October 2022 according to its page. Its list for Wakayama, as I opened it, has two: the Kumano Hongū and Ryūjin spring areas. Shirahama is not on it. The list carries no update date of its own, and the ministry does not say anything about Shirahama directly; this is simply what the list shows.

## The water

The town describes the Gyōkō and 砿湯 water as clear and moderately salty, and the 生絹湯 water as clear and strongly salty. The inn cooperative writes the same type slightly differently, as sodium-chloride onsen; it is the same thing.

One description differs. The town’s geosite page calls Shirahama a high-temperature spring whose main component is sodium hydrogen carbonate, while the town’s bath pages and the association’s table centre on sodium chloride. I give both and use the per-source tables above for the spring types.

I could not find an official total of sources or a total flow for Shirahama alone. The environment ministry’s statistics are by prefecture, and the association’s 15 sources come with no year. The prefecture’s English tourism site says there are more than 90 hotels with their own onsen.

### Why it rises here

The town’s geosite page explains the setting rather than the heat. Shirahama lies on the northern extension of the Kareki-nada arc-shaped dyke, and the water rises from the upper part of the Tanabe Group, a layer of sedimentary rock. Most sources were originally free-flowing, and they are concentrated in Yuzaki.

The prefecture’s 2022 research report says some sources exceed 80°C, the main components are sodium with chloride and hydrogen carbonate, and many sources contain sulphur. It adds that there were once many free-flowing sources but that most are now pumped. Only the prefecture says that; the town and the association do not.

Where the heat and the water come from, I could not find an official explanation specific to Shirahama. The national geological survey’s general page on deep fluids says Japan has many hot springs away from volcanoes and that for most of them the heat source and the origin of the water are not well understood; it does not mention Shirahama.

### Is the supply stable?

On 7 October 1976 the prefecture drew up guidelines for protecting the springs of Shirahama, Tsubaki and the surrounding area, and since 1997 its research centre has surveyed them every four years. Its 2022 report, covering five sources surveyed in November 2021, says that before the protection measures the sources varied widely and were unstable, and that since then the four sources in Shirahama and Tsubaki have been stable in temperature, flow, evaporation residue and the balance of main components. It concludes it sees no effect of depletion. One source in Tanabe city had stable components but a flow that has kept falling since 1997.

## How the town works

The town’s onsen pages list its public baths: the open-air Shirasuna, Muro no Yu, Saki no Yu, Shirara-yu, another public bath (Matsu no Yu), footbaths, and springs in the Hiki River area. The tourism association lists 24 baths, footbaths and sources, and names Saki no Yu, Shirara-yu, Muro no Yu, Shirasuna and Matsu no Yu among those open for day bathing. The inn cooperative’s "public baths and footbaths" page (copyright 2019) picks out Saki no Yu, Muro no Yu and Shirara-yu — the last with a bath on its second floor (2F) looking out over Shirarahama beach — and three footbaths, at Yusakihama plaza, Tsukumoto and Mifune. It also says some public baths are free, and advertises a bath stamp rally I could not read.

So the shape is 15 sources spread around the town, used by town-run public baths, inn baths and footbaths.

### The shore

The baths sit on a coast the town describes in its own pages:

- **Shirarahama**, the beach, is about 620 m long and made of quartz sand, about 90% silica. The town says it opens for swimming around Golden Week, earlier than any other beach on Honshu by its own account, and stays open until mid-September, and that it has been twinned with Waikiki Beach in Honolulu since 2000. The town’s page is in Japanese only.
- **Engetsu island**, officially Takashima, is 130 m north to south, 35 m east to west and 25 m high, and takes its popular name from the sea cave through its middle. The town lists it among Wakayama’s 100 sunset spots and gives about 18:30 in summer and 16:30 in winter as the time to see it.
- **Senjōjiki** is a broad shelf of sandstone formed 18 to 15 million years ago, sloping from the tip of Setozaki towards the Pacific. The town warns of strong winds.
- **Sandanbeki** appears on the tourism association’s list of footbaths; I did not read an official description of the cliffs themselves.

### No shared pass

I could not find an official shared pass for visitors walking between baths, as at Kinosaki, from the town, the tourism association or the prefecture. The town does sell a public bath pass valid for one year from issue, but it covers only Shirara-yu and Muro no Yu, not Saki no Yu, and is bought at the town office’s tourism section. The page lists prices for three age bands without saying whether they include tax, so I do not give them.

### Saki no Yu

Saki no Yu is the town’s open-air bath on the shore. JNTO describes its rock pools facing the Pacific, where you can feel the waves hitting the rocks; the prefecture’s English page says waves can spray the bathers. Because the bath water flows into the sea, the town says shampoo, conditioner and soap cannot be used.

### Shirasuna

Shirasuna is an open-air bath inside the Shirarahama beach area. The town’s page (updated 5 August 2020, but with fees and hours given for fiscal 2026) says it is free for fiscal 2026, uses the 砿湯 source, is free-flowing, mixed, worn with swimsuits, and has no supplies, so shampoo and the like cannot be used. The prefecture’s English page also says men and women bathe together in swimsuits.

## Bathing there

### Hours and closing days

From the town’s bath pages (Saki no Yu updated 16 July 2026; Muro no Yu and Shirara-yu 1 March 2025; Matsu no Yu 9 June 2026; Shirasuna as above), checked 11 October 2026:

| Bath | Hours | Closed |
|---|---|---|
| Saki no Yu | October–March 8:00–17:00; April–June and September 8:00–18:00; July–August 7:00–19:00. Last entry 30 minutes before closing | No regular closing day; may close temporarily for maintenance |
| Muro no Yu | 7:00–9:00 Shirahama residents only; 9:00–11:00 (last entry 10:30) and 14:00–21:30 (last entry 21:00) for everyone | Every Tuesday |
| Shirara-yu | Same as Muro no Yu | Every Thursday |
| Matsu no Yu | 13:00–21:00 | Every Tuesday |
| Shirasuna (fiscal 2026) | May–June and 1–13 September, weekends and holidays only, 10:00–15:00; 1 July–31 August daily, 12:00–17:30. Last entry 30 minutes before closing | — |

The official sources do not all agree:

- **Saki no Yu’s closing day.** The town and the tourism association say it has no regular closing day. The prefecture’s English page, which has no update date, says it is closed on Wednesdays and open 8:00–17:00 (to 18:00 in May, June and September). I do not know why they differ.
- **Shirasuna’s hours.** The prefecture’s English page, also undated, gives 10:00–15:00 (to 19:00 from 1 July to 15 September), closed Mondays (or the next day after a holiday), open daily from 1 July to 15 September, with room for 100. That does not match the town’s fiscal 2026 schedule above.

Check the town’s pages before you go.

### Children and mixed bathing

The tourism association’s Saki no Yu page says that, following a change in the ordinance, at the three town baths — Shirara-yu, Muro no Yu and Saki no Yu — children of roughly seven and over may no longer bathe on the side of the opposite sex.

### Prices

The town’s pages list admission prices for Saki no Yu, Muro no Yu, Shirara-yu and Matsu no Yu, but none says whether they include tax, so I do not give them. None of the town, tourism association or prefecture pages I read gives a price with tax included.

### Footbaths

The tourism association’s list of footbaths is Sandanbeki, Yusakihama plaza, Yanagibashi, Tsukumoto, Mifune and Tsubaki Onsen. For Mifune it gives free use from 8:00 to 22:00, from 7:00 in July and August, with the lights going off at 22:00, and a view of Engetsu island. The town’s footbath page also calls its footbaths free, but it was last updated in February 2016, so check the others before relying on it.

### Tattoos and English

**Tattoos.** I found nothing on tattoos from the town, the tourism association, the inn cooperative, the prefecture’s English tourism site or JNTO’s Shirahama pages. JNTO’s national onsen page says policies differ by facility and suggests checking in advance. Ask each bath or inn before you go. The water types guide covers the national picture, including private baths.

**In English.** I could not find an official English guide to bathing in Shirahama. The town’s "Foreign Language" page links its site to Google Translate for English, Chinese and Korean and says the machine translation may not be accurate. The tourism association links to a translated version of its site, which I could not open, and its practical-information page lists translation apps but says nothing about how to bathe. The prefecture’s English pages say nothing about washing or tattoos; the one rule they give is swimsuits at Shirasuna. What a visitor can rely on is the town’s: no soap at Saki no Yu or Shirasuna, swimsuits at Shirasuna, and the residents-only morning hours.

## Food

The one dish I found on both the farm ministry’s list and Shirahama’s official side is *sanma-zushi*, pressed sushi of Pacific saury. The farm ministry lists it for southern Wakayama, made with saury from the Kumano Sea, and names the Nishimuro and Higashimuro districts as where differences in how the fish is opened can be seen; Shirahama is in Nishimuro district, but the ministry’s page does not name Shirahama. The tourism association’s autumn page lists *sanma-zushi* as a fish sushi essential for celebrations in the Kinan and Shirahama area, made with saury from off the Kii Channel and citrus vinegar of *yuzu* or *daidai*. The two describe the sea differently, the Kumano Sea and the Kii Channel; what they share is the dish.

*Kue*, longtooth grouper, appears only on the Shirahama side. The inn cooperative — an industry body, not a public one — features it as "the phantom fish": wild fish live on the rocky shores of the Kii Channel and are hard to catch, which makes them costly, while farming in Shirahama has recently made a steady supply possible. It is served as sashimi, in hot pot and in each inn’s own dishes. The tourism association calls it a costly fish with very few wild catches, says Kindai University succeeded in fully farming it, and calls it ideal for winter hot pot; another of its pages describes it as a grouper growing to about 1 m and 30 to 50 kg. The farm ministry’s list of Wakayama’s local dishes has nothing under *kue*.

The tourism association also lists seasonal local foods such as spiny lobster (*Ise-ebi*), which it says is at its best in winter with a season from October to April, though its autumn page says the season opens between September and December. None of these is on the farm ministry’s list. The prefecture’s English article on Shirahama and Kushimoto (February 2024) mentions spiny lobster grilled or as sashimi, bonito at its best in spring and autumn, and the Toretore fish market.

Some foods people link with this coast are tied by official pages to other places. The farm ministry gives *umeboshi*, pickled plums, to Minabe and Tanabe, and *shirasu-don*, whitebait on rice, to northern and central Wakayama; neither page mentions Shirahama, and the tourism association calls the Nankō plum only a variety representing the Kinan region. Tuna is tied officially to Katsuura: JNTO describes Katsuura’s market as handling the world’s largest landings of fresh tuna, with auctions from 7 a.m., and the ministry’s Wakayama overview mentions longline tuna fishing in Nachikatsuura. The association’s Shirahama food pages do not mention tuna, so I do not call it a Shirahama dish.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026.

- **Limited express Kuroshio.** JR West’s page says the Kuroshio runs between Kyoto or Shin-Osaka and Wakayama, Kainan, Shirahama and Shingū, with no journey times. The tourism association’s access page (copyright 2023, no update date) gives about 2 hours 30 minutes from Osaka, about 2 hours 15 minutes from Tennōji, about 2 hours 40 minutes from Shin-Osaka, and about 3 hours from Kyoto via Shin-Osaka. The inn cooperative gives about 2 hours 30 minutes from Shin-Osaka, about 2 hours from Tennōji and about 2 hours 50 minutes from Kyoto. JNTO gives about 2.5 hours from Shin-Osaka.
- **Highway bus.** The tourism association gives ten round trips a day, about 3 hours 20 minutes from OCAT in Namba and about 3 hours 30 minutes from JR Osaka, and about 10 hours 30 minutes from Busta Shinjuku. The inn cooperative gives about 3 hours 30 minutes from Osaka.
- **Air.** The tourism association and the inn cooperative give about 75 minutes from Haneda by JAL to Nanki-Shirahama Airport; JNTO gives about an hour.
- **Into the onsen area.** The tourism association gives about 10 to 15 minutes by bus or taxi from the station or the airport. JNTO gives about 20 minutes by bus from the airport to Shirahama Station on one page, and 10 minutes by bus from the airport, or from the station to Shirarahama, on another; the destinations differ.

The figures differ between sources — from Shin-Osaka about 2 hours 30 minutes or about 2 hours 40 minutes, from Tennōji about 2 hours 15 minutes or about 2 hours — so plan on roughly 2 hours 15 minutes to 3 hours from Kyoto, Osaka or Shin-Osaka by Kuroshio.

JR West’s Kansai Wide Area Pass page (information as of 1 August 2024) says the pass can be used for a trip to Shirahama and that reserved seats on trains such as the Kuroshio must be booked before boarding. Its price is not given with its tax status, so I do not give it, and fares are not on this page. JR West’s English page for a rail pass sold with a JAL flight from Haneda to Nanki-Shirahama says a separate limited-express ticket is needed for trains such as the Kuroshio. I could not find official directions from Kansai Airport: the tourism association, the prefecture, JR West and JNTO pages I read give none, the prefecture’s English access page has only a route-search tool, and the airport’s own pages could not be opened.

## What people get wrong

- **"Every public bath is open to visitors all day."** Muro no Yu and Shirara-yu are for residents only from 7 to 9 a.m., close in the late morning, and each has a weekly closing day. Matsu no Yu opens only in the afternoon, and Shirasuna only in the warmer months.
- **"An onsen by the sea is seawater."** The sources here are listed as sodium chloride, chloride-hydrogen carbonate, sulphur and simple springs. The town explains them by the local geology, a dyke and the Tanabe Group, not by the sea; where the heat comes from is not officially explained.
- **"Shirahama is exactly 1,300 (or 1,400) years old."** The official sources give anything from about 1,300 to more than 1,400 years. The fixed point they share is Prince Arima’s visit in 657.
- **"The three ancient springs is an official ranking."** The town, the association, the prefecture and Dōgo’s side all name the same three; none says who chose them.
- **"Shirahama is a designated health resort."** It is not on the environment ministry’s list as I opened it; Wakayama’s two are Kumano Hongū and Ryūjin.
- **"Shirahama is a tuna town."** Official pages tie tuna to Katsuura, not to Shirahama.
- **"There is a pass for all the public baths."** The only pass I found is the town’s one-year pass for Shirara-yu and Muro no Yu.
- **"Saki no Yu is a place to wash."** No soap or shampoo, because the water runs into the sea.
