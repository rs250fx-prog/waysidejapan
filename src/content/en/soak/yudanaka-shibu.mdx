Yudanaka-Shibu Onsen is a group of spa towns in the town of Yamanouchi, Nagano. Its best-known part, Shibu, keeps nine public baths that only its overnight guests may open, with a key borrowed from their inn; day visitors get one. About 2.5 km up the Yokoyu river is the monkey park where Japanese macaques bathe in hot water. This page explains how it works using Yamanouchi town, the town’s tourism bureau (Yamanouchi Machizukuri Kankōkyoku), the Shibu Onsen ryokan association, the monkey park’s own site, the Japan Tourism Agency and the national geological survey. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what the spring names mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for what "free-flowing" means on a notice, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). The [Honshu hot springs guide](/en/soak/onsen-honshu/) covers the region, and [Nozawa Onsen](/en/soak/nozawa/), also in Nagano, has shared village baths too.

## Where it came from

### Nine towns under one name

Yamanouchi town’s list of hot springs (updated 26 March 2024) places these under Yudanaka-Shibu Onsen: Yudanaka, Shin-Yudanaka, Hoshikawa, Hotaka, Kakuma, Andai, Shibu, Kanbayashi and Jigokudani. It lists the springs on the Shiga Kōgen side separately. The tourism bureau describes nine spa towns spread along historic stone-paved streets, which flourished as *tōji* resorts, places to stay for days or weeks to bathe, from the Edo period.

### The legends

The sources tell the same stories, and all of them as stories.

- **The Shibu Onsen ryokan association** says the spring is said to have been found 1,300 years ago by the monk Gyōki. In the Warring States period, it says, it was one of Takeda Shingen’s hidden springs, where soldiers wounded in the battles of Kawanakajima were said to have been nursed. Since then it has grown as a *tōji* resort and a post town on the Kusatsu road.
- **Yamanouchi town** says Shibu is said to have been opened by Gyōki, a monk of the Nara period, and also has a history as Shingen’s hidden spring.

About 1,300 years ago and the Nara period fit together. Both sources mark the Gyōki story as something "said" or "handed down", and no record dates it.

Yamanouchi town also says Yudanaka’s history goes back to the 7th century and links it to the haiku poet Kobayashi Issa, with a walk named after him. That is a different spa from Shibu, with a different date, so I keep them apart.

### The baths and the people who keep them

The association says Shibu’s outside baths, *sotoyu*, have long been shared baths and part of residents’ daily lives, and that residents clean them, look after the sources and run them. It calls visitors "one-night residents" who join in, and frames this as responsible tourism.

## The water

### What the town lists

Yamanouchi town’s list gives a main spring type and a temperature range for each spring, with no year of analysis. For example:

- **Yudanaka:** chloride spring, sulphate spring; 65–93°C.
- **Shibu:** simple spring, chloride spring; 58–98°C; colourless and clear, earth-coloured, or cloudy white.
- **Kanbayashi:** chloride spring, sulphate spring; 65–70°C.
- **Jigokudani:** chloride, sulphate and sulphur springs; 70–85°C.

The town and the other official sites do not give a count of sources or a total flow.

### What the Shibu association lists

The association says Shibu’s inns and outside baths are 100% *gensen kakenagashi*, straight from the source, with no water added and no heating. It says there are so many sources that hot water comes up almost wherever you dig, and that each source is different. Its page for each bath gives these spring names and temperatures, with no year of analysis:

| Bath | Spring name (association’s wording) | At the source | In use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Hatsu-yu | Sodium-calcium sulphate-chloride, hypotonic, weakly acidic, high-temperature | 65.0°C | 56°C |
| 2 Sasa no Yu | Same, weakly acidic | 65.0°C | 55°C |
| 3 Wata no Yu | Same, weakly acidic | 65.0°C | 42°C |
| 4 Take no Yu | Same, weakly alkaline | 86.5°C | 50°C |
| 5 Matsu no Yu | Sodium-calcium sulphide-sulphate, weakly alkaline | 86.5°C | — |
| 6 Mearai no Yu | Similar type, neutral | 50.6°C | — |
| 7 Nanakuri no Yu | Neutral | 50.5°C | — |
| 8 Shinmei-taki no Yu | Neutral | 58.7°C | — |
| 9 Ōyu | Neutral | "50.°C", as printed | — |

The town’s "simple spring, chloride spring" for Shibu and the association’s longer names differ in wording. The town gives a main type for the whole area; the association gives a name for each bath. I give both and do not reconcile them. The association also lists what each bath is said to help; this page does not repeat it.

### Where the heat comes from

The town’s, the bureau’s and the association’s pages do not explain where the heat comes from.

The national geological survey’s list of Quaternary volcanoes includes "Shiga", the "Shiga Kōgen volcano group" and Kōsha-san, but not Yokoteyama as a separate entry. Quaternary means active within roughly the last 2.6 million years; it does not mean active today. The weather agency’s list of the volcanoes it monitors does not include Shiga Kōgen, Kōsha-san or Yokoteyama, though it does include Kusatsu-Shirane. No official source links these mountains to the water here.

**The Jigokudani geyser.** Yamanouchi town says the "Shibu no Jigokudani Funsen", which shoots up from the bed of the Yokoyu river, is a national natural monument. The date of designation is not confirmed. The tourism agency’s multilingual description, written in 2019, calls it a geothermal phenomenon in which water above 90°C spouts constantly from the river bed in a column reaching 20 m.

## How the town works

### The street

The association describes stone-paved streets, many wooden buildings from the Taishō to early Shōwa periods, and hot-water pipes running everywhere.

### Kyūtō meguri, the nine-bath round

The association’s rules, as checked on 13 October 2026:

- **Who.** Only people staying at an inn in Shibu may use the nine *sotoyu*. Each inn lends a key made for them.
- **Cost and hours.** Free, 6:00 to 22:00. Sometimes a bath is shut for cleaning or other reasons.
- **What is not there.** No showers, shampoo or body soap, and no lockers for valuables.
- **What to bring.** Change into your *yukata* and bring a bath towel and a *tenugui*, the thin hand towel. No shoes in the changing room.
- **Heat.** The baths are free-flowing and very hot. If you add cold water, turn it off once the bath is bearable.

The association’s Japanese page on how to enter the baths sets out ten steps, among them pouring water over yourself first, turning off the tap and greeting the people already there.

**The prayer towel.** The association says you go round the nine baths, each from a different source, stamping a *kigan tenugui*, a prayer towel, and finish by paying your respects at Shibu Takayakushi and receiving a final seal, which completes your wish. It says the round is believed to wash away suffering (*ku*, which sounds like nine), ward off misfortune, and bring safe childbirth and long life. The towels are sold at each inn; the association gives no price. No official source dates when the nine baths began.

### Day visitors: Ōyu only

The association says day visitors may use only the ninth bath, **Ōyu**. You buy a ticket at the Shibu tourist information office or at an inn near Ōyu and show it at the place that unlocks the door. Day bathing is from 10:00 to 17:00, with occasional closures. Washing your hair is not allowed, and there is no shampoo. As checked on 13 October 2026, the association’s front page carried notices of Ōyu closures on 9 and 18 September, so check its site for current closures. I do not give the price, because the association does not say whether it includes tax.

Yamanouchi town says the three shared baths at Kakuma Onsen are free for overnight guests. Official sources give no total for the outside baths across the whole area.

### The monkey park

**Jigokudani Yaen-kōen**, the monkey park, is about 2.5 km up the Yokoyu river from Shibu, according to the association. What the park’s own site says, as checked on 13 October 2026:

- **Fees, tax included.** Adults (18 and over) 800 yen; children (primary to high school) 400 yen; under 6 free with a guardian. There are group, annual-pass and disability discounts. The park has announced new fees **from 1 December 2026**; their amounts are not confirmed, so these are the fees until 30 November 2026.
- **Hours.** Summer (April to October) 8:30 to 17:00; winter (November to March) 9:00 to 16:00. No closing days, but it may close for weather, the monkeys’ behaviour or disasters. It announced online tickets on 6 August 2026.
- **The monkeys.** There are days when no monkeys appear at all.
- **No bathing.** The park states that it is not a bathing facility and that you cannot bathe with the Japanese macaques.
- **Rules.** Do not show or give them food, do not touch them, and do not stare into their eyes. Drones, selfie sticks and putting a camera into the bath are banned. Dogs and cats, including assistance dogs, may not enter. Wheelchairs and carts cannot be used, because the paths are unpaved and have steps. The park says people who break the rules may be asked to leave.

**Why the monkeys bathe.** The park says it opened in 1964 and that its monkeys were on the cover of the American magazine *LIFE* in 1970. Feeding made the monkeys stay longer, and at one point a young monkey learned to get into the outdoor bath of Kōrakukan, an inn near the park; the habit spread, so the park built an outdoor bath of its own for the monkeys. It says they bathe as a way to get through winters colder than −10°C. The tourism agency’s description says the same, naming the riverside bath of Kōrakukan, founded in 1864, as where it started.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The Shibu Onsen ryokan association has English pages, including its front page and pages on the nine baths and how to use them. Yamanouchi town’s site uses a translation widget, with no separately written English text. The monkey park has an English site with hours, fees and rules.
- **Tattoos.** The association, the town and the tourism bureau say nothing on tattoos. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn in advance.
- **Crowds.** The association’s answer is not an entry limit but the key: the nine baths are kept for guests, as "one-night residents". At the monkey park, the park asks visitors to follow its rules.
- **Compared with city baths.** No official source here compares the water with baths in the cities. What the association does describe is a difference within Shibu itself: the inn’s bath is the inn’s, while the *sotoyu* are residents’ shared baths that guests join.

## What it is eaten with

No official page ties a dish to Yudanaka-Shibu. The town’s, the tourism bureau’s and the association’s pages on the springs and baths mention no food. This matches what the guide to [Chūbu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/) found.

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, because the official pages do not say whether they include tax.

- **From Tokyo by train.** The tourism bureau gives about 2 hours 15 minutes: the Hokuriku shinkansen to Nagano, then the Nagano Electric Railway to Yamanouchi. From Osaka it gives about 4 hours 30 minutes, via Nagoya or by the Thunderbird and the Hokuriku shinkansen.
- **The park’s own route.** The monkey park gives 79 minutes at the fastest from Tokyo to Nagano by the Hokuriku shinkansen, 38 minutes from Nagano to Yudanaka by the Nagano Electric Railway’s limited express, then 15 minutes by bus from Yudanaka station to the Snow Monkey Park stop and about 35 minutes on foot to the park. Or take the express bus from Nagano station towards Shiga Kōgen, 41 minutes to the Snow Monkey Park stop.
- **In summer.** The park also mentions a taxi from Yudanaka station by way of the Jigokudani car park, about 15 minutes’ walk from the park. The association says the paid Jigokudani car park on the Shibu side closes in winter.
- **By car.** The tourism bureau gives about 3 hours 30 minutes from Tokyo.
- **Snow.** The park says that in winter the paths and steps are slippery with snow and ice, and recommends simple crampons. Winter road closures are not covered here.

## What people get wrong

- **"You can bathe with the monkeys."** The park says you cannot, and that it is not a bathing facility.
- **"Anyone can do the nine-bath round."** Only Shibu’s overnight guests get a key. Day visitors can use Ōyu.
- **"Shibu was founded by Gyōki 1,300 years ago."** The association and the town both give that as a legend.
- **"The springs come from the Shiga Kōgen volcanoes."** The geological survey lists them as Quaternary volcanoes, not active ones, and no official source ties them to the water.
- **"The monkey park fee is fixed."** It changes from 1 December 2026.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
