Sukayu Onsen is not a town but a single inn, about 900 m up on the western foot of Ōdake, the main peak of the Hakkōda mountains, some 30 km south of central Aomori. Its best-known bath is the Sennin-buro, a large mixed bath built entirely of hiba wood. It was among the first places designated as a national health resort onsen, in 1954, and in winter it lies in what the ministry calls one of Japan’s heaviest snow areas. This page explains it using the environment ministry’s plan for Sukayu (revised in March 2025), the ministry’s list of designations, the Japan Meteorological Agency, Aomori prefecture, the Japan Hot Spring Association, JR Bus Tōhoku and the operator, Sukayu Onsen Co. The operator is a single business, so I mark what comes only from it. It does not rate the inn.

For what the spring names mean, see the page on [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) and the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). The [Honshu hot springs guide](/en/soak/onsen-honshu/) covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

### Three hundred years

The ministry’s plan calls Sukayu "a mountain hot spring inn opened 300 years ago". Its basic policy speaks of building on the character of the place as a self-catering *tōji* spring, where people, mainly farmers, stayed for days or weeks to bathe. Aomori prefecture’s tourism site also says about 300 years, and calls it a spring where the traditional cure of *tōji* took shape.

### The deer

Two accounts tell how the name came about.

- **Aomori prefecture’s tourism site** says deer were seen bathing in the water to heal their wounds, so it was called Shika-yu, "deer spring", and that the name later changed to Sukayu because the water is strongly acidic. It gives no source for this.
- **The operator** says that in 1684 a hunter from Yokouchi, now part of Aomori city, named Osanai Saemon-shirō, followed a wounded deer into the mountains, found the spring and named it Shika no Yu. It says that from the Edo period many *tōji* guests came, that hut owners built huts by the water near today’s Netsu no Yu and opened them to mountain-plant gatherers and hunters, and that their forming an association was the start of the present inn.

The 1684 date and the hunter’s name come only from the operator. No public record confirms them.

### The designation

The environment ministry’s list (as of November 2020) gives Sukayu’s designation as a national health resort onsen as **11 October 1954**. Shima Onsen and Okunikkō Yumoto Onsen were designated on the same day. The ministry says designations began in 1954.

The operator says Sukayu was designated in 1954 as "national health resort onsen No. 1". Aomori prefecture’s tourism site calls it the first national onsen. The Japan Hot Spring Association lists Sukayu among national health resort onsen, but the text of its page does not use the word "first". The ministry’s list does not rank the three. So I say Sukayu was one of three designated on the first day, which is also how this site treats [Shima](/en/soak/shima/).

## The water

### Seven sources in the ministry’s plan

The plan’s text gives the spring name as an **acidic, sulphur-bearing sodium sulphate spring (hydrogen sulphide type)**. Its table lists seven sources, all owned by Sukayu Onsen Co. It does not say when they were measured.

| Source | Temperature | Flow (litres a minute) | Spring type in the table | How it comes out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Netsu no Yu | 48.1°C | 110 | Acidic sulphur, hydrogen sulphide type | Natural flow |
| Hiya no Yu (large) | 64.8°C | 94 | Acidic sulphur | Natural flow |
| Hiya no Yu (small) | 69.7°C | 25 | Acidic sulphur | Natural flow |
| Shika no Yu | 67.6°C | 23.7 | Acidic sulphate-chloride | Natural flow |
| Shibu-rokubu no Yu | 56.7°C | 97 | Acidic sulphur, hydrogen sulphide type | Natural flow |
| Arakawa Onsen | 76.1°C | 38 | Acidic chloride-sulphate | Natural flow (used by Hakkōda Hotel) |
| Fukashi-yu | 88.0°C | 48 | — (not for bathing) | Drilled and pumped |

The plan gives no pH. It says temperature has been recorded every day, and water level or flow every month, since 1954.

### Five or seven

The operator says its "five sources" are all acidic and sulphur-bearing, and gives the water as an acidic sulphur spring (containing gypsum; acidic hydrogen sulphide spring), hypotonic. It does not explain how it counts five. The ministry’s seven include the Arakawa source used by Hakkōda Hotel and the Fukashi-yu, which is not for bathing. If you need a number, "seven, in the ministry’s plan" is the one with a source.

### What else the official sources say

Aomori prefecture’s tourism site gives 52.3°C and an acidic sulphur spring. The Japan Hot Spring Association gives a sulphur spring, at 925 m, with one inn holding 420 guests. So the plan, the prefecture and the operator all say "acidic", and the association’s "sulphur spring" reads as the broader class, though it does not explain. None of them gives a pH figure.

## The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency lists Hakkōda as a volcano under constant observation, made of at least 17 stratovolcanoes and lava domes in a northern group (about 400,000 years ago to the present) and a southern group (about 1.1 million to 300,000 years ago). It says vents are scattered around the south-western foot of Ōdake, the highest peak of the northern group, **from Sukayu to Jigokunuma**. A caldera about 9 km across lies under and north-east of the northern group.

The agency counts at least eight eruptions in the northern group in the last 6,000 years. Those since records began were all small steam explosions from Jigokunuma: one in the 13th to 14th centuries and two in the 15th to 17th. Among recorded events it lists:

- **1997.** Three members of the Self-Defence Forces died from carbon dioxide at Tashirotai, on the north-eastern foot.
- **June 2010.** A junior high school girl gathering mountain plants **near Sukayu** died from volcanic gas (hydrogen sulphide).

**Alert levels.** The Sendai regional weather office announced that eruption alert levels for Hakkōda would start at 15:00 on 30 July 2019, at level 1 if nothing changed. The assumed craters are Ōdake and Jigokunuma, and the agency publishes its criteria as of 22 January 2025. The current level could not be confirmed on 13 October 2026; see the Japan Meteorological Agency’s Hakkōda page before you go.

The ministry’s plan says Hakkōda was named a volcanic disaster alert area in 2016, with a hazard map. Its text still says alert levels "will be set"; I follow the agency’s 2019 start.

The Japan Hot Spring Association describes Jigokunuma as the trace of an explosion crater that still sends up steam. No official source says Sukayu’s water is heated by the volcano, so I give the vents and the springs side by side and do not join them.

## How the place works

### One inn, three buildings

Sukayu is a single business. The plan describes the inn (room for 500) and Hakkōda Hotel (room for 120, opened in May 1991, which the plan calls one of the largest Western-style wooden buildings in Japan). The operator says its *tōji* building No. 3 was renovated in 2016, with step-free access and rooms with toilets.

The plan’s figures for fiscal 2023 give 51,000 overnight guests and 69,000 day visitors at Sukayu, and 15,400 overnight guests at Hakkōda Hotel: 135,400 in all, up from 82,700 in fiscal 2021 and 122,900 in fiscal 2022.

### The Sennin-buro

The plan and the operator both describe the Sennin-buro, the "thousand-person bath", as a large bath built wholly of hiba wood.

- **The plan** gives about 80 *tsubo* (the Japan Hot Spring Association gives about 260 m²), with four baths, Netsu no Yu, Shibu-rokubu no Yu, Shika no Yu and Hiya no Yu, in one space with not a single pillar.
- **The operator** and Aomori prefecture give 160 tatami mats, and the operator lists Netsu no Yu, Hiya no Yu, Shibu-rokubu no Yu and a hot-water fall.

The two units do not contradict each other. No official source explains where the name comes from.

**Mixed bathing.** The plan states that the Sennin-buro is mixed. It says that manners have declined in recent years and some women find it hard to bathe; that screens and awareness campaigns were tried with limited effect; that since 2021 the ministry has held a "*yuami-gi* day", on which bathing wear is worn, to study the question; and that it remains an issue for the future.

### Day bathing

As announced by the operator, checked on 13 October 2026:

- **Until 30 November 2026.** The large bath 7:00 to 18:00 (last entry 17:30); Tama no Yu 9:00 to 17:00 (last entry 16:30).
- **From 1 December 2026.** 9:00 to 16:00 (last entry 15:30), with both baths included and new fees.
- **Women only** from 8:00 to 9:00.
- ***Yuami-gi*.** Rented for a fee to both men and women since May 2024. Wearing it is optional, and you may not bring your own.

I do not give the bathing fee, because the operator does not say whether it includes tax.

### Snow

The plan calls Hakkōda one of Japan’s heaviest snow areas, with mountain skiing among frozen trees and, in early spring, walls of snow over 5 m high. The operator says winter snow reaches over 5 m and that the inn stands on Route 103. A 2020 staff blog on Aomori prefecture’s tourism site says a record of 566 cm, the highest at any of the weather agency’s automatic stations, was set in February 2013. The agency’s own record is not confirmed here, so this is the prefecture site’s statement.

**The snow corridor.** Aomori prefecture announced in March 2026 that it was aiming to open Route 103, the Hakkōda-Towada Gold Line, at 9:00 on 1 April, after clearing snow on the 8.0 km between Sukayu and Yachi from late February, with a ceremony at Kasamatsu pass on 27 March. The operator posted on 1 April 2026 that the "snow corridor" had opened. "Snow corridor" is the operator’s and the prefecture blog’s term; the prefecture’s release says snow clearing. The date changes every year.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The "ENGLISH" link on the operator’s site goes only to a booking site, and there is no official English bathing guide.
- **The operator’s notes.** Its Japanese FAQ covers mixed bathing, the women-only hour, *yuami-gi* and the discolouring of metal.
- **Tattoos.** Neither the operator nor the ministry’s plan says anything on tattoos. Ask the inn in advance.
- **Crowds.** The plan says the inn is considering internet access, cashless payment and *yuami-gi* for the growing number of overseas visitors. Official sources give no entry limit.
- **Compared with city baths.** No official Sukayu source compares its water with baths in the cities. What the plan does show is that most of its sources flow naturally.

## What it is eaten with

No official page ties a regional dish to Sukayu. The farm ministry’s Aomori list includes *ke no shiru*, *senbei-jiru* and *ichigo-ni*, but does not mention Sukayu or Hakkōda; hiba appears in official sources only as the wood of the bathhouse.

What the operator says about itself: it has a soba restaurant, Kimen-an; its shop sells ginger-miso *oden*, which it calls an Aomori city street food; and meals come either as inn meals or as smaller, home-style *tōji* meals. That is the operator’s description only. For the region’s food, see the guide to [Tōhoku local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-tohoku/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, because none of the official pages says whether they include tax.

- **JR bus.** The operator gives about 1 hour 10 minutes from Aomori station to the Sukayu Onsen-mae stop, and about 1 hour 20 minutes from Shin-Aomori. The Japan Hot Spring Association gives 1 hour 10 minutes from Shin-Aomori.
- **The winter bus.** JR Bus Tōhoku runs the Hakkōda service between Aomori and Sukayu from **12 November 2026 to 31 March 2027**; in that period the service on to Oirase and Lake Towada does not run. Boarding at Aomori or Shin-Aomori towards Sukayu needs **a booking in advance**; the booked services leave Aomori station at 8:10, 9:20 and 10:50. From Sukayu towards Aomori no booking is needed, on a space-available basis. There is a booking charge on top of the fare, seats are not assigned, and the JR EAST PASS and JAPAN RAIL PASS are accepted, though some passes are not.
- **Shuttle for guests.** The operator runs a free shuttle for overnight guests only, by booking, from the east exit of Shin-Aomori in about 60 minutes. It announced in November 2025 that it had moved to Shin-Aomori.
- **Air and taxi.** The operator gives about an hour by car from Aomori airport, with no direct bus, and about 45 minutes by taxi from central Aomori.

Official sources do not give a time from Tokyo to Shin-Aomori.

**Winter roads.** The operator says the roads are not, as a rule, cleared of snow after 17:00, that a four-wheel-drive car or chains are needed, and that JR buses run every day of the year. Its FAQ, undated and probably from around 2024, says Routes 102 and 394 and prefectural road 40 close at night from mid-December to mid-March, and that Route 102 at Kasamatsu pass is closed all winter. Aomori prefecture’s road department lists winter closures only up to fiscal 2025, so the closure dates for the 2026–27 winter are not confirmed.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sukayu is just a sulphur spring."** The ministry’s plan names it an acidic, sulphur-bearing sodium sulphate spring. The Japan Hot Spring Association’s "sulphur spring" is the broader class.
- **"Sukayu’s pH is…"** No official source gives a pH figure.
- **"It was Japan’s single first national health resort onsen."** The ministry’s list gives three, including Sukayu, on 11 October 1954.
- **"The spring was found in 1684."** That date, and the hunter, come only from the operator.
- **"The volcano heats the water."** The weather agency places vents between Sukayu and Jigokunuma, but no official source ties the springs to them.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
