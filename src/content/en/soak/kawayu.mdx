Kawayu Onsen is in the town of Teshikaga, in eastern Hokkaido, just north of Iōzan, the "sulphur mountain" whose Ainu name is Atosanupuri. The local tourism association describes its water as strongly acidic and rare in Japan, and Hokkaido’s tourism organisation puts its pH at 1.6 to 1.8. The mountain still steams, and in the 19th century it was mined for sulphur on a scale that brought a railway to the area. This page sets out what the association, the Japan Meteorological Agency, the tourism agency, Hokkaido’s tourism organisation and JNTO say, keeping the association’s account of the water apart from the agency’s account of the volcano. It does not rate any inn or bath.

For how Hokkaido’s onsen towns compare, see the [Hokkaido overview](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/). For how an acidic, sulphur-bearing spring is named and what the official rules say about it, see [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/), and for the names in general, the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### The mountain

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Atosanupuri as a caldera about 4 km across, the Atosanupuri caldera, formed about 35,000 to 25,000 years ago in the middle of the Kussharo caldera, which measures 26 km east to west and 20 km north to south. It says:

- Atosanupuri is also called Iōzan, "sulphur mountain".
- Many sulphur deposits, mainly of sublimated sulphur, formed on its lava domes, and they were mined until 1963.
- Fumarolic activity (steam and gas venting) continues today.
- The latest eruption was a phreatic (steam-driven) explosion a few hundred years ago, which left the Kumaotoshi explosion crater.

The agency keeps Atosanupuri under constant observation, and its Sapporo office lists it among the nine volcanoes in Hokkaido it watches around the clock. The agency defines an active volcano as one that has erupted within roughly the past 10,000 years or that has active fumaroles now; that definition was set again in 2003 by the national committee on volcanic eruption prediction, and Japan has 111.

The association calls Iōzan a "rank C" active volcano. I could not find a rank on the agency’s page, so that is the association’s statement.

### The mountain and the water

The link between the mountain and the springs comes from the tourism side, not the weather agency, whose page does not mention them.

- The association calls Iōzan the source of the famous Kawayu Onsen. Elsewhere it says the water is heated by magma under Iōzan, in the middle of the Kussharo caldera, flows through the rock underground and comes up where the town stands, and it calls Kawayu a famous spring born from Japan’s largest caldera.
- The tourism agency’s text, written in fiscal 2019, says the town lies directly north of Iōzan, an active volcano, and that the spring water flows underground to the baths.

### Sulphur and the railway

The association tells the mining story:

- **1877.** Sano Magoemon, a fishery owner from Kushiro, began serious sulphur mining on Iōzan with government permission and some thirty-odd miners.
- **1883.** The mine’s output was the largest in Hokkaido.
- **1887.** The mining rights passed to Yasuda Zenjirō, who used convicts from the Kushiro prison to lay a railway to Shibecha in eight months. The association calls it the second-oldest railway in Hokkaido and the foundation of today’s JR Senmō line.
- **1896.** Moving so much sulphur exhausted the deposits; the railway lasted only nine years, and mining stopped.

The weather agency, as above, says sulphur was mined on the lava domes until 1963. So the sources end the mining at different times: the association has Yasuda stopping in 1896, the agency has mining until 1963. They may be talking about different deposits or different operators on the same mountain, but I found no explanation, so I give both.

The tourism agency’s text says sulphur mining on Iōzan was the major industry that made Kawayu known, that it declined early in the 20th century, and that the hot springs kept the area prosperous. The same text says Mashu Onsen, a different district of the town, opened in 1885 and is the oldest in eastern Hokkaido.

**When Kawayu itself opened**, I could not find. The association says only that the town has more than 100 years of history.

### Ainu names

The association says *Atosanupuri* means "bare mountain" in Ainu, and that Kussharo comes from the Ainu *kutcharo*, "throat", meaning the outlet of a lake or marsh. I could not find an official account of how the Ainu used the springs at Kawayu. The association lists the Kussharo Kotan Ainu Folk Museum, which closes in winter.

## The water

**The name.** The Lake Mashu tourism association names it an acidic, sulphur-bearing, iron(II)-sodium-sulphate-chloride spring (hydrogen sulphide type), and calls it strongly acidic. It also says it is one of the few volcanic spring types in Japan. Its page on the town of Teshikaga describes strongly acidic water, rare in Japan, as Kawayu’s feature.

**The pH.** The association gives no number. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation gives pH 1.6 to 1.8, without a year of measurement or a source, on a page with no update date. The tourism agency’s text says the water contains sulphur and is acidic, and gives no number either. I give the figure as the tourism organisation’s.

What that long name means, piece by piece, and what the official rules say about acidic and sulphur springs, is in the [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) guide. I found no caution specific to Kawayu’s water on the official pages I read, so I do not add one here; the analysis posted at each bath lists its own.

**Not the same as Sunayu.** On Lake Kussharo the association names the water at Sunayu a sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring, a different type. The tourism agency also sets the two areas side by side, describing Kawayu’s water as sulphur-bearing and acidic and Mashu’s as soft and alkaline.

## How the town works

### The footbath

The association lists a footbath in Kawayu Park, where steam rises off the river. It is free and has no closing days; no hours are given. Its rules, as given: wipe your feet with a towel or similar before bathing, bring your own towel, and do not use soap. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation calls it a *gensen kakenagashi* footbath; what that term means, and what it does not, is in [kakenagashi explained](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

### Iōzan up close

The association’s Iōzan page promises "the fumaroles you can get closest to in Japan". It gives:

- **The mountain:** 508 m, a lava dome.
- **Protection:** the fumaroles and sulphur crystals are among Teshikaga’s designated natural tourism resources, and most of the mountain is an area with restricted entry under the Ecotourism Promotion Act. You can climb only on a trekking tour with a certified guide.
- **Parking:** 500 yen for a car, which the page marks as including consumption tax. It lists separate rates for motorbikes and buses. The ticket is shared with the car park at the first Lake Mashu observation deck. From November to April the car park is closed for business and open free of charge.
- **Iōzan MOKMOK Base** reopened after refurbishment in 2023.

**Tsutsujigahara nature trail.** About 2.5 km, roughly an hour on foot, from the town to Iōzan, through about 100 ha of marsh Labrador tea (*Ezo-iso-tsutsuji*), which flowers from early June to early July.

### Sunayu, the beach on Lake Kussharo

The association says that at Sunayu you dig into the sand and you have an open-air bath. Because of the geothermal heat, this spot does not freeze even in the coldest months, and whooper swans from Siberia rest here. It is free and open 24 hours with no closing days; the contact is the tourism and commerce section of Teshikaga town office. The tourism agency’s text says the same of the Sunayu campsite: dig, and naturally heated water fills the hole. Remember that the water here is the sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring, not Kawayu’s.

### The town itself

The association posted a call for people to attend a briefing, held on 1 September, about redeveloping the Kawayu onsen town. The notice does not state the year; it looks like 2026, but I have not confirmed it.

## Bathing there

**Day bathing.** The association’s day-bathing list gives prices for Kawayu and the Kussharo area, but the word "tax" does not appear with them, so I give no figures.

**Tattoos and manners.** I found nothing on tattoos or bathing manners on the association’s Japanese or English pages, its day-bathing list or its footbath page, beyond the footbath rules above, or on Hokkaido’s tourism organisation’s page. Ask each facility before you go; the general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

**Compared with city baths.** I found no official Kawayu source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What the sources do say is that the water is strongly acidic.

## Getting there

Checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

**By train.** The nearest station is JR Kawayu Onsen on the Senmō line, which JNTO describes as running between Abashiri and Kushiro. Buses run between the station and the town; Hokkaido’s tourism organisation gives about 10 minutes. JNTO gives about 45 minutes on the Senmō line from Shiretoko-Shari station to Kawayu Onsen.

**From the airports.** The association says Kushiro, Memanbetsu and Nakashibetsu airports are each a little over an hour by car, as a rough guide for the town of Teshikaga as a whole.

**By car.** Hokkaido’s tourism organisation gives about 70 minutes from Memanbetsu Airport and about 110 minutes from Akan interchange on the Dōtō expressway. JNTO gives about 70 minutes from Memanbetsu Airport by Routes 334 and 391, and about 90 minutes from Kushiro Airport by Routes 53 and 391. JNTO’s 90 minutes from Kushiro is longer than the association’s "a little over an hour", which is a guide for the whole town; I give both.

**In winter.** The association says only that limited-period tour buses link the sights of eastern Hokkaido in winter. I could not find a winter timetable for Kawayu. JNTO says seasonal buses run from Akanko Onsen to Lake Mashu and Lake Kussharo in summer and winter.

For the town to the south-west, see [Akanko Onsen](/en/soak/akanko/). For the other towns in this series, see [Jozankei Onsen](/en/soak/jozankei/), [Toyako Onsen](/en/soak/toyako/), [Sounkyo Onsen](/en/soak/sounkyo/) and [Noboribetsu Onsen](/en/soak/noboribetsu/).

## What people get wrong

- **"The weather agency says the springs come from Iōzan."** Its page on Atosanupuri does not mention them. The link is made by the tourism association and the tourism agency’s text.
- **"Kawayu’s pH is an official figure."** The 1.6 to 1.8 is Hokkaido’s tourism organisation’s, with no year or source. The association says only "strongly acidic".
- **"Sunayu is Kawayu water."** The association names Sunayu’s water a sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring.
- **"Sulphur mining ended in 1896"** (or 1963). The association has the Yasuda mine stopping in 1896; the weather agency says sulphur was mined until 1963. I found no explanation of the difference.
- **"Iōzan is rank C."** That is the association’s statement; I could not find it on the agency’s page.
- **"You can walk up Iōzan."** Most of it is restricted; climbing is only on a guided tour.
- **"The water treats a condition."** This page makes no health claims.
