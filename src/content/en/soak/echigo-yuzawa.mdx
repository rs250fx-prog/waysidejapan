Echigo-Yuzawa Onsen is the spa town around Echigo-Yuzawa station in the town of Yuzawa, Niigata, on the Jōetsu shinkansen. It is where Kawabata Yasunari wrote *Snow Country*, its five shared baths each have their own water, and inside the station there is a bath with sake in it. This page explains how it works using Yuzawa town, the town’s tourism organisation (Yuzawa-machi Kankō Machizukuri Kikō, formerly the tourism association), the town’s shared-bath site, Niigata prefecture, the Ponshukan tasting room and the national geological survey. Where sources disagree, each is given with its source. It does not rate any inn or bath.

For what the spring names mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for the notices about added water and heating, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). The [Honshu hot springs guide](/en/soak/onsen-honshu/) covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

### How old it is

Official sources give no date for when Yuzawa’s hot water was first used. The nearest is a story Niigata prefecture tells about the name of one inn, Yukiguni no Yado Takahan. More than 900 years ago, the prefecture says, Takahashi Hanroku, a country samurai from Shibata in Echigo, fell ill on his way to the Kantō, stopped here, and while trying to catch vipers and red frogs in a valley stream to make medicine, came by chance on hot water flowing naturally from the ground. That is the prefecture introducing an inn’s name, not the prefecture dating the spa as a whole, and this page gives it only as that.

Among the shared baths, the tourism organisation calls **Yama no Yu** the oldest shared bath in Echigo-Yuzawa, and Yuzawa town’s page on it (updated 22 June 2026) calls it the shared bath with the most history.

### Snow Country

The two official accounts agree on the main points and add different details.

- **Niigata prefecture** says *Snow Country* was written at Takahan from 1934 to 1937. A sequel followed in 1947; the film of 1957 was set at Takahan; the English translation, *Snow Country*, came out in 1957; and Kawabata received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1968. The room he stayed and wrote in, Kasumi no Ma, was moved and kept as it was when the inn was rebuilt in reinforced concrete. Whether you can see it depends on the inn, so this page does not say.
- **The tourism organisation’s museum newsletter** (8 July 2026) says Kawabata first came to Yuzawa in June 1934 and wrote while staying at the Takahan ryokan. The Shimizu tunnel had opened in September 1931, completing the Jōetsu line and linking Tokyo to Echigo-Yuzawa by rail, about 4 hours 40 minutes direct to Ueno. The book came out from Sōgensha on 12 June 1937. In the afterword to the Sōgensha edition of 1948 he did not name the place; in the afterword to the Iwanami edition of 1952 he wrote that the place in *Snow Country* is Yuzawa Onsen in Echigo. In an essay of 1959 he wrote that the place was Yuzawa Onsen and no more, and also that he had not wanted to give a place name.

**When the inn was rebuilt** depends on whom you read. The newsletter says it was converted in 1987 from a three-storey wooden building to a hotel style; the prefecture gives no year.

**The characters.** The prefecture says the geisha Matsuei is "said to be" the model for Komako, and that Shimamura is "said to be" Kawabata himself. Both are given as hearsay.

**The baths.** The tourism organisation and the prefecture say the shared bath **Komako no Yu** is named after Komako in the novel. The prefecture says Kawabata is said to have used **Yama no Yu**, and that the slope up to it is the one the novel calls Yuzaka.

**The museum.** Yukiguni-kan, the town’s history and folk museum, shows a first edition of *Snow Country* and things Kawabata owned, among them calligraphy in his own hand. Its admission is not given here, because it does not say whether it includes tax.

## The water

### Different water at each bath

Yuzawa town has several sources, and the spring name differs from bath to bath. The tourism organisation and the shared-bath site give:

- **Yama no Yu:** simple sulphur spring (hypotonic, alkaline, high-temperature).
- **Komako no Yu:** sodium-calcium chloride spring.
- **Egami Onsen bath:** simple spring (hypotonic, weakly alkaline).
- **The bath inside the station:** weakly alkaline, hypotonic, high-temperature.

The town and the prefecture do not give the number of sources, their total flow, their temperatures or their pH. A count of sources appears in some search summaries, but the page it came from could not be opened, so this page does not use it.

### What the prefecture says

A Niigata prefecture paper on its main spa areas, drawing on the national geological survey’s geothermal potential map of 2009, names Senami, Muramatsuhama, **Yuzawa**, Matsunoyama, Itoigawa and Ikenotaira as springs that show especially high temperatures. It counts 17 of 205 springs in the prefecture above 60°C, and says its list does not cover every spring. These are figures from 2009 data.

### Where the heat comes from

Official sources do not explain where Yuzawa’s water gets its heat, do not say the area is not volcanic, as some describe it, and do not link Naeba-san to Yuzawa’s water. So this page does not call the area non-volcanic.

The geological survey’s list of Quaternary volcanoes includes Naeba-san, on the Nagano–Niigata border. Quaternary means active within roughly the last 2.6 million years; it does not mean active today. As checked for this page, the weather agency’s list of active volcanoes shows none near Yuzawa.

Yuzawa is not on the environment ministry’s list of *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the designated health resort onsen areas. Niigata’s entries on that list are Iwamuro, Muikamachi, Seki and Tsubame, and Gozu.

## How the town works

### The five shared baths

The spa town spreads around Echigo-Yuzawa station. The town’s shared-bath site lists five baths for its *sotoyu meguri*, the round of outside baths: **Yama no Yu, Komako no Yu, Iwa no Yu, Kaidō no Yu and Shukuba no Yu**. As checked on 13 October 2026:

- **Closing days.** Each bath has its own: Yama no Yu on Tuesdays and Komako no Yu on Thursdays, for example. When that falls on a public holiday, or over the New Year, the day moves.
- **Closures.** The site’s front page carries notices of temporary closures for repairs. One of them does not show its year, so check the site before you go.
- **Who uses them.** Residents of Yuzawa, and people who own a flat or other property in the town, get a free membership card. Others from outside the town can buy a paid card valid for five years. Visitors simply pay the ordinary fee.
- **Fees.** The town and the shared-bath site give each fee as including the bathing tax, but neither says whether consumption tax is included, so no fees are given here, and none for the round ticket or books of tickets either.

### The footbath

**Ashiyasume Kannakkuri** is free and open from 9:00 to 21:00, the tourism organisation says, but cannot be used in winter, roughly from early December to early April. In a town this snowy, the footbath closes for the season.

### The sake bath in the station

The tourism organisation lists a bath inside Echigo-Yuzawa station, in Ponshukan. Ponshukan’s own site says its sake bath is natural hot-spring water with sake made for bathing added to it. The page gives its price as including the bathing tax, and does not say whether consumption tax is included, so this page does not give it.

### Skiing and snow

The tourism organisation puts skiing and playing in the snow among the town’s main attractions. Winter snow and the ski season shape how the spa runs, as the closed footbath shows. Official sources give no figure for the number of skiers or ski areas.

## Bathing there

The town, the tourism organisation, the shared-bath site and the prefecture give nothing on tattoos, and no official Yuzawa source compares its water with baths in the cities.

- **Language.** The tourism organisation has English and other language menus; its English pages have not been checked here.
- **Manners.** The shared-bath site has a Japanese page on manners. Take everything off and do not bathe in a swimsuit. Pour water over yourself first, starting from the feet. Do not thin the water with cold water without need. Say a word to the people already in the bath. Do not bring valuables.
- **Tattoos.** The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn or bath in advance.
- **Compared with city baths.** What the town’s own pages do show is that even its five shared baths do not share one water.

## What it is eaten with

**Sake at Ponshukan.** Niigata prefecture’s page on Ponshukan (updated 16 January 2026) says Niigata has the most sake breweries of any prefecture and is a sake region, and recommends the sake of the local Minamiuonuma breweries, Shirataki Shuzō, Aoki Shuzō, Takachiyo Shuzō and Hakkai Jōzō. The prefecture’s page gives an older tasting price than Ponshukan’s own site; for how the tasting room works and what it costs now, see [Niigata sake](/en/eat/sake-niigata/). The one place where the official pages join the spa and the sake is the sake bath in the same building.

**What is not linked.** The farm ministry’s Niigata list includes *kirizai*, natto mixed with chopped vegetables, from the Uonuma area, and *hegi soba*, said to come from Uonuma. Neither of its pages mentions Yuzawa, and Yuzawa’s official pages do not mention hegi soba either; the guide to [Hokuriku local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokuriku/) came to the same conclusion. No official page pairs Uonuma Koshihikari rice with the spa.

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **From Tokyo.** The tourism organisation gives 66 minutes at the fastest on the Jōetsu shinkansen. Niigata’s tourism site says about 1 hour 20 minutes from Tokyo into Niigata prefecture, counted as 50 minutes to Takasaki and 30 more to Echigo-Yuzawa. The two count differently, and do not contradict each other.
- **From Niigata.** 37 minutes at the fastest, the organisation says.
- **From Kanazawa.** About 2 hours on the Hokuriku shinkansen to Takasaki, then about 30 minutes to Echigo-Yuzawa, about 2 hours 30 minutes in all.
- **From Osaka.** About 3 hours 40 minutes from Shin-Osaka.
- **Other lines.** About 1 hour 50 minutes from Naoetsu on the Hokuhoku line.
- **By car.** About 1 hour 50 minutes from Tokyo (167 km), and about 1 hour 30 minutes from Niigata (131 km).
- **Winter roads.** The organisation’s access page links to road information from the transport ministry and others but gives no dates for winter closures. Those linked pages have not been checked here.

## What people get wrong

- **"The water is the same all over town."** The town’s own pages give a simple sulphur spring at Yama no Yu and a chloride spring at Komako no Yu.
- **"Yuzawa is 900 years old."** That figure comes from the prefecture’s story of one inn’s name, not from a date for the spa.
- **"Komako was a real geisha" or "Shimamura is Kawabata."** The prefecture says each is "said to be" so.
- **"Kawabata named the place in the book."** He did not in the afterword to the 1948 edition; he named Yuzawa Onsen in the afterword to the 1952 edition.
- **"The footbath is open all year."** It closes for winter, roughly early December to early April.
- **"Yuzawa’s water is not volcanic."** No official source says either way.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
