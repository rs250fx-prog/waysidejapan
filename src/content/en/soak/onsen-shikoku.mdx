On the environment ministry's figures, all four Shikoku prefectures have fewer onsen areas than the national average. It also has no volcano on the weather agency's list of active volcanoes. Yet it has Dōgo, which counts itself among Japan's three ancient springs, and a set of cold springs that count as onsen under the law. This page sets out what the figures say, what the official sources can and cannot say about where the water comes from, and how the towns tell their own history. The individual town guides go deeper; for how to read a spring's name and the sign on the wall, start from the onsen overview.

## Where the hot springs are

The environment ministry publishes a yearly table of hot spring use by prefecture. The latest, for fiscal 2024 (as of the end of March 2025), gives the four Shikoku prefectures these figures. The ministry counts a place as an onsen area when it has somewhere to stay, and marks the overnight-stay figure as a reference value.

- **Tokushima** — 25 onsen areas, 82 springs, 29 places to stay, 453,168 overnight stays, 39 public baths using hot spring water.
- **Kagawa** — 28 onsen areas, 200 springs, 55 places to stay, 1,253,690 stays, 64 public baths.
- **Ehime** — 37 onsen areas, 192 springs, 97 places to stay, 1,553,354 stays, 115 public baths.
- **Kochi** — 37 onsen areas, 96 springs, 73 places to stay, 528,915 stays, 47 public baths.

The table has no ranking column, and the ministry does not publish a Shikoku total. Worked out from its figures, the four prefectures together have 127 onsen areas (about 4.5% of Japan's 2,839), 570 springs (about 2.0% of 27,899) and 3,789,127 overnight stays (about 3.0%). Four prefectures are about 8.5% of Japan's 47, so on all three measures Shikoku's share is smaller than its share of prefectures. The national average is about 60 onsen areas per prefecture; each Shikoku prefecture has 25 to 37. The table does not say why, and this page has not compared by area.

One detail is worth noticing. Kagawa has the most springs in Shikoku, 200, but the table shows 117 of them not in use against 83 in use.

## Why here

The short answer is that the official sources do not say, for any individual Shikoku spring.

**No active volcano.** The Japan Meteorological Agency defines an active volcano as one that has erupted within roughly the last 10,000 years or shows active steaming now, and counts 111. Its list groups them by region, and there is no Shikoku group; on an older version of the list, no volcano carries the name of Tokushima, Kagawa, Ehime or Kochi. That is a statement about the active list. Older volcanoes have not been checked here, so this page does not say Shikoku has none.

**How water gets hot without a volcano.** The environment ministry's guidelines on protecting hot spring resources say that in volcanic areas the heat comes from magma, while the heat for deep hot springs in non-volcanic areas is "thought to be broadly" the heat of the earth's crust. The national geological survey (part of the government research institute AIST) writes that Japan has many non-volcanic springs whose heat and water sources are mostly not well understood. One idea it describes is that water carried down by a sinking ocean plate is released at shallow depths without forming magma and rises as hot water, a type named after Arima Onsen. A 2025 survey report adds that such deep fluids rise through faults and cracks in the rock. Neither source mentions Shikoku.

**The Median Tectonic Line.** The geological survey describes the Median Tectonic Line as a fault that divides the geology of south-west Japan, running especially from western Kanto to Shikoku. Its 2021 geological map of the Ikeda area in Tokushima calls it "the great fault that splits the geology of south-west Japan in two". Neither source says anything about hot springs.

So this page does not say that Shikoku's springs rise along the Median Tectonic Line, or that they come from deep fluids. Those may be things people say, but official sources do not apply them to Dōgo, Iya, Shionoe or Yunoura, and no prefecture explains where Shikoku's water comes from.

## The towns

These are sketches, not guides. Each town's own page is the place to go further. Official sources give no count of which spring types are most common in Shikoku.

**Kagawa's cold springs.** Kagawa's prefectural environment and health research centre reported 199 springs in the prefecture as of 31 March 2023, of which 87 were in use as hot springs. It says springs that qualify as onsen through radon and similar components (without reaching the weakly radioactive or therapeutic standard) make up about 40% of all the prefecture's springs. Of the 32 springs it analysed on request between fiscal 2014 and 2023, 31 were cold (below 25°C), 29 were hypotonic, and 27 had hydrogen carbonate as the main negative ion; only six were salt springs, and most held less than 1 g of dissolved matter per kilogram. Those 32 are the springs someone asked to have tested, not every spring in the prefecture. They are a good illustration of a point that surprises visitors: under the law, water below 25°C is still an onsen if its minerals meet the standard.

**Shionoe** (Kagawa). The ministry's plan for the Shionoe onsen district lists six springs, four in use, at 15.6 to 20.1°C, with spring names including a sodium hydrogen carbonate cold spring and an alkaline simple sulphur cold spring. It does not name a single representative water. The plan says the springs are "said" to have been found by the monk Gyōki about 1,300 years ago, early in the Nara period, and that Kōbō Daishi is "said" to have trained here and urged people to bathe. The Kagawa tourism association calls Shionoe the oldest onsen district in the prefecture and "the inner parlour of Takamatsu".

**Yunoura** (Imabari, Ehime). The ministry's plan for Yunoura calls it Shikoku's first national health resort onsen and says it has long been known as a place to take the waters. Its three springs run at 19.4 to 20.4°C. The plan gives the first as "a spring containing radon and fluorine" and the other two as simple weakly radioactive cold springs, a category defined by the amount of radon. ("Radium spring" and "radon spring" are not official names.)

**Dōgo** (Matsuyama, Ehime). The official site gives the water as *alkaline simple spring* (the current official name is alkaline simple onsen), from 18 sources at 20 to 55°C. It says the sources are blended to about 42°C and that the water is neither heated nor diluted, which it calls *gensen kakenagashi*. It does not give the pH, composition or flow. Matsuyama city's timeline says chlorine disinfection began at the main bathhouse in 2003, the year it went fully non-smoking; disinfection, like dilution or heating, is something a public bath must post with its reason.

Dōgo's history is told several ways, and this page does not choose between them.

- **Matsuyama city's timeline** begins with Prince Shōtoku bathing in 596, then visits by Emperor Jomei in 639 and Empress Saimei in 661, and the poet Yamabe no Akahito in 713. The city's page on Prince Shōtoku cites a surviving fragment of the *Iyo Fudoki* and says he set up a stone monument, which has never been found.
- **The city's 2015 plan** says visits by several emperors and Prince Shōtoku are recorded in the *Nihon Shoki* and the *Iyo Fudoki* fragments, without saying which visit is in which.
- **Legends.** The official site and the city tell a legend of a white heron, recorded in a collection said to have been completed in 1710, and a story from the *Iyo Fudoki* fragments of the gods Ōkuninushi and Sukunahikona. The "Tama-no-ishi" stone linked to Sukunahikona's recovery is enshrined north of the main bathhouse.
- **The Man'yōshū.** The official site says Dōgo is one of only five hot springs in the eighth-century poetry anthology, and that Akahito's long poem of 713 is carved on the spout in the men's Kami-no-Yu bath. A poem from Saimei's stay of 661 is "also said" to be by the poet Nukata no Ōkimi.
- **Later.** The official site says the monk Ippen is said to have written the six-character Buddhist invocation on the bath's stone spout in 1288 (it is now kept in Dōgo Park), and that from 1636 the new lord of Matsuyama divided the baths by rank and sex. The present main bathhouse was finished in 1894 and became an Important Cultural Property in 1994.
- **"3,000 years" and "the oldest".** The official site says the main bathhouse is "said to have 3,000 years of history"; Ehime's tourism site says it "is said to be" Japan's oldest; JNTO's headline calls it the oldest hot spring in Japan. The earliest date on the city's timeline is 596. None of these pages gives the document or excavation behind 3,000 years.
- **"Three ancient springs".** The official site and the city's plan count Dōgo, with Arima and Shirahama, among Japan's three ancient springs. Neither says who chose the three or when, and no government body is confirmed as having done so. It is the town's description, not an official ranking.

**Iya and Ōboke** (Miyoshi, Tokushima). Miyoshi city's tourism site lists the inns of the Ōboke–Iya onsen district, and each gives its water differently: alkaline simple sulphur spring, simple sulphur spring, hydrogen carbonate spring, and one inn that gives no spring name and says "strong alkaline spring". At least one inn's label differs between two places on the same page. These are the inns' own labels.

**Kochi.** The prefecture's tourism site says many facilities advertise "skin-beautifying" water and that alkaline and hydrogen carbonate springs are common, with some pumped from deeper than 1,000 m and others red-brown with iron, cloudy, carbonated or salty. It notes on the same page that the basis for each spring's name varies by facility and some do not state one. That is a tourism summary, not a prefectural count.

**Ehime's three springs.** Ehime's tourism site groups Dōgo, Hondani and Nibukawa as the "three springs of Iyo", describing Hondani as clear and smooth and Nibukawa as alkaline and rich in radon. It gives no official spring names.

**National health resort onsen.** The environment ministry designates some onsen areas as *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, places it considers suitable for health and recuperation. Its list shows two in Shikoku: Shionoe in Kagawa and Yunoura in Ehime. The dates on the list are when each plan was revised, not when the area was designated, and official sources do not give the designation years. No Tokushima or Kochi area is on the list.

## How people use them

**On the pilgrimage.** Ishiteji, temple 51 of the Shikoku pilgrimage, is close to Dōgo. Ehime's tourism site puts it about 1 km from the onsen and suggests bathing in Dōgo after visiting; JNTO gives 15 to 20 minutes on foot from the Dōgo Onsen tram stop, or a short bus ride. The tourism site also says the temple holds soil from all 88 temples, and that touching it is held to bring the same merit as the whole pilgrimage. Shionoe's story ties it to Kōbō Daishi, and Kochi's tourism site notes a Kōbō Daishi legend at an inn in Ashizuri. No official source covers the pilgrimage and hot springs as a whole.

**As a neighbourhood bath.** Dōgo's official site says the ground-floor bath of the main building is "loved by locals too" and can be used "like a sentō". The main bathhouse lists ¥700 for adults and ¥350 for children aged 2 to 11 for that bath (60 minutes), and ¥2,000 for the second-floor seat of the Tama-no-Yu, all tax included, as checked on 10 October 2026. The site also sells a ticket for its three public bathhouses; whether that price includes tax is not confirmed, so it is not here.

**Next to city spas.** No Shikoku source compares the water in an onsen town with a day spa in a city. Two facts sit side by side. The ministry's plan for Yunoura notes that several private hot spring businesses have opened in central Imabari in recent years while Yunoura's visitor numbers have stayed roughly flat. Kochi's tourism site lists hot spring facilities inside Kochi city, one calling itself the city's first natural hot spring. What applies everywhere is the national rule that a bath open to the public must post its water's analysis and say whether, and why, the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected.

**With food.** These are cases where an official source names the town or its district and the food. None says the hot spring made the dish.

- **Iya soba.** The farm ministry says buckwheat growing in Iya is "said" to have begun with the Heike, who fled here after losing the Genpei war; rice grew poorly, so buckwheat became the staple. Made with buckwheat flour alone, the noodles break easily and are thick and short. Miyoshi city's tourism site lists both the Iya onsen inns and soba shops in the area. The ministry does not mention the springs.
- **Shionoe.** The Kagawa tourism association names dishes of *amego*, a river fish it calls "the queen of clear streams", and wild boar as the district's specialities. Neither is on the farm ministry's list for Kagawa.
- **Imabari.** Yunoura's plan says a day-bathing facility came up with "onsen shiitake", mushrooms grown on logs with hot spring water, and holds a festival for them every year. The farm ministry lists Imabari *yakitori*, chicken skin pressed flat on a hot plate, and in the Tōyo area including Imabari a *tai-meshi* of whole sea bream cooked with the rice. Neither ministry entry mentions Yunoura.
- **Matsuyama.** The farm ministry's *Matsuyama-zushi* is a scattered sushi with conger eel, and its entry tells of the novelist Natsume Sōseki being served it at the home of the poet Masaoka Shiki. It does not mention Dōgo. JNTO's Dōgo page suggests walking the streets in a *yukata* after bathing, and mentions Dōgo beer and mandarin juice.

## Getting there

These are the times the official pages give, checked on 10 October 2026. The pages show no update date and timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page.

- **Dōgo** — The official site gives about 43 minutes from Matsuyama Airport by limousine bus to Dōgo Onsen station (about 30 by taxi), and about 25 minutes from JR Matsuyama station by Iyo Railway tram. JNTO gives about 30 minutes by tram from JR Matsuyama station, and Ehime's tourism site about 20 minutes by tram and about 40 by airport bus. Allow roughly 20 to 30 minutes by tram and 40 to 45 by bus. From the tram stop to the main bathhouse is about 5 minutes on foot through the arcade, says JNTO.
- **Shionoe** — The ministry's plan puts it at the southern tip of Takamatsu city, about 25 km and 40 minutes by car south of the centre, and about 10 minutes by car from Takamatsu Airport.
- **Yunoura** — The ministry's plan gives 3 minutes by car from the Imabari-Yunoura interchange and 20 minutes from the Imabari interchange of the Shimanami Kaidō, with a roadside station at the entrance to the town.
- **Iya and Ōboke** — Miyoshi city's tourism site gives about 1 hour by JR from Takamatsu to Awa-Ikeda, about 1 hour 10 minutes from Tokushima, about 1 hour from Kochi to Ōboke, and about 20 minutes by limited express from Awa-Ikeda to Ōboke. From the region's four airports, it says, Awa-Ikeda and Ōboke are about 1 hour 20 minutes to 2 hours 10 minutes by train, bus or car.

Official sources give no public transport time for Shionoe or Yunoura.

## What people get wrong

- **"Shikoku has no volcanoes."** It has none on the weather agency's active list. Older volcanoes have not been checked here.
- **"Shikoku's springs come up along the Median Tectonic Line."** The fault runs through Shikoku, but no official source connects it to any Shikoku spring.
- **"An onsen has to be hot."** Many of Shikoku's springs, including those at Shionoe and Yunoura, are below 25°C and qualify through their minerals.
- **"Dōgo is 3,000 years old."** That is how the town describes itself. The earliest dated record on the city's timeline is 596.
- **"The three ancient springs are an official list."** Official sources do not say who chose them.
- **"The water will treat a condition."** The ministry's rules list indications by spring type and say treatment at a spa is best done with a doctor's guidance. This page does not claim any town's water cures anything, and the lists of conditions some inns print are their own.
