The last four of Japan's ten official spring types: the hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs. Then two words you will meet on signs that are not types at all, "moor" water and water with oil in it. Each type has a trap. The first carries a nickname the ministry only reports, the second is easily confused with sulphur, the third has two thresholds, and the fourth is often misread as "weak". The ten types as a whole, and how to read the analysis sign, are in [the guide to onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); the [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/) and the [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/) have their own pages. This page does not rank any spring and does not say what any water will do for you.

## What it is

### Two tiers, and where the numbers differ

The Hot Springs Act decides what counts as an onsen at all. Inside that, the ministry's *Guidelines for Mineral Spring Analysis* (revised 2014) define the therapeutic spring (*ryōyōsen*), and only a therapeutic spring gets one of the ten type names. Per kilogram of water:

| Component | Onsen under the Act | Therapeutic spring (the type) |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved substances (excluding gases) | 1,000 mg in total | 1,000 mg or more — salt springs, including hydrogen-carbonate and sulphate; under 1,000 mg — simple spring |
| Iodide ion (I⁻) | 1 mg | **10 mg** — iodine spring |
| Temperature at the source | 25°C | 25°C |

Only the iodine line differs. Water with 1 mg of iodide per kilogram can qualify as a legal onsen; the iodine spring starts at 10 mg.

### Hydrogen-carbonate spring

A hydrogen-carbonate spring (*tansan-suisoen-sen*) is a salt spring whose main negative ion, by milliequivalents (mval), is hydrogen carbonate (HCO₃⁻). By main positive ion it splits into sodium-, calcium- and magnesium-hydrogen carbonate springs. Even where carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻) is the larger share, the guidelines say not to call it a "carbonate spring".

The old names survive in conversation. From the ministry's table of old and new names:

- *Jūsō-sen* (sodium bicarbonate spring) is today's **sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring**.
- *Jūtansan-dorui-sen* (bicarbonate-of-earths spring) is today's **calcium (·magnesium)-hydrogen carbonate spring**.
- Compounds such as "common salt-containing *jūsō* spring" became "sodium-hydrogen carbonate·chloride spring", and so on.

So, on my reading of the table, "*jūsō*" meant the sodium kind and "*jūtansan-dorui*" the calcium and magnesium kind. The term "*jūtansan-sen*" does not appear in the table. This type is separate from the carbon dioxide spring: one is defined by an ion, the other by dissolved gas. The ministry's English booklet heads this type with a form of "carbonate" rather than "hydrogen-carbonate", which adds to the confusion. The Japan Hot Spring Association, a private body, says calcium-hydrogen carbonate springs can leave limy deposits.

### Sulphate spring

A sulphate spring (*ryūsan'en-sen*) is a salt spring whose main negative ion is sulphate. By main positive ion it can be sodium-, magnesium-, calcium-, iron(II, III)- or aluminium-sulphate. In an acidic sulphate spring, sulphate and hydrogen sulphate are added together to decide whether sulphate is the main ion. The old names map neatly:

- *Bōshō-sen* (Glauber's salt spring) is today's **sodium-sulphate spring**.
- *Sekkō-sen* (gypsum spring) is today's **calcium-sulphate spring**.
- *Sei-kumi-sen* (bitter spring) is today's **magnesium-sulphate spring**.

**A sulphate spring is not a sulphur spring.** The sulphur spring is a special-component type, 2 mg or more of total sulphur per kilogram. Some water is both, as in the old "gypsum hydrogen sulphide spring", now "sulphur-containing calcium-sulphate spring (hydrogen sulphide type)".

### Iodine spring

An iodine spring (*gan-yōso-sen*) has 10 mg/kg or more of iodide ion. With under 1,000 mg/kg of dissolved substances it is a "simple iodine onsen" at 25°C or more, or a "simple iodine cold mineral spring" below that. A salt spring with that much iodide gets "iodine-containing" at the front, as in the guidelines' example "iodine-containing sodium-chloride spring", the new name for the old "iodine-containing common salt spring". Among the special components, iodide comes last in the fixed order: hydrogen ion, sulphur, carbon dioxide, radon, iron, iodide.

Both the association and the ministry's booklet say iodine springs are common among non-volcanic onsen and turn yellow when left standing.

### Simple spring

A simple spring (*tanjun onsen*) has **less than 1,000 mg/kg** of dissolved substances and a source temperature of 25°C or more. If it also passes a special-component threshold, it takes that name instead, such as "simple iodine onsen". The plain "simple spring" name is what is left when no special component reaches its threshold.

"Simple" is a definitional word about the amount dissolved, and the limit is under 1,000 mg, not zero. Cooler water with no special component is not a therapeutic spring at all; its analysis records which item in the Act made it a legal onsen, such as metasilicic acid.

## Reading the names: "alkaline simple spring" versus "alkaline"

Two scales use the same word. The pH scale on an analysis runs: acidic under 3, weakly acidic 3 to under 6, neutral 6 to under 7.5, **weakly alkaline 7.5 to under 8.5**, alkaline 8.5 and above.

The type name "alkaline simple spring" (*arukari-sei tanjun onsen*) is given only to a simple spring whose pH, measured on site, is 8.5 or more. A simple spring at pH 8.07 is weakly alkaline on the pH scale and is named just "simple spring". Yumura's Nishinogaki source in Hyōgo is exactly that: "simple spring (hypotonic, weakly alkaline, onsen)" at pH 8.07.

On a sign the two can sit side by side. Dōgo's water is an "alkaline simple spring (hypotonic, alkaline, high-temperature onsen)", with "alkaline" once in the type name and once on the pH scale. For salt springs the pH never enters the type name; it appears only in the bracket. Yumura's town analysis shows sulphate springs that are neutral, at pH 7.38 and 6.88.

## Where moor and oil water fit

### Not types, but recorded

The ministry's ten types have no category based on organic matter or oil. A moor or oily water is named by its ions and special components like any other. The guidelines do record these things, though. Analysts measure humic matter (*fushokushitsu*), and the sensory test lists odours including **"peaty"** and **"petroleum"**. So a peaty or petroleum smell can appear on an analysis, but never as the type name.

### Oil: Toyotomi

The one official example I found is Toyotomi in northern Hokkaido. The ministry's plan for it (January 2022) says the onsen gushed out with natural gas in 1925 during an attempt to drill for oil. It gives the type as a sodium-chloride spring, and in its source table writes "**sodium-chloride spring containing oil**". The oil is a note in front of the type name.

Toyotomi town names two waters: an "iodine-containing sodium-chloride onsen" and an "iodine-containing sodium-chloride·hydrogen carbonate cold mineral spring", both yellow and cloudy with a little oil. **The ministry's plan does not mention iodine; the town's page does.** I have not resolved the difference. For Niigata, Akita or elsewhere, I could not find an official source describing oily onsen.

### Moor: Tokachigawa and Obihiro

*Mōru onsen* is a Hokkaido name, used for Tokachigawa among others and chosen as a Hokkaido Heritage in 2004. **I found no official definition.** The ministry's documents never use the word, and neither Otofuke town's plan for Tokachigawa nor the local tourism association's home page defines it or gives a type name. I could not find the official type name for Tokachigawa's water.

The descriptions that exist are local. The Hokkaido Heritage page says moor onsen rises through peat, is dark, and has more plant-derived humic matter than mineral content, with heat thought to come partly from fermenting buried plants. It says it is found in Tokachi, the Ishikari plain and Toyotomi. Obihiro city says the water rises through lignite beds and contains humic and fulvic acids, and that most of Obihiro's onsen are plant-based moor onsen. I could not confirm the origin of the word from an official source.

Research describes the colour, not a type. A conference abstract from Otsuma Women's University says brown-to-black waters with humic substances, "so-called black-water onsen and moor onsen", are found across Japan. In Tokyo's Ōta ward it found ten black-water (*kuro-yu*) wells to be mainly sodium-hydrogen carbonate water at 16.1–18.7°C, with organic matter thought to come mainly from algae. That is Tokyo, not Tokachigawa.

## Where the water comes from

I could not find a general explanation from the ministry, the Geological Survey of Japan or the Japanese Society of Hot Spring Sciences for how any of these four types forms. What exists is regional and hedged.

- **Hydrogen-carbonate.** A 2023 review in the *Journal of Geography* says the original high-temperature water in southern Kii is the *jūsō* type, "probably" from rising carbon dioxide reacting with rock, and that Shirahama turned salty as heavy pumping from around 1941 drew in seawater.
- **Sulphate.** A 2010 lecture in the journal of the Japanese Society of Balneology, Climatology and Physical Medicine says the leading theory for sulphate in acidic springs is volcanic gas, and that there is an idea that sulphate in neutral springs forms when acidic water is neutralised. I found nothing official on neutral sulphate springs in general.
- **Iodine.** A 2021 short paper from the Hokkaido Institute of Public Health found Hokkaido's iodine springs mostly in the north and the Ishikari plain, pumped from wells mostly deeper than 1,000 m, with up to 76.2 mg/kg of iodide. Low sulphate led the authors to think they are "mainly fossil seawater", though more study is needed. Chiba prefecture says nearly all Japan's iodine is produced there, but I found no public source linking that iodine to iodine onsen.
- **Simple.** The 2010 lecture says low-mineral alkaline water, found in granite areas, is thought to come from water reacting with rock.

## The official indications and contraindications

The ministry's posting standard of 1 July 2014 lists these. They are reproduced as what the standard lists, not as a promise; the same standard describes spa therapy as easing symptoms and restoring health rather than curing disease, usually over two to three weeks, and best under a doctor.

| Type | Bathing (official list) | Drinking (official list) | Type-specific contraindications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen-carbonate | Cuts, peripheral circulation disorders, sensitivity to cold, dry skin | Gastric and duodenal ulcers, reflux oesophagitis, impaired glucose tolerance (diabetes), hyperuricaemia (gout) | None listed |
| Sulphate | Same as chloride: cuts, peripheral circulation disorders, sensitivity to cold, depressive states, dry skin | Biliary dysfunction, high cholesterol, constipation | None listed |
| Iodine | None listed | High cholesterol | None listed for the type |
| Simple | Autonomic instability, insomnia, depressive states | None listed | None listed |

**On simple springs.** The pre-2014 wording said type-specific indications were not set for simple springs, while noting traditional indications for alkaline simple springs. That is where "simple springs have only the general indications" comes from. The current standard does list type-specific indications for them. It also says effects come from many factors together and are hard to pin on the components alone. So the position is neither "a simple spring does nothing" nor "it works". The general contraindications apply to every onsen, simple springs included.

Where a name fits more than one type, such as "sodium-hydrogen carbonate·chloride spring", the standard says all applicable indications count.

## Bathing in them

### Hydrogen-carbonate springs and "bijin no yu"

The ministry's booklet calls the hydrogen-carbonate spring the "**so-called 'onsen of beauty'**" (*bijin no yu*) and says it softens the stratum corneum of the skin. That is the booklet's wording. I found no separate official explanation or test result, and no official basis for "beauty" claims beyond it. The association's type page does not use the phrase.

### Sulphate springs

The booklet's note is about drinking: it says the water contracts the gall bladder and stimulates the intestine. There is no bathing note, and the posting standard has no precaution specific to sulphate springs.

### Iodine springs and "iodine sensitivity"

**I could not find any official bathing caution for people sensitive to iodine** in the ministry's standard, guidelines or booklet. The official caution is about drinking. For water with iodide, drinking more than (0.1 ÷ A) × 1,000 mL a day, where A is mg of iodide per kilogram, is contraindicated for hyperthyroidism; the bathing column is blank. The booklet also cautions drinkers with hyperthyroidism. Ōita prefecture separately advises drinking iodine-bearing water after meals; that is a prefectural note.

I found no official warning about the yellowing staining towels or clothes.

### Simple springs

The ministry's booklet calls the stimulation "mild". The association says the water is gentle on the skin, and that alkaline simple springs feel smooth (*sube-sube*); that is the association's description, not a ministry standard. The posting standard's examples of strong water to rinse off are acidic and sulphur springs, not simple springs. I found no official source saying simple springs are colourless or odourless.

## Places named in official sources

Only what an official or association source states; no facility is assessed here.

- **Ureshino, Saga.** The city gives its public bath as a "sodium-hydrogen carbonate·chloride spring"; the association lists Ureshino as a "hydrogen-carbonate spring". I could not confirm a plain "sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring" name.
- **Yumura, Hyōgo.** In Shin'onsen town's 2018 analysis, the Arayu source is a "sodium-chloride·sulphate·hydrogen carbonate spring" at 100.1°C and Nishinogaki a simple spring. **Yumura is not listed as a sulphate spring.** Shichikama and Futsukaichi, other onsen in the town, are "sodium·calcium-sulphate springs".
- **[Gero](/en/soak/gero/) and [Dōgo](/en/soak/dogo/)** are alkaline simple springs, not hydrogen-carbonate; in **[Yufuin](/en/soak/yufuin/)** simple springs are the large majority in a prefectural study.
- **Tsuyokubi, Akita.** The association lists it as an iodine spring; I did not find a city or prefecture source. Its database returns six iodine-spring onsen in all, which is its own listing, not a national count.
- **Association examples.** Hōshi (Gunma) and Amagi-Yugashima (Shizuoka) for sulphate, Kawayu (Wakayama) and Otari (Nagano) for hydrogen-carbonate, Kakeyu (Nagano) for simple.

## Local and elsewhere

- **City onsen are legal onsen.** Tokyo says Ōta ward's black water has long been used in public bathhouses, and warns that such wells can hold flammable natural gas. Obihiro's bathhouses draw on moor water.
- **Natural gas is excluded, not oil.** The Act's definition excludes natural gas consisting mainly of hydrocarbons; that is about the gas, not oily water.

## What people get wrong

- **"Simple springs have only general indications."** That was the pre-2014 wording.
- **"pH 8 means alkaline simple spring."** The name needs pH 8.5 on site.
- **"Iodine springs start at 1 mg."** That is the onsen threshold; the iodine spring starts at 10 mg/kg.
- **"Sulphate means sulphur."** Different types, sometimes both in one name.
- **"Yumura is a sulphate spring."** Its own analysis says otherwise.
- **"Moor is an official type."** It is a local name with no official definition.
