Yubara Onsen is in Maniwa city in northern Okayama, along the Asahi river where it runs out from below the Yubara dam. Its best-known bath is the Sunayu, a large open-air pool fed from the riverbed, free and open all day and night, and mixed. Unlike [Kaike](/en/soak/kaike/) and [Yuda](/en/soak/yuda/), it is a designated health resort onsen area with an environment ministry plan behind it, and that plan gives figures the other towns lack. This page covers the water and its sources, what the "yokozuna" title rests on, the Sunayu and how to bathe in it, the giant salamander, food and the way in. It uses the environment ministry's April 2026 plan, the Maniwa tourism bureau, Okayama's tourism federation, the national tourism organisation and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

What "alkaline simple spring" means, and why the pH matters to the name, is in the guide to [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). For reading the analysis sign, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### What the ministry's plan says

The environment ministry's *Yubara Onsen national health resort onsen plan*, revised in April 2026, covers five spring areas in north-eastern Maniwa: Yubara, Shimo-Yubara, Gōroku, Taru and Maga. Together they make a narrow onsen district about 83.7 hectares in area, running north to south along the Asahi river valley. The areas other than Shimo-Yubara were **designated in 1956** (Shōwa 31) as *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the designated health resort onsen areas.

The plan gives the type for all of them as **alkaline simple spring, pH 9.2 to 9.6**, and says **10 sources supply 49 facilities**. Its source table, with no survey year that I could read:

| Source | Temp. | Flow (L/min) | How it rises | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunayu source | 43.0°C | 60 | natural flow | city |
| Mixed source (four Yubara sources combined) | 44.8°C | 1,262 | natural flow | city |
| Borehole above the Sunayu | 40.4°C | 250 | pumped | city |
| Shimo-Yubara | 39.8°C | 564 | drilled, self-flowing | city |
| Gōroku | 34.2°C | 30.5 | natural flow | private |
| Taru | 34.0°C | 150 | natural flow | city |
| Maga | 39.5°C | 205 | natural flow | private |

The Sunayu source supplies the one open-air bath; the mixed source supplies 14 inns, a public bath, three footbaths and other buildings. Another table in the plan counts three sources for Yubara itself, and the plan does not say which rows those are, so I do not match them up.

### What the tourism bureau says

The Maniwa tourism bureau says Yubara has the most water of the district, that **all its sources flow naturally**, at around 45°C, and that they are managed beneath the Yumoto Onsenkan bathhouse. It calls the water strongly alkaline at pH 9 or above and rich in metasilicic acid. The bureau's "all natural flow" fits the plan's main sources; the plan also lists one pumped borehole, used by a fisheries facility.

### Water rising under your feet

*Ashimoto yūshutsu* is the term for water that rises through the floor of the bath itself. Okayama's tourism federation says there are only 50 to 60 such places in Japan and four in the prefecture: the Sunayu, the Maga Onsenkan, the Gōrokukan and the Okutsu-sō. The Maniwa tourism bureau says 20 to 30 in Japan. **The counts differ**, so I give neither as fact.

### Where the heat comes from

The Maniwa tourism bureau says the heat source is **presumed** to be magma under nearby Mt Daisen or the Hiruzen volcanoes. It gives no source for that. I found no geological survey or prefectural document on the origin of Yubara's water. The 1962 geological map explanation for the Yumoto sheet, covering the Hiruzen area, discusses how the Hiruzen basin formed after Daisen's eruptions blocked the Asahi river, but does not mention Yubara Onsen. So I give the volcanic explanation as the bureau's presumption, and do not call the spring volcanic or non-volcanic.

For background, the ministry's onsen use table (end of March 2025) gives Okayama 221 sources, only six of them 42°C or above. The Sunayu and mixed sources' temperatures fall in that band, but I could not tie the table's six to particular sources.

## Where it came from

### What the ministry's plan records

Citing a history of Yubara town published in 1953, the plan says Yubara can be traced back to the **Keichō era** (1596–1615), that in the Tenmei era (1781–1789) it was managed by an onsen village headman, and that inns were running by the Bunka era (1804–1818). Through the Edo period it was a bathing place for cattle and horse traders on the Daisen road and for pilgrims of the Daisen faith.

The plan also gives the other areas' traditions: Gōroku said to be a gift of Yakushi Nyorai and developed in 1625; Taru, the story of hot water carried in barrels in the Genki era; Maga, the tradition of a warrior healing his wounds in 1333.

### What the tourism bureau says

The Maniwa tourism bureau says the area was known for *tatara* iron-making from the Kofun period and that the water was used for healing, and that Yubara enters history with a record of a bathing place opened for Ofuku no kata, mother of Ukita Hideie. That is the bureau's account, and I have not checked it against historical records.

### The "yokozuna of the west"

You will see Yubara called the *nishi no yokozuna*, the western grand champion, of open-air baths. Both the ministry's plan and the Maniwa tourism bureau name who gave the title: an **open-air bath ranking made in 1980** (Shōwa 55) by **Noguchi Fuyuto**, a travel writer and onsen critic, published by Akatsuki Kyōiku Tosho. The bureau says the criteria included whether a bath was free or paid, public access, management and the surroundings. So it is a writer's and publisher's ranking, not a public selection. The plan adds that Maga was placed as third *maegashira* of the west in the same publisher's 1980 ranking of therapeutic springs. The Yubara Onsen Museum has a room dedicated to Noguchi.

### The dam and the salamander

The **Yubara dam**, finished in 1955, stands directly above the Sunayu, the plan says, and its lake, at 4.55 km², is among the largest in Chūgoku.

The river is home to the giant salamander, a special natural monument, called *hanzaki* locally. In 1971 Japan's first protection centre for it, known as the Hanzaki Centre, was built in Yubara. Next to it is a shrine, Hanzaki Daimyōjin, which **is said** to have been built to calm the curse of a giant salamander killed in the Azuchi-Momoyama period by a young man called Mitsui Hikoshirō. The Hanzaki festival is held every year on 8 August and has been for more than 50 years; in 2026 it was the 64th, with an eve on 7 August.

## How the town works

### The Sunayu

The Sunayu, "sand bath", is a large natural open-air bath made from water rising through the riverbed. The environment ministry describes it as a mixed open-air bath, also called *suna-fuki-yu*, where the water has long risen from the river bottom blowing up sand. The national tourism organisation translates the name as "literally 'sand bath'".

It is **on the Asahi river below the Yubara dam**, not on the dam lake. The ministry's plan treats the view of the Sunayu with the dam behind it as the symbol of the town.

- **Free, 24 hours.** Okayama's tourism federation and the Maniwa tourism bureau say it is open free, 24 hours, thanks to local cooperation.
- **Cleaning.** Both say it cannot be used on **Wednesday mornings** while it is cleaned. It also closes for the year-end clean from the morning until the cleaning is finished, into the evening; in 2025 that was 24 December. The federation's English page says "24 hours, no regular holiday" with no mention of cleaning; I go by the Japanese pages.
- **Three pools.** The pools are at different temperatures and are named Bijin-no-yu, Kodakara-no-yu and Chōju-no-yu.

### How to bathe there

The Maniwa tourism bureau and Okayama's tourism federation give the same rules:

- It is **mixed bathing**. There are simple changing areas, separate for men and women.
- For women, a rented bathing wrap (*yuami-gi*) is recommended; wrapping a towel around yourself is also fine. Men should wrap a towel too.
- **Swimwear is allowed.** Bathing in underwear or other clothes is not.

Wraps can be rented at the Yubara tourist information centre (closed Tuesdays) and the Yumoto Onsenkan (closed Wednesdays), and inns lend them to their guests. Prices are shown without a tax note, so I do not give them.

The bureau also carries a piece of advice from the town's "onsen guides", whom the plan lists among staff trained by the local inns' cooperative: at the Sunayu, bathe once a day on the first and second days and twice, morning and evening, from about the third. That is their advice, not an official rule, and I do not repeat the health claims that go with it.

### Footbaths and the public bath

Okayama's tourism federation lists a hand-and-foot bath by the Tsuzumi bridge, and the Yubara Yuttari Hiroba by the Asahi river, about 26 metres long and big enough for 50 people at once. The ministry's plan says these, and footlights marking the 24 solar terms, were built under a national urban renewal programme over five years from 2012.

The **Yumoto Onsenkan** public bath opens 10:00 to 21:00, last entry 20:30, and is closed on Wednesdays. Fees are shown without a tax note, so I do not give them.

### The rest of the district

The plan counts about 14 inns and hotels over about a kilometre, north to south. Okayama's tourism federation lists 17 (five hotels and twelve inns). The counts are made differently.

**Shimo-Yubara** is new: water came up from about 27 metres down on 10 January 1989, during a survey for a new source. The plan and the bureau mention a city day-bathing facility with an open-air bath and a bath for pets.

## Bathing there

- **English and tattoos.** Okayama's tourism federation's English page, the national tourism organisation's page and the Maniwa tourism bureau's English top page say nothing about tattoos. The full bathing rules above are on the Japanese pages only. I found no official rule on tattoos. Ask each bath or inn.
- **Mixed bathing in the open.** The Sunayu is beside the river and below the dam, in public view.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Yubara source that compares its water with baths in the cities. The posted sign, saying whether the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected, is the fair way to compare; see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

**No salamander dishes.** The Maniwa tourism bureau's 17 food entries for Yubara include local chicken and game, but nothing made from *hanzaki*, which the town protects, displays and uses as a festival symbol. I do not write of "hanzaki cuisine".

**Wild boar.** The farm ministry lists *shishi-nabe*, boar hotpot, as a dish of the Mimasaka area, famous as a winter dish there. On the town's side, Okayama's tourism federation lists the Yubara Onsen food festival, the Shishi Matsuri, held each January on the second Sunday and the Monday after (11 and 12 January in 2026; 2025 was the 16th), when restaurants and inns in the town serve game. The ministry's page does not name Yubara, so I say both sides feature boar, not that the ministry calls it a Yubara dish.

**Mackerel sushi.** The farm ministry places *saba-zushi* in Maniwa and Takahashi, a festival dish made with salted fish carried over from Tottori. Its Okayama story names Yubara, with Okutsu and Yunogō, among the three Mimasaka springs, and mentions the sushi in the same piece. Yubara is in Maniwa, but the ministry does not name the spring for the dish, and mackerel sushi is not among the bureau's 17 Yubara entries. More in the [Setouchi local dishes guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/).

## Getting there

Checked 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **By train and bus.** Okayama's tourism federation gives JR Chūgoku-Katsuyama station, then the bus for Hiruzen Kōgen about 35 minutes to "Yubara Onsen", and about 10 minutes on foot.
- **From Okayama station.** The national tourism organisation gives 2 hours 35 minutes by bus, changing at Katsuyama bus centre, then 5 minutes on foot. Its page address carries the year 2022, so the times may be old.
- **By car.** From Yubara interchange on the Yonago expressway, about 10 minutes (the federation's town page) or about 5 minutes (its Sunayu and cleaning pages); the plan says about 5 minutes to each area. The city car park at the Sunayu takes 50 cars.

I found no official times from Yonago, Izumo-shi or Shin-Yamaguchi. On the coast to the north is [Kaike](/en/soak/kaike/); for Misasa, another riverside bath in Tottori, see the [Misasa guide](/en/soak/misasa/). For the alkaline simple spring at [Yuda](/en/soak/yuda/) in Yamaguchi, see its own guide.

## What people get wrong

- **"The Sunayu is on the dam lake."** It is on the river below the dam.
- **"It is open around the clock."** Except on Wednesday mornings and for the year-end clean.
- **"You have to bathe naked."** Swimwear, a wrap or a towel is allowed; underwear is not.
- **"It was officially ranked the best open-air bath in western Japan."** The "yokozuna of the west" comes from a travel writer's 1980 ranking, published by a private publisher.
- **"Yubara is a volcanic spring."** The tourism bureau presumes a volcanic heat source; I found no official geological account.
- **"There are only 20 to 30 springs like it in Japan."** The bureau says 20 to 30, the prefecture's federation 50 to 60.
- **"Simple means weak."** "Simple" is a word about how much is dissolved, as the [simple springs guide](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) explains, not a verdict on the water.
