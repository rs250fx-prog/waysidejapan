Akanko Onsen is on the shore of Lake Akan, in the city of Kushiro, inside Akan-Mashu National Park. The lake is a caldera lake listed under the Ramsar Convention and the home of the marimo, the round green balls of algae that are a special natural monument. Beside the hot spring town is Akan Ainu Kotan, which its own site calls one of the largest kotan (Ainu settlements) in Hokkaido. The lake, the marimo and the kotan are well documented. The water, oddly, is not; official sources give no spring name, opening year or single figure for it. This page sets out what the environment ministry, Kushiro city, the tourism agency, the Agency for Cultural Affairs and the local tourism association say, and marks the gaps. It does not rate any inn, bath or performance.

For how Hokkaido’s onsen towns compare, see the [Hokkaido overview](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/). For how to read the analysis posted at a bath, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### The lake and the volcanoes

The environment ministry’s Ramsar leaflet for Lake Akan (March 2023) gives the plainest account. About 150,000 to 200,000 years ago, eruptions of the Akan volcano formed an old caldera lake, Ko-Akanko. Volcanic activity at Me-Akandake (1,499 m), O-Akandake (1,370 m) and others then shrank and divided it, and Lake Akan is one of the lakes left. It is a freshwater lake 420 m above sea level, with an area of 1,318 ha and an average depth of 18.7 m. It was registered as a Ramsar site on 8 November 2005, number 1540.

The tourism agency’s multilingual text database calls Lake Akan the third-largest lake in Akan-Mashu National Park, and says natural hot springs rise along its southern shore, where there are pools of bubbling mud volcanoes called *bokke*.

The Japan Meteorological Agency’s Sapporo office lists Me-Akandake among the nine volcanoes in Hokkaido it watches around the clock with instruments. That page does not talk about hot springs, and no official source explains where Akanko Onsen’s water comes from. So this page does not tie the water to a particular volcano.

### The marimo

The Ramsar leaflet calls Lake Akan the only place in the world where large spherical marimo grow in colonies. They were made a national natural monument in 1921 and a special natural monument in 1952. The tourism agency’s text says they were found in 1897 by Kawakami Takiya, a student at Sapporo Agricultural College, and Kushiro city’s timeline also lists the discovery of marimo in Lake Akan in Meiji 30 (1897). The same text gives the Ainu word as *torasampe*, which it glosses as "the spirit of the lake".

The marimo are protected: in the agency’s words, it is forbidden to take them from the lake, let alone touch them. The marimo exhibition and observation centre is on Churui island, reached by sightseeing boat.

### How the town grew

Kushiro city’s page on the former town of Akan gives the history available. It is a history of the lakeshore, not of the spring.

- **Matsuura Takeshirō** was the first to explore the lake and make it known, the city says.
- **1893.** Japanese settlers (*wajin*) began moving to the lakeshore, starting with the keeper of a kokanee hatchery. Sulphur mining later brought many workers and labourers.
- **1906.** Maeda Masana received land around the lake and began developing it, as Ippoen.
- **1907.** The city’s timeline says Yamaura Masakichi began running an inn on the lakeshore. The city writes that although inns and hotels now crowd the town, in the Meiji era there was only the Yamaura inn, serving timber workers and a few climbers.
- **1924** the Marimo national road was improved; **1926** buses began; **1930** the Akan crossing road opened; **1934** the area became a national park.
- **1929.** Wakasagi (pond smelt) were moved from Nishi-Abashiri lake into Lake Akan.

The city does not give a year for the opening of the spring or say how the water was found. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation says only that the town has more than 100 years of history. This page does not treat 1907 as the opening year; the city calls it the start of an inn.

### The Ainu and the kotan

Kushiro city writes carefully here, and this page follows its wording.

- Pottery from the early Jōmon period, 8,000 to 5,000 years ago, has been found at sites in Akan, which shows that people lived there from that time. The city adds that it is too hasty to equate those first inhabitants straight away with the Ainu.
- Ainu living in the area appear in a document of 1804, and Matsuura Takeshirō’s diary of 1858 describes an Ainu kotan along the Akan river.
- On the lakeshore kotan, the city says that the third head of the Maeda family, Mitsuko, gave land free of charge and opened the way to a livelihood and to schooling, and that Ainu gathered at the lakeshore and formed a settlement there. It gives no year.
- Folk crafts, traditional dance and the Marimo Festival, the city says, gave a push to the growth of tourism.

The name, in the city’s account, is said to come from the Ainu *akan*, meaning "immovable", given to O-Akandake and Me-Akandake.

**The Marimo Festival.** The tourism agency’s text says that after a drought in 1950 killed large numbers of marimo, local people set up a society to protect them (now the Marimo Conservation Society) and began the Marimo Festival, at which traditional Ainu rites are performed. Kushiro city shows a photograph of the festival in 1963. The Akan Ainu Kotan site posted a notice on 13 September 2026 that some Ikor dance performances would be suspended because of the 77th Marimo Festival.

**Ainu traditional dance.** The Agency for Cultural Affairs’ database lists *Akan Ainu traditional dance* as an intangible folk cultural property for which records should be made, selected on 8 December 1975, with the Akan Ainu Folk Preservation Society as the body that keeps it. It describes it as a performing art handed down in the Ainu kotan on the shore of Lake Akan, danced at the feast after rituals and at various events. Kushiro city calls it a nationally designated folk cultural property without giving a category or year. This page gives the 1975 selection, which is the one in the agency’s own record.

## The water

The tourism association, Kushiro city, Hokkaido’s tourism organisation, the tourism agency’s database and the environment ministry do not give the spring name, the number of sources, the flow, the temperature or the pH for Akanko Onsen. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation’s page on the town does not name a spring type.

The nearest thing is Kushiro city’s English tourism guide. The site calls itself official, but the page does not name who runs it. It says most of Akanko Onsen’s water is simple spring, colourless and without a strong smell. It gives no spring name in the official form and no figures, so this page treats it as a description, not an analysis. If that is right, the guide to [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) explains what "simple" means; the way to be sure is the analysis each bath must post.

The environment ministry’s list of national health resort onsen shows five in Hokkaido, and Akanko is not among them. That is all the list says.

## How the town works

**Akan Ainu Kotan.** The kotan’s official site, run by the Akan Ainu Crafts Cooperative, says about 120 people live there and calls it one of the largest kotan in Hokkaido. It sorts what it offers into watching (traditional dance, staged performances in new styles, seasonal festivals), taking part (craft workshops, tours guided by Ainu, restaurants serving Ainu food) and buying. The performances it lists are *Akan Yukar "Lost Kamuy"*, Ainu traditional dance, *Rimse* under the full moon and an international mouth-harp festival. It posted a change to performance start times on 11 February 2026. The pages checked give no prices, so none are given here. The tourism association describes the kotan as a place to meet a way of life still handed down through crafts, food, dance and storytelling, and says opening hours and closing days differ from place to place.

**Onnechise.** Hokkaido’s prefectural guide to Ainu culture describes Onnechise as an art museum of the Akan Ainu at the top of the slope in Akanko Ainu Kotan, keeping tradition while creating new work.

**At night.** The tourism association also features KAMUY LUMINA, a night walk through the forest based on Ainu myth. Its prices are not given here.

**Footbaths and hand baths.** Kushiro city’s English guide says there are footbath and hand-bath spots in the town, and that hand baths are said to have been first devised at Akanko Onsen. That is the guide’s "it is said", and it gives no basis. Official sources do not give the names, hours or prices of the spots.

**Through the year.** The tourism agency’s text says sightseeing boats run from the end of April to November, and that in winter people fish for wakasagi on the ice and ski; from January there is wakasagi fishing and snowmobiling. The tourism association’s top page mentions frost flowers and walks on the frozen lake in winter.

## Bathing there

Official sources give no day-bathing prices marked as including tax, nothing on tattoos or bathing manners, no comparison of Akan’s water with baths in the cities and no town-level figures to set beside them.

**Day bathing.** With no tax-inclusive prices on the tourism association’s pages, no figures are given here.

**Tattoos and manners.** The association’s Japanese and English pages, its access page, the Akan Ainu Kotan site and Hokkaido’s tourism organisation’s page say nothing on either. Kushiro city’s English guide describes the water only. Ask each facility before you go; the general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## What it is eaten with

No dish is tied to Akanko Onsen by both the farm ministry and the town. The ministry’s 27 local dishes for Hokkaido, and its page on the region as a whole, do not mention Akan, marimo or wakasagi; the region page names Lake Akan once, as a place, not with a dish. The marimo are a protected monument, not food. Wakasagi were brought into the lake in 1929 and are fished through the ice, but no official source ties wakasagi as a dish to the onsen town. For Hokkaido’s dishes in general, see [Hokkaido’s local dishes](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokkaido/).

## Getting there

Checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

**From Kushiro Airport.** The tourism association gives:

- Direct bus, about 65 minutes.
- Akan Bus route bus, about 65 to 70 minutes, three to six a day, with more from spring to autumn.
- Akan Airport Liner, two a day, booking recommended.
- By car or hire car, about 60 to 70 minutes, about 60 km, by Route 240.

JNTO gives about 60 minutes from Kushiro Airport, and Hokkaido’s tourism organisation about 60 minutes by car from the airport and about 80 minutes from JR Kushiro station. So from the airport the sources agree on roughly an hour to an hour and ten minutes. The summary at the top of the association’s access page does not quite match its detailed section: the summary gives about an hour by direct bus and about 1 hour 20 minutes by hire car. Both are given.

**From Memanbetsu Airport.** The association gives about 1 hour 10 minutes to 1 hour 45 minutes by car, about 67 km. JNTO gives about 70 minutes by Route 240.

**In winter.** The association’s access page lists winter services mainly for 2025, such as the White Pirika (Akan Bus) from 11 January to 23 March 2025 and the winter Kushiro-Shiretoko bus from 1 February to 1 March 2025. The page has no update date and its footer says 2025. The Higashi Hokkaido Express Bus ran one way only, from Lake Akan to Memanbetsu Airport, from 30 January to 1 March 2026. Services for the coming winter are not confirmed. JNTO says seasonal buses run from Akanko Onsen to Lake Mashu and Lake Kussharo in summer and winter.

## What people get wrong

- **"Akanko Onsen opened in 1907."** Kushiro city’s timeline says an inn began then. It gives no year for the spring.
- **"Its water type is well known."** Official sources give no spring name. A city tourism guide says most of it is simple spring, with no figures.
- **"You can pick up a marimo."** They are a special natural monument; taking or touching them is forbidden.
- **"The kotan has always stood on the lakeshore."** Kushiro city says Ainu gathered on the lakeshore after the Maeda family’s Mitsuko gave land, and gives no year. It records Ainu in the area from 1804 and a kotan on the Akan river in 1858.
- **"The first people at Akan were Ainu."** The city says people lived here from the early Jōmon period, and warns against equating them straight away with the Ainu.
- **"The water treats a condition."** This page makes no health claims.
