Zao Onsen sits on the western foot of the Zaō range, in the south-east of Yamagata city, about 880 m up. Its water is strongly acidic and smells of sulphur, it is shared out from five groups of sources to some fifty inns and baths, and in winter the same town is a ski resort under the frozen trees known as snow monsters. This page explains how it works using the environment ministry, the tourism association, Yamagata prefecture and city, the weather agency, the Agency for Cultural Affairs and JNTO. The fullest single source is the ministry’s plan for Zao as a national health resort onsen, revised in March 2023. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For what pH and the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/) and the page on [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/); for the posted notices about added water, heating and disinfection, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Where it came from

### The legend

The ministry’s plan says the history "goes back to around 110 AD", and tells it as a legend. When Yamato Takeru came north on the emperor’s orders to subdue the Emishi, his retainer Kibi no Tagayu was struck by a poisoned arrow and suffered sores all over his body. He found a spring with a strange scent and a sour, vinegar-like taste, bathed in it and recovered within days. The spring was named Tagayu after him, and the name turned into Takayu.

Elsewhere the same plan speaks of 1,900 years since the spring opened. The tourism association says the same: opened 1,900 years ago, found by Kibi no Tagayu during Yamato Takeru’s campaign. Yamagata city calls it 1,900 years and the oldest history in the prefecture. JNTO’s English page gives 110 AD. These are the same story told in different units. The plan itself marks it as "according to legend" and "it is said", and I found no record that dates it.

### From Takayu to Zao Onsen

The plan sketches what came after:

- **Edo period.** The town was the western trailhead for pilgrims climbing to Zaō Gongen and did well as lodgings for mountain ascetics.
- **Meiji.** The government’s break-up of Buddhism and Shinto thinned out the ascetics, and the place became a quiet spa.
- **Meiji to Taishō.** A road to the plain below opened, and a post office and electric light arrived.
- **Shōwa.** Climbers, *tōji* guests (people staying for days or weeks to bathe) and skiers all increased.

The plan says that in 1950 the Zaō range came first in the mountain section of the "New Hundred Views of Japan", run by the Mainichi newspaper, after which Horita village became Zaō village and Takayu became Zao Onsen. That was a newspaper’s poll, not a government selection. The Agency for Cultural Affairs, on its page about Zao’s jingisukan, gives the year of the same poll as 1949. The two do not agree, and I do not pick one.

The plan also says Zao was designated a national health resort onsen in 1958 under a health ministry notice, and that the area became Zaō Quasi-National Park in 1963.

### The "three Takayu of Ōshū"

The plan says Takayu was long known, along with Shinobu Takayu in Fukushima city and Shirabu Takayu in Yonezawa, as one of the "three Takayu of Ōshū", the old north-east. It says only that the three were "familiar" by that name. It does not say who grouped them, and I could not find any official selection.

### The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency lists Zaō as a volcano under constant observation: a group of stratovolcanoes of basalt to andesite, with a caldera about 2 km across formed at the summit some 30,000 years ago. Inside it, the cone of Goshikidake has been active for about 2,000 years and holds the crater lake Okama, 360 m across and also called Goshikinuma. Since records began, eruptions have come mainly from Okama. The highest point, Kumanodake, is 1,841 m.

The agency’s page does not mention hot springs, and I found no official source that ties Zao Onsen’s water to the volcano. This page says only that the town lies in a volcanic range. It does not say where the water comes from.

## The water

### Two sets of figures

The ministry’s plan and the tourism association both give figures, and they do not match.

| | Environment ministry plan (revised March 2023) | Tourism association |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 43–64°C | 45–66°C |
| pH | 1.5–2.1 | 1.25–1.6 |
| Flow | 5,900 a minute, natural flow (unit unclear) | About 5,700 litres a minute; about 8,700 tonnes a day |

Neither gives the year the figures were measured; the only year on the plan is the year it was written. I read the plan’s Japanese text from a PDF whose characters did not display properly, and the unit after 5,900 could not be made out. It may be litres, but I could not confirm that, so I leave it out. The plan notes that the flow is the total of what each facility uses. I do not know why the two sets differ.

What they agree on is the band. Every figure is strongly acidic, well under pH 3, and the association calls it the most acidic in Tōhoku.

### Five groups, forty-six sources

The plan says Zao has five groups of sources with 46 sources between them, all flowing naturally, and that they supply 50 facilities. The groups range from 23 sources serving 22 facilities down to two sources serving two. It calls the town *gensen kakenagashi*, with water running straight from the source.

The plan’s name for the water is an acidic, sulphur-bearing sulphate-chloride spring, some of it iron-bearing. In its description of the town it calls it a strongly acidic sulphur spring and says the smell of sulphur, hydrogen sulphide, hangs over the streets. How to take the long name apart is in the water types guide.

### Yunohana

The plan says the water is so rich in minerals that it turns milky white with *yunohana*, mineral deposits, as it comes out. The deposits have long been gathered and pressed into blocks as a souvenir for *tōji* visitors.

Both the plan and the association describe what sulphur water is said to do for the skin and circulation. This page does not repeat it.

## How the town works

### The three shared baths

The plan and the tourism association both name three shared baths: Kami-yu, Shimo-yu and Kawara-yu. The association gives all three as open 6:00 to 22:00, all year, and says each has a box for the fee. JNTO’s English page, which still carries a COVID-19 note and is evidently not recent, also gives 6:00 to 22:00 and says the baths are simple and inexpensive, that some are unstaffed so you put the fee in the box, and that you may need to bring your own towel and toiletries.

I have not given the fee. The association lists one figure without saying whether it includes tax, and Yamagata prefecture’s English page gives a different, lower figure, with no date. The prefecture’s Japanese page tells readers to check fees and hours on Zao Onsen’s official site.

The plan says the shared baths are cleaned every new year in a tradition called *dōkai*, which thanks the god of the spring and has been kept since the Edo period.

### Footbaths

The tourism association lists three footbaths:

- **Shimo-yu** — 6:00 to 22:00, all year.
- **Robata** — 8:00 to 21:30.
- **Shinzaemon no Yu**, at Yunohana-jaya — 10:00 to 18:00, April to November.

Yamagata prefecture’s English page and JNTO say there are three free footbaths, two open all year and Shinzaemon no Yu closed in winter. That fits the association’s list, but I did not find the word "free" on the association’s page.

### Day bathing and inns

The plan, describing the town, says there are five day-bathing facilities, among them a large open-air bath open from spring to autumn, and more than 75 inns and hotels, counting company retreats and places that do not use the hot spring water. The tourism association also gives five. The plan’s own water table, though, counts eight day-bathing facilities among the 50 it supplies. The plan does not explain the difference, and I do not know whether the two counts measure the same thing.

### The river, the shrine and the townscape

The plan says the Sukawa river runs along the town, with Sukawa Onsen Shrine upstream. Yamagata city designated the Zao Onsen area a priority landscape district in March 2021, in four zones. One, along Takayu-dōri and Yunoka-dōri, is the old town where the three shared baths and the inns stand; another, Juhyō-dōri, is the gateway where the ropeway leaves.

### Skiing and the snow monsters

The plan describes three ropeways from the town: trekking in spring and summer, autumn leaves, and in winter the Zao Onsen ski resort, one of Japan’s major ones, with the world-famous frozen trees. It also says that as fewer people ski, the number of inns has tended to fall, and that more visitors have come from overseas to see the trees in recent years.

Yamagata prefecture’s English page explains the snow monsters (*juhyō*) as trees on which supercooled water droplets freeze in the strong winter wind and snow, building up into huge shapes, a sight found in only a few places in Japan. It gives December to February, at their best in February, viewed from the Zao Ropeway and lit up at night, and says you can also ski among them on the slopes. It warns that conditions change with the weather.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The tourism association’s site switches language through a translation widget. There is no separately written English page, and its `/en/` address does not exist.
- **Manners and tattoos.** I found no official guidance for Zao on tattoos or bathing manners, on the association’s Japanese pages, Yamagata prefecture’s English pages or JNTO’s Zao page. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn or bath.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Zao source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What the plan does say is that the water flows naturally and goes straight from source to bath.

## What it is eaten with

**Jingisukan.** The Agency for Cultural Affairs recognised Zao Onsen jingisukan, grilled mutton or lamb, as a "100-year food" in fiscal 2022, in its category for foods to carry into the next hundred years. Its page tells the story: sheep were raised in the early Shōwa period, the wool price collapsed after the war, and someone had a Yamagata foundry make iron pans modelled on a Mongolian helmet-shaped grill, so that the dish could become a speciality of Zao Onsen after its first place in the newspaper’s poll. It says fresh meat, not frozen, was used from the start, and that at the time of listing there were six restaurants in Zao Onsen, two at the foot of the mountain and one in front of Yamagata station. The tourism association’s food page also lists jingisukan as a Zao speciality. The dish more widely is covered in the [jingisukan guide](/en/eat/meat-jingisukan/).

**Other things on the association’s list.** The same page names cold *niku soba* (soba with meat, served cold) and *ita soba* (soba served on a wooden board), and says Yamagata’s "soul food", *tama konnyaku*, round konjac balls, is available. The farm ministry’s page on *tama konnyaku* ties it to Yamadera and Yamagata as a whole, not to Zao, so I do not call it a Zao dish. The Agency for Cultural Affairs also lists cold *niku soba* as a 100-year food, but without mentioning Zao.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Tokyo to Yamagata.** Yamagata prefecture’s tourism site gives about 2 hours 35 minutes by the Yamagata shinkansen from Tokyo to Kaminoyama-Onsen, and about 10 minutes from there to Yamagata. It does not give a total.
- **Yamagata station to Zao Onsen.** About 40 minutes by bus, says the prefecture. JNTO also says about 40 minutes, with one bus an hour, on an older page. I could not open the bus company’s timetable, so I cannot confirm the current service.
- **From Sendai.** JNTO gives 1 hour 15 minutes by the JR Senzan line to Yamagata.
- **By air.** The prefecture gives about 1 hour 5 minutes by Japan Airlines from Haneda to Yamagata airport, then a shuttle bus into Yamagata city in about 35 minutes.
- **Highway bus.** The prefecture gives about 6 hours 50 minutes from Tokyo.
- **Car.** About 30 minutes from Yamagata-Zaō interchange on the Yamagata expressway by the Nishi-Zaō Kōgen Line, or about 25 minutes from Yamagata-Kaminoyama interchange on the Tōhoku-Chūō expressway, says the prefecture.

I could not open JR East’s own pages.

## What people get wrong

- **"The water comes from the volcano."** It may, but I found no official source that says so. The weather agency describes the volcano without mentioning the springs.
- **"Zao’s pH is 1.25" or "Zao’s pH is 2".** The tourism association gives 1.25 to 1.6, the environment ministry’s plan 1.5 to 2.1, neither with a year. Both are strongly acidic.
- **"Zao Onsen has 1,900 years of recorded history."** The 1,900 years, and the 110 AD date, come from the Kibi no Tagayu legend. The ministry’s plan calls it a legend.
- **"The shared baths cost the same everywhere you read."** The tourism association and the prefecture give different amounts, and neither says whether tax is included.
- **"The rename to Zao Onsen followed a government ranking."** It followed a newspaper’s poll. The ministry’s plan dates it 1950, the Agency for Cultural Affairs 1949.
- **"Zao is officially one of the three Takayu."** The phrase is old and familiar, but the plan does not say who chose the three.
- **"Jingisukan is only a Hokkaido dish."** The Agency for Cultural Affairs recognises Zao Onsen’s own jingisukan as a 100-year food.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
