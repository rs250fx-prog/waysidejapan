Kaike Onsen sits on the coast of Yonago city in western Tottori, at the eastern end of the Yumigahama peninsula, facing Miho Bay with Mt Daisen to the south-east. It is a salt spring by a sandy beach, and it is known for two things beyond the water: the story that it was found bubbling up in the sea, and the triathlon that its organisers say was the first held in Japan. This page covers what the water is and how it reaches the baths today, the different versions of how it was found, how the town works, food and the way in. It uses Yonago city, Tottori's tourism federation, the Kaike inns' union, the Yonago tourism association, the Kaike triathlon association, the national geological survey (AIST) and the environment ministry. Where they disagree, I give each. It does not rate any inn or bath.

How chloride springs are defined, and what the old name "common-salt spring" means, are in the guide to [carbon dioxide, chloride and iron springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/). For reading a spring's name and the analysis sign, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### What it is called

- **Yonago city** gives a sodium-calcium chloride spring and also uses an older description, a *shokuen-sen* (common-salt spring) "containing chlorides of the earths". Its page is dated 27 March 2011.
- **The inns' union** gives a sodium-calcium chloride spring.
- **Tottori's tourism federation** gives a sodium, calcium chloride spring.
- **AIST**, in the second edition of its Yonago geological map (2025), gives a sodium-calcium chloride spring.

So the sources agree on the type. The national tourism organisation's English page calls Kaike a "rare saltwater hot spring"; "rare" is its description.

### How much, and how hot

Yonago city and the inns' union both give **19 sources** and a flow of **4,456.5 litres a minute**, which the city calls the most in Tottori. Neither gives a date for the figures.

The temperature depends on whom you read:

- **Yonago city**: sources up to 83°C, and on another line of the same page, 70 to 85°C.
- **The inns' union**: 63 to 83°C.
- **AIST (2025)**: 65 to 83°C.
- **Tottori's tourism federation**: an average of 66.5°C.

They probably measure different sources in different years, but none of the pages says so, and I do not pick one. A top figure of 83°C appears in three of them. The only pH figure I found is AIST's, 7 to 8; the city, union and federation pages give none.

### "A spring in the sea"

Yonago city, the inns' union and the Yonago tourism association describe Kaike as a hot spring that wells up in the sea. AIST puts it differently. Citing the facilities' published material, it says Kaike began with the discovery, in the Meiji era, of hot water rising in the shallows by the shore, but that **the water used now is drawn by boreholes from 200 to 300 metres underground**. So the finding was in the sea; the supply today, according to the geological survey, comes from wells. I do not say that the baths are fed straight from the seabed.

### Where the heat comes from

AIST says plainly that **the geology of the source is unknown**, although a published borehole log shows acidic pyroclastic rock and mudstone, equivalent to the Kawai and Kuri formations, at 100 to 230 metres down. I found no official source that says whether Kaike is volcanic or not, and I do not guess.

For background, the environment ministry's table of onsen use (as of the end of March 2025) gives Tottori 363 sources, of which 114 are 42°C or above. That is the prefecture as a whole, not Kaike.

Kaike is not on the environment ministry's list of *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, the designated health resort onsen areas. In Tottori those are Shikano, Yoshioka, Sekigane and Iwai.

## Where it came from

### 1900, told three ways

The year is the same in every source I read: 1900 (Meiji 33). The place is not.

- **Yonago city**: fishermen came upon boiling water rising in the **shallows** of Kaike beach by chance.
- **Tottori's tourism federation**: fishermen had long said that if you **dived offshore** you would find warm water, and a natural spring was found in 1900.
- **The Yonago tourism association**: a fisherman working on the beach happened to see **bubbles** coming up, where hot water was rising in the shallows.
- **The national tourism organisation**, in English: fishermen saw bubbles rising from the seabed **about 200 metres offshore**.

Shallows, a dive offshore, or 200 metres out: I give all of them and choose none.

### The name

The Yonago tourism association tells an anecdote about the name. In the Tenshō era (1573–1592) there was a large pond here called *Kaike* written as "sea pond"; in 1867 the local name, written with different characters, became the official one. The characters now used mean "all live", which the association also points out at the Kaike Onsen shrine, whose deity is Ōkuninushi.

### The man who laid it out

The association names Arimoto Matsutarō as the founder of the town's development. It says he prepared the land, drew up a town plan, secured the springs in a safe place and pushed for a railway from Yonago station. His bust stands in the seaside park.

### What it is called, and by whom

- **"The largest onsen area in San'in"** is Tottori's tourism federation's description, which it backs with about 30 inns and capacity for 4,000 guests.
- **"One of the finest springs in San'in"** is the Yonago tourism association's phrase, and the same words appear in the environment ministry's description of its bathing beach selection (below).
- **The environment ministry's "88 bathing beaches of Japan"**, chosen in 2001, includes Kaike Onsen. The ministry's note says the beach sits next to Kaike, so you can enjoy both onsen and sea bathing, and mentions the triathlon.
- The inns' union and the tourism association also say Kaike was chosen among "100 beaches of Japan", "100 white sand and green pine sites", "100 sunsets and sunrises" and "100 townscapes", **without saying who chose them**. The ministry's bathing beaches are the only one of these where I could confirm the selector.

I found no "three great" title for Kaike.

## How the town works

### The beach

The beach is now called Kaike Onsen Kaiyū Beach (formerly Kaike Onsen bathing beach), and Yonago city notes it is one of the ministry's 88 bathing beaches. The inns' union calls Kaike one of the few onsen areas in Japan where you can bathe in hot water and in the sea at once, and says trained lifeguards are on duty. The Yonago tourism association explains the shape of the shore: breakwaters stand offshore, sand builds up between them and the land in what it calls a tombolo, and the beach runs in a line of bowl-shaped curves.

### The footbath

Yonago city says there is a **free footbath** in the front garden of the Yonago tourist centre, with towels to borrow. That page is dated 27 March 2011, and I have not confirmed the footbath today. The centre's own site gives its address as 3-1-1 Kaike Onsen, opening hours of 8:30 to 18:00 with irregular closing days, and bicycles for hire.

### Day bathing

I found **no communal public bath** on any of the official pages. Day bathing is at the inns and facilities. The Yonago tourism association says you pay the bathing fee at each one, and that inns may turn you away, or change their hours, when they are closed or full. The national tourism organisation's English page says there are more than 20 day-bathing facilities. The association lists fees, but without saying whether tax is included, so I do not give them.

### The triathlon

This is the town's central event, and the triathlon association tells its beginning in detail. On **20 August 1981**, at 7 am, 53 competitors started on Kaike beach over a course of about 100 km in all. It began, the association says, with a committee member remarking that there was apparently some sport in Hawaii called "tri-something", after which the organisers went to two people in Kumamoto for advice. The rules of that first race included driving competitors from the swim to the cycle start, allowing ordinary town bicycles, and weighing competitors on the way. The association says the idea came from a planning meeting for the 60th anniversary of Kaike's development; it does not say which year the 60 years were counted from.

Today, according to the Yonago tourism association, the race is held in **mid-July**: a 3 km swim, 145 km on the bike along the Daisen foothill road, and a 42.195 km run out and back between Yonago and Sakaiminato, with around 3,000 volunteers on the day. The inns' union calls Kaike the birthplace of triathlon in Japan and says the All Japan Triathlon Kaike race has been held every July since 1981.

The association's site (marked ©2026) announces the **44th** race and says it is a Tottori open event of the World Masters Games 2027 Kansai. I could not reconcile 44 races with the years since 1981 from the association's pages, and I have not checked the "first in Japan" claim with the national triathlon federation. So I say the race association describes Kaike as where Japan's first triathlon was held in 1981.

There is also a Triathlon Street with a commemorative statue, on the way to the finish.

## Bathing there

- **English and tattoos.** The inns' union's English page, the national tourism organisation's page and the Yonago tourism association's pages say nothing about tattoos or bathing manners. I found no official rule. Ask each inn or facility.
- **Where you bathe.** With no communal bath, check with the inn or facility before you go, since the tourism association warns they may refuse day visitors when full.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Kaike source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What you can compare anywhere is the posted sign, which says whether the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected; see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

The Yonago tourism association names Kaike's local food as rock oysters from the Japan Sea in summer and *matsuba-gani*, snow crab, in winter, with wild boar, mushrooms and mountain vegetables from the hills. Another of its pages, announcing the crab season, lists places in Yonago, Sakaiminato and Kaike, and says crab needs booking and that prices and dishes should be checked with each. Prices are shown without a tax note, so I do not give them, and I do not name restaurants.

That is the town's side. The farm ministry's list of Tottori's local dishes does not name Kaike, and I did not confirm whether the prefecture's snow crab pages name it. So I say the tourism association presents snow crab as Kaike's winter food, not that it is a Kaike dish in the ministry's sense. More on San'in crab is in the [San'in local dishes guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-sanin/). The inns' union adds that Sakaiminato, with the Mizuki Shigeru Road, is about 30 minutes away by car.

## Getting there

From the Yonago tourism association's access page and Tottori's tourism federation (no update dates; checked 13 October 2026). Fares are not on this page.

- **From Yonago station.** About 5 km. Local bus about 20 minutes, taxi about 15 minutes (tourism association); the federation gives about 15 minutes by car and about 20 by bus. Buses run from stand 2 at the Yonago station bus terminal, Hinomaru and Nihon Kōtsū, to the "Kaike Onsen Kankō Center-mae" stop.
- **From Okayama.** Limited express *Yakumo* to Yonago, about 2 hours. Highway bus about 2 hours 20 minutes, five a day each way. The association puts Tokyo to Okayama at about 4 hours by shinkansen and Shin-Osaka to Okayama at about 1 hour.
- **By air.** Haneda to Yonago Kitarō airport, six ANA flights a day each way, about 1 hour 20 minutes. From the airport, about 20 minutes by taxi, or about 45 minutes by local bus via Yonago station.

The same access table lists a route from Shin-Yamaguchi, which I could not match with current timetables, so I leave it out. I found no official time from Izumo-shi. For the radon spring further east, see the [Misasa guide](/en/soak/misasa/); for Tamatsukuri near Matsue, the [Tamatsukuri guide](/en/soak/tamatsukuri/). Further south, in the mountains of Okayama, is [Yubara](/en/soak/yubara/), and further west, in Yamaguchi, [Yuda](/en/soak/yuda/).

## What people get wrong

- **"The hot water comes straight out of the seabed."** It was found in the sea. AIST says the water used now comes from boreholes 200 to 300 metres deep.
- **"Kaike is 83°C" or "66.5°C".** Both appear, along with 63–83, 65–83 and 70–85°C. They come from different sources and none is dated.
- **"It was found 200 metres offshore."** That is one version. Others say the shallows, or warm water found by diving offshore.
- **"Kaike is a volcanic spring."** AIST says the geology of the source is unknown.
- **"The 100 beaches selection is official."** I could confirm a selector only for the environment ministry's 88 bathing beaches.
- **"There is a town bathhouse."** I found none on the official pages; bathing is at inns and facilities.
- **"Snow crab is Kaike's official dish."** The tourism association features it; the farm ministry's list does not name Kaike.
