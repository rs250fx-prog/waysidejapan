Itō is a city on the eastern side of the Izu peninsula in Shizuoka, reached by train from [Atami](/en/soak/atami/). Its hot water comes almost entirely from pumped wells, hundreds of them, and the town centre has old shared baths, each guarded by a statue of one of the Seven Lucky Gods. It is also where, by the local account, the English pilot William Adams built ships for Tokugawa Ieyasu. This page uses official and industry sources. Where they differ, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

Two sites need telling apart. **itospa.com**, "Izu-Itō Sightseeing Guide", carries the Itō Tourism Association in its footer, and the city’s tourism section is involved in running it; I call it the tourism association’s site. **ito.or.jp** gives contact details for the Itō Onsen inn and hotel cooperative and for a company called ITO ONSEN Co., Ltd.; I call it the cooperative’s site, an industry source.

For spring types and temperature bands, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for what *kakenagashi* means and the notices about added water and recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Where it came from

### "Said to be" Heian

Neither site quotes an early record for when the springs were found.

- **The tourism association’s site** says Itō "is said to" have been open as a spa already in the Heian period (794–1185), and that in the Edo period it was known as a spring whose water was presented to the Tokugawa shoguns.
- **The cooperative’s site** says the springs are said to have been found in the Heian period or earlier, and that in the Edo period the water was presented to the third shogun, Iemitsu, as water for *tōji*, bathing as a cure.
- **The association’s page for one shared bath**, Wada Jurōjin no Yu, says it is the oldest of the Seven Lucky Gods baths and that in the Edo period it presented its water to Iemitsu.

Whether "water presented" and "presented as *tōji* water" describe the same thing, the sources do not say. All of it is in the "said to be" form.

### William Adams and the ships

The tourism association’s feature on Miura Anjin, the name given in Japan to the English pilot William Adams, tells this story:

- Adams was born in Medway, England, and drifted ashore at Usuki in Kyushu in 1600. Ieyasu took him on as an adviser on foreign affairs. *Anjin* means a pilot.
- In **1601**, Ieyasu called him to Edo Castle and ordered a Western-style sailing ship. The mouth of the Matsukawa river in Itō "is said to" have been chosen as the yard, because timber was easy to bring down from the Amagi range behind it. Adams went to Itō with the shogun’s ship commissioner, Mukai Shōgen.
- The first ship, of about **80 tonnes**, was finished in **1604** and used to survey the Kantō region. A second, the 120-tonne *San Buena Ventura*, followed. The page puts it in 1607 in one place and says "from the following year" in another, so I say only that a second ship followed.
- A pit was dug in a sandbar and lined with logs; when a ship was finished, the river was dammed and the water let in to float it. Whether the yard was on the right or left bank of the Matsukawa, the page leaves open, and so do I.
- The second ship was later lent to Rodrigo de Vivero, a former governor of the Philippines who had been shipwrecked, and it left Uraga for Mexico in 1610.

It is a tourism association’s account, and I could not open a prefectural or national source on the shipbuilding. I also found nothing in Itō’s sources linking Ieyasu to Itō’s hot springs. Atami’s city pages have Ieyasu bathing there in 1604, the same year; that is a separate story, told in the [Atami](/en/soak/atami/) guide.

The association lists the **Anjin Festival** every year on 8–10 August, with fireworks over the sea.

### Tōkaikan and the railway

Itō city’s page on Tōkaikan (updated 28 December 2020) says it was founded as an inn in 1928 by Inaba Yasutarō. When the Itō line opened in 1938, its guests changed from people staying to bathe as a cure to groups. The city took over the building in 1997 and reopened it in 2001 as a sightseeing and cultural facility, with displays on writers including Kawabata Yasunari, Shimazaki Tōson and Yosano Akiko. The cooperative’s site adds Kinoshita Mokutarō, who was born here, among others.

### The volcanoes

The Izu Peninsula Geopark says the springs of Atami and Itō are a gift of three volcanoes, **Yugawara, Taga and Usami**, active from about a million to about 300,000 years ago. It does not explain how that heat becomes hot water.

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes the East Izu volcano group, the small volcanoes crowded on the east of the peninsula, the largest on land being Mount Ōmuro. It records a small undersea eruption off Itō, at the Teishi Knoll, on 13 July 1989. That page does not mention hot springs. So I do not say Itō’s springs are heated by the East Izu group.

## The water

### The prefecture’s survey

Shizuoka prefecture’s hot spring survey, as of 1 February 2023, has a row for Itō city:

- **689** sources in total. That includes 63 that have run dry and 32 that are buried, and 298 pumped sources not in use.
- **296** in use: 294 pumped, and 2 drilled wells that flow out on their own. None flows naturally from the ground.
- A total flow of about **30,841 litres a minute**.
- An average temperature of about **44.1°C**.

The average hides a wide range. By district, the averages run from about 27.9°C in Kusumi and 32.1°C in Matsubara to about 46.0°C in Oka, 49.6°C in Komuro and 58.0°C in Usami. Oka has the most sources (230) and the most water (about 11,921 litres a minute). There is no one temperature for Itō’s water.

The same table has Atami at 522 sources and about 19,168 litres a minute. Counts depend on how a place is divided and what is included, so I do not rank them.

### Three different totals

The other sites give other numbers, with no year or surveying body. The tourism association’s site says about 30,000 litres a minute; the cooperative’s site says about 32,000 litres a minute from more than 750 sources. None of the sites says why the figures differ; the way dry and buried wells are counted, or the year, may explain it, but that is my guess. I use the prefecture’s, because it has a date.

### Spring types

The sites describe the water differently. The tourism association’s site says it is colourless, clear and odourless, and that chloride and sulphate springs are the two main types. The cooperative’s site says mostly simple springs and weak salt springs, and JNTO, undated, says the same. I found no national, prefectural or city table of spring types for Itō, and the prefecture’s survey has no column for it. For what the names mean, see the guides to [chloride springs](/en/soak/carbon-dioxide-chloride-iron-springs/) and [sulphate and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/).

## How the town works

### The Matsukawa promenade

The Matsukawa river runs through the centre of Itō, with a walk along its bank. The tourism association’s site says it has cherry blossom in spring and Tōkaikan across the water, and that it is the stage for festivals including the Itō Sukechika festival, a race in washtubs on the river and a lantern float. It is about ten minutes’ walk from JR Itō station.

**Bamboo lights.** For *Itō Onsen Take-akari*, about 500 bamboo lanterns stand permanently along roughly 700 m of the promenade, at Otonashi shrine and in front of the station. The association calls it one of the largest in the prefecture. It gives 18:00 to 22:00, or from 17:30 from 1 October through autumn and winter.

### Footbaths and hand baths

The tourism association lists Fureai no Yu, a footbath in Matsukawa park; Biashi no Yu, a footbath in front of Izu-Kōgen station; Tsubaki no Oteyu, a hand bath on Yunohana-dōri; and Ōgi no Oteyu, a hand bath at the entrance to Tōkaikan. It does not say whether they are free, so I do not say so.

### The Seven Lucky Gods baths

The tourism association’s site says the town centre has old shared baths, *kyōdō yokujō*, that are all *kakenagashi*, with a stone statue of one of the Seven Lucky Gods in front of each. It and JNTO give different numbers, so I do not give a count. The page for Wada Jurōjin no Yu lists adult and child fees without saying whether tax is included, so I do not give them.

The association’s Japanese page on how to use them says:

- Bring your own towel and soap; they are sold at the counter.
- Wash before you get in.
- Keep your towel out of the bath.
- Most are "Kansai style", with the bath in the middle of the washing area.

### Tōkaikan

The three-storey wooden inn, from the early Shōwa period, closes on the third Tuesday of each month, among other days. The tourism association’s page has a revised admission fee from fiscal 2026 and a day-bathing fee for weekends and holidays only, but neither says whether tax is included, so I give no price.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** The tourism association’s English address returned Japanese text when I opened it, and I found no English page of its own on bathing.
- **Tattoos.** I found no official English guidance for Itō on tattoos, and the Japanese pages I read on its baths and footbaths say nothing about them. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each bath or inn.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Itō source that compares its water with day spas or bathhouses in the cities. What the sources do say is that almost all the sources in use are pumped (prefecture) and that the shared baths are all *kakenagashi* (tourism association).

## What it is eaten with

**Mikan.** Shizuoka prefecture’s food site has a feature on mikan picking (published 31 January 2024, with the tourism association as the contact). It calls Itō a well-known growing area, with eight farms clustered around Usami. The tourism association calls Usami "the town of the sea and mikan" and gives picking seasons of about October to January for *unshū* mikan and about February to June for *amanatsu*. The prefecture’s dates differ slightly.

**Daidai.** A prefectural feature (29 January 2026) says the Atami and Itō area ships among the most branch-on *daidai*, bitter oranges for New Year decorations, in Japan.

**Dishes the farm ministry places in Itō city.** The ministry’s list of Shizuoka’s local dishes names Itō city, not the spa, as an area where three dishes are passed down:

- ***Iruka no misoni***, dolphin simmered in miso. The ministry says dolphins were driven ashore in Izu from long ago, that the hunt stopped from 2004, and that from 2019 it was allowed again only to catch animals for aquariums.
- ***Saba no hako-zushi***, pressed mackerel sushi, for Itō and the Izu area. The ministry says it is said to come from 1261, when the monk Nichiren was exiled to Izu and the fishermen who hid him brought him food in tiered boxes. The fish used for the *oboro*, a flaked fish topping, varies by area, horse mackerel or *kinmedai*.
- ***Mago-chazuke***, a fishermen’s dish of chopped horse mackerel on rice, with tea or stock poured over, for Itō and the Izu area.

I could not find these names on the tourism association’s site, so I do not call them dishes of the spa. More Shizuoka dishes are on the [Chūbu local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/) page.

**Himono.** The tourism association’s souvenir page has a heading, "Itō’s speciality dried fish", with nothing beneath it. I found no prefectural or ministry source tying *himono* to Itō. What *himono* are is in the guide to [dried fish](/en/eat/seafood-himono/).

**Not Itō’s, on the record.** The farm ministry places *kinme no nitsuke*, simmered golden-eye snapper, in Shimoda and Higashi-Izu (Inatori), not Itō. I found no official source tying Itō to *kinmedai* or sakura shrimp, and Shizuoka prefecture’s food page for Itō does not list them.

## Getting there

As checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **The tourism association.** Its access page could be read only in its summary line: from Tokyo in as little as 1 hour 45 minutes, from Shin-Osaka by shinkansen in as little as 2 hours 20 minutes, and about 20 minutes by train from Atami. The rest of the page is images and links to the railway and bus companies.
- **JNTO** (undated) gives about two hours from Tokyo station to Itō by ordinary train, and says limited expresses and the shinkansen are faster.
- **Via Atami.** For times from Tokyo to Atami, see the [Atami](/en/soak/atami/) guide; then the JR Itō line, about 20 minutes, by the association’s figure.

I could not find official times for the limited express Odoriko to Itō from Tokyo, Shinjuku, Shinagawa, Odawara or Mishima, and I could not open JR East’s pages.

## What people get wrong

- **"Itō’s water is about 44°C."** That is the prefecture’s average for the whole city. District averages run from about 28°C to about 58°C.
- **"Itō has 750 sources."** That is the cooperative’s figure, with no year. The prefecture counts 689 as of 2023, including dry and buried wells.
- **"Itō’s water bubbles up by itself."** On the prefecture’s count, all but two of the sources in use are pumped.
- **"Itō’s heat comes from Mount Ōmuro and the active East Izu volcanoes."** The geopark names three old volcanoes, Yugawara, Taga and Usami. The meteorological agency’s page on the East Izu group says nothing about the springs.
- **"Golden-eye snapper is Itō’s dish."** The farm ministry places *kinme no nitsuke* in Shimoda and Higashi-Izu.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide.
