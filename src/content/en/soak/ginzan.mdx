Ginzan Onsen is a small spa town in a mountain gorge in Obanazawa, Yamagata, where old wooden inns of several storeys stand on both banks of the Ginzan river under gas lamps. It grew out of a silver mine, became famous again through a television drama in the 1980s, and in winter now limits who may drive in. This page explains how it works using the environment ministry’s plan for Ginzan as a national health resort onsen (revised in January 2021), the ministry’s list of designations, Obanazawa city, the Ginzan Onsen association and a Japan Tourism Agency paper from June 2026. Where sources disagree, I give each with its source. It does not rate any inn.

For what the long spring names mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for the notices about added water and heating, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). The [Honshu hot springs guide](/en/soak/onsen-honshu/) covers the region as a whole.

## Where it came from

### The silver mine

The ministry’s plan says the silver mine is said to have been found in the mid-15th century by a prospector from Kanazawa, and that because the local lords were the Nobesawa family it was called the Nobesawa silver mine. It says the mine was at its height around 1631–32 and closed in 1689 after a major collapse of its tunnels. The hot water, the plan says, was found by chance by miners looking for ore.

Obanazawa city puts the discovery of the silver in the 16th century and says that in the Edo period it prospered as a mine under the shogunate. A Japan Tourism Agency paper, written by the city, calls it the Nobesawa mine under direct shogunate control and Ginzan a spring that came out during its development, with over 400 years of history.

So the plan says mid-15th century and the city says 16th. I do not pick one; the safest wording is that the mine was found in the 15th or 16th century.

### What came after

The plan gives these points:

- **1913.** A great flood caused devastating damage, and the town was rebuilt with large wooden inns.
- **1968.** Ginzan was designated a national health resort onsen in November 1968; the ministry’s list gives the date as 19 November 1968. The plan says it was the second in Yamagata, after [Zao](/en/soak/zao/).
- **1983.** The NHK morning drama *Oshin* was set and filmed here. The plan says the town came into the spotlight once the drama aired, and that when it was broadcast abroad, visitors from around the world increased. The plan says no more than that about the drama, and neither do I.
- **1985.** The remains of the Nobesawa silver mine were designated a national historic site in December 1985; the plan calls it the second silver mine to be so designated, after Iwami.
- **1986.** Prompted by that, the city passed an ordinance in March 1986 to preserve the townscape of Ginzan Onsen. The plan prints it in full.
- **1997.** The plan records that Notoya Ryokan was registered as a national tangible cultural property in May 1997. I give the registration only, not a view of the inn.

### How small it is

As of December 2019, the plan counts 24 households and 85 people, of which 12 households were inns (one with no bath of its own), with room for 667 guests. The tourism agency paper of June 2026 puts the rooms at about 160.

The plan’s figures for fiscal 2018 show what kind of visitors come: 116,861 overnight guests, 2,483 day bathers, and about 110,000 "other" visitors passing through. The tourism agency paper gives the city’s estimate for 2025 as about 318,000 visitors, about 22,000 of them from overseas, and about 132,000 overnight stays.

## The water

### Three sources, one supply

The plan lists three sources, the cooperative’s No. 2, No. 3 and No. 6, which are gathered in one place and managed together. Each is 66.5°C. They give 170, 190 and 100 litres a minute, 460 in all by my addition. All are pumped, and all belong to the Ginzan Onsen cooperative. They supply 11 inns and two public baths.

The plan gives the spring name as a sulphur-bearing sodium chloride-sulphate spring, hypotonic, neutral and high-temperature, with pH 6.9 and 2,130 mg of solids per kg on evaporation. Its figures come mostly from fiscal 2018 to 2019, but it does not say when the water was analysed. It adds that No. 3 was deepened in fiscal 2018, that temperature and flow are measured once a month, and that the water levels at the sources and in the storage tank are checked every day.

Obanazawa city’s page gives the spring as a sodium chloride-sulphate spring, without "sulphur-bearing". The city does not give a year of analysis either. I use the plan’s fuller name, and take the city’s to be a shortened form, though the city does not say so.

### Where the heat comes from

I found no official source that explains where Ginzan’s water gets its heat, or says whether it is volcanic. The only account of its origin is the plan’s story of the miners. This page does not guess.

## How the town works

### The street

The plan places the town in the east of Obanazawa, near the border with Miyagi, on the western foot of the Ōu mountains, with Mount Funagata (1,500 m, also called Goshoyama) behind it. It sits in a mountain gorge at 260 m, inside Goshoyama Prefectural Natural Park. The Ginzan river, a class A river, runs through the middle, with old wooden inns on both sides.

How tall the inns are depends on whom you read. Obanazawa city says wooden inns of three and four storeys stand side by side; the ministry’s plan says four and five storeys. I give both.

The plan says a three-year street project from fiscal 1989 put in guardrails, gas lamps and paving, and that the power lines were moved underground by fiscal 2006. In winter, it says, the town is covered by nearly 2 m of snow. The tourism agency paper describes Obanazawa as a heavy-snow area.

### The shared baths and the footbath

The plan names two shared baths, Shirogane-yu and Omokage-yu, managed by the Ginzan Onsen association. Obanazawa city’s page, dated 17 August 2026, gives:

- **Shirogane-yu** — open all year; for hours the city says to ask. The official spelling is in hiragana, しろがね湯.
- **Omokage-yu** — currently closed. The city gives no reopening date.

I do not give the fee for Shirogane-yu, because the city’s page does not say whether it includes tax.

The city also lists a footbath, **Warashi-yu**, open all year from 6:00 to 22:00 and free, using the source water as it comes. The plan says the footbath was built, and a shared bath moved, in work that began in fiscal 2000 and finished in fiscal 2003.

### Day visitors and the shuttle bus

Ginzan is one of the few spa towns where day visitors are now kept out of the street by car, and the rules have been changing year by year. As checked on 13 October 2026:

- **The city.** Obanazawa city’s page (17 August 2026) asks people not to drive into the town. It explains that in winter the shared car park is closed by snow, the road narrows, and a way must be kept clear for emergency vehicles. Day visitors, and large buses, are to use the Taishō Roman-kan car park, from which a shuttle bus runs to the entrance of the town. It warns that snow can upset the timetable. It gives a fare but does not say whether tax is included, so I do not.
- **The association’s guide for 2025–26.** The Ginzan Onsen association’s English and Chinese guide applies the rules to day visitors only. Private and rental cars, taxis from outside Yamagata, chartered buses, motorbikes and bicycles may not enter, and change to the shuttle at Taishō Roman-kan (364 Kamiyanagi-watarido, Obanazawa). The winter period ran from 20 December to 1 March, with the city in charge until 31 January and the association from 1 February. Buses left every 20 minutes from 9:00, taking about 10 minutes; the last outbound bus was at 18:00 and the last return at 19:30. They stop in front of Ginzansō, about 10 minutes’ walk from the town entrance. The same guide gave autumn restrictions on 1–3 and 22–24 November. It calls the scheme a measure against overtourism, supported by the Japan Tourism Agency, and warns against unlicensed drivers offering lifts.
- **The tourism agency paper (8 June 2026).** It says the shuttle is run by Ginzansō Co., began in 2019, and uses three 36-seat mid-sized buses, one kept in reserve or used only for return trips. Until fiscal 2023 the city only asked for cooperation. The winters of fiscal 2024 and 2025 were a trial period with clear rules and stewards on the ground. In fiscal 2024 the evening shuttles had a cap on passengers and needed a booking; in fiscal 2025 fares varied by time of day and a priority pass was tried. It reports that over 80% of residents surveyed said traffic problems had fallen, and that visitors did not drop in the dearer hours. These were trials, not a settled system, and I give none of the fares.
- **The coming winter.** The association has a page for 2026–27, planned from December 2026 to early March 2027. It strongly asks people not to walk from Taishō Roman-kan. The page itself says it is a draft and that dates, fares, times and booking links are still to be confirmed, so I give nothing else from it. Check the association’s site before you go.

## Bathing there

- **Language.** Obanazawa city’s tourism page switches language through a machine-translation widget; there is no separately written English text. The association’s English and Chinese guide is about traffic and says nothing about bathing.
- **Manners.** The ministry’s plan says the town has used pamphlets and interpreting devices to help overseas visitors understand Japanese customs such as how to wear a *yukata* and how to bathe. It does not give the guidance itself.
- **Tattoos.** I found nothing on tattoos from the association, Obanazawa city or the plan. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn in advance.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Ginzan source that compares its water with baths in the cities. What the plan does say is that three sources are pooled and pumped to 11 inns and two public baths.

## What it is eaten with

**Curry bread and soba.** Yamagata prefecture’s tourism site names curry bread as a Ginzan speciality, and Obanazawa city promotes its soba; both are covered, with the details, in the guide to [Tōhoku local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-tohoku/). Neither is in the farm ministry’s Yamagata list. For the noodle in general, see [soba](/en/eat/soba/).

**Seasonal events.** The plan lists the association’s events for drawing visitors: a mountain-vegetable festival in June (wild-plant soup and local sake), the Obanazawa watermelon festival in August, a mushroom festival in October (mushroom soup and local sake) and a new-rice festival in November (rice balls of new rice and pickles). That is as of fiscal 2019, and I have not checked whether they are still held. The plan also says Obanazawa’s watermelon leads Japan in summer watermelon production; it does not say who measured that or when, so I give it only as the plan’s statement.

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, because none of the official pages says whether they include tax.

- **Rail.** The ministry’s plan gives about 3½ hours from Tokyo to Ōishida station on the Yamagata shinkansen, then about 30 minutes by route bus. Obanazawa city says a route bus, the Ginzan line, runs from Ōishida to Ginzan Onsen and publishes its timetable as a PDF, but gives no journey time. Yamagata prefecture’s tourism site, quoted in the Tōhoku food guide, says about 40 minutes. So the bus takes 30 to 40 minutes, depending on the source.
- **Air.** The plan gives about 50 minutes by direct route bus from Yamagata airport; the city also says a direct bus runs and has a timetable.
- **Car.** The plan gives about 2 hours from Sendai via Ōsaki in Miyagi. Day visitors then park at Taishō Roman-kan and take the shuttle (see above).

The association’s own access page still carries a notice from April 2023 about a suspended service, so I have not used it for current information. I did not check JR East’s timetables.

## What people get wrong

- **"You can drive up to the inns."** Day visitors are sent to Taishō Roman-kan and a shuttle bus, at least in winter and on some autumn days. Overnight guests are not covered by the association’s 2025–26 rules for day visitors.
- **"The rules are fixed."** The tourism agency paper calls fiscal 2024 and 2025 a trial period, and the association’s page for 2026–27 is marked as a draft.
- **"Both shared baths are open."** Obanazawa city says Omokage-yu is closed, on a page dated 17 August 2026.
- **"The inns are four or five storeys" or "three or four".** The ministry’s plan says the first, Obanazawa city the second.
- **"The silver was found in the 16th century" or "in the 15th".** The plan says mid-15th, the city 16th.
- **"Ginzan’s water is volcanic."** I found no official source on where its heat comes from.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
