Three more of Japan's ten official spring types: the carbon dioxide spring, the chloride spring and the iron spring. Each has a trap. The popular word for the first does not match its legal threshold, the second runs from mildly salty water to brine, and the sources cannot agree on the colour of the third. This page uses the environment ministry's documents and a handful of public bodies. The ten types as a whole, and how to read the analysis sign, are in [the guide to onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); the acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs have [their own page](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). This page does not rank any spring and does not say what any water will do for you.

## What it is

### Two tiers, and where the numbers differ

The Hot Springs Act decides what counts as an onsen at all. Inside that, the ministry's *Guidelines for Mineral Spring Analysis* (revised 2014) define the therapeutic spring (*ryōyōsen*), and only a therapeutic spring gets one of the ten type names. Per kilogram of water:

| Component | Onsen under the Act | Therapeutic spring (the type) |
|---|---|---|
| Free carbon dioxide | 250 mg | 1,000 mg — carbon dioxide spring |
| Total iron ion (Fe²⁺ + Fe³⁺) | 10 mg | 20 mg — iron spring |
| Dissolved substances (excluding gases) | 1,000 mg in total | 1,000 mg in total — salt springs, including chloride |

So water with 250 to 999 mg of free carbon dioxide can be a legal onsen but is not a carbon dioxide spring. If you read that "a carbonated spring is 250 ppm", that is the onsen threshold; the Japan Hot Spring Association, a private body, also gives 1,000 mg for the carbon dioxide spring. The guidelines treat free carbon dioxide as a gas, so it is not counted in dissolved substances.

### Carbon dioxide spring, or "carbonated spring"

The official name is carbon dioxide spring (*nisanka-tanso-sen*). Its old name was *tanjun tansan-sen*, simple carbonated spring, and *tansan-sen* survives as a nickname: the ministry's 2026 plan for the Taketa onsen group writes "carbon dioxide spring (carbonated spring)", and the association does the same. The ministry's booklet calls it the "so-called 'onsen of bubbles'".

Most water people call carbonated is not named as a carbon dioxide spring. A salt spring (1,000 mg/kg or more of dissolved substances) that also carries 1,000 mg/kg of free carbon dioxide is named after its salts, with "carbon dioxide-containing" (*gan-nisanka-tanso-*) at the front, as in "carbon dioxide-containing sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring". Only weaker water gets the plain name "simple carbon dioxide spring".

There is a trap in English too. A separate type, the hydrogen-carbonate spring, is defined by its main negative ion (HCO₃⁻), not by dissolved gas. The ministry's own English booklet heads that type "Carbonate springs" and uses "Carbon dioxide springs" for the gas type. So "carbonated spring" in English could be read, by the ministry's translation, as the hydrogen-carbonate type. The old Japanese names were always distinct too: the hydrogen-carbonate spring was *jūtansan-dorui-sen* or *jūsō-sen*, not "carbonated spring".

### Chloride spring

A chloride spring (*enkabutsu-sen*) is a salt spring whose main negative ion, by milliequivalents (mval), is chloride. By main positive ion it splits into sodium-, calcium- and magnesium-chloride springs. The association calls it relatively common in Japan and says it tastes salty.

A sodium-chloride spring with 5,500 mg/kg or more of sodium and 8,500 mg/kg or more of chloride is a **sodium-chloride strong salt spring**. The guidelines note these have become more common with deep drilling that reaches groundwater resembling oil-field brine. The old common-salt spring (*shokuen-sen*) is today's sodium-chloride spring; of its old "pure", "weak" and "strong" grades, only "strong" kept a separate name. "Salt spring" is not an official type.

Tonicity is a separate scale, set by dissolved substances: hypotonic under 8,000 mg/kg, isotonic up to 10,000, hypertonic above. Since a chloride spring starts at 1,000 mg/kg, it can fall in any band. By my own arithmetic, a strong salt spring is at least 14,000 mg/kg and so hypertonic; the guidelines do not say so in those words. I could not find an official explanation comparing the bands with body fluids.

### Iron spring

An iron spring (*gantetsu-sen*) has 20 mg/kg or more of iron ion, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ together. Weaker water is a "simple iron onsen" (or "simple iron cold mineral spring" below 25°C); a salt spring with that much iron gets "iron-containing" (*gantetsu-*) at the front. The association splits iron springs into a hydrogen-carbonate type and a sulphate type, matching the old "carbonate iron" and "green vitriol" names. The detailed rule for writing iron(II) and iron(III) into a name is more involved than it looks, and I could not reconcile the sources, so I leave it out.

## Reading the names

The pattern is *[special component]-[main positive ion]-[main negative ion] spring*.

- **Secondary ions** follow in descending order if they reach 20 mval%, as in "sodium·calcium-chloride·sulphate spring".
- **Carbon dioxide and iron are special components.** Above their thresholds they go at the front, in a fixed order: hydrogen ion, sulphur, carbon dioxide, radon, iron, iodide. The guidelines' own example is a "carbon dioxide-containing, iron(II, III) simple cold mineral spring".
- **Below 25°C** a salt spring ends in "cold mineral spring" rather than "onsen".
- **The old prefix "carbonic acid-containing"** (*gan-tansan-*) is now "carbon dioxide-containing".

So a visitor looking for "carbonated" on a sign may not find it. The ministry's plan gives Nagayu's main types as "hydrogen-carbonate spring and carbon dioxide spring (carbonated spring)", and in its source table names such as "carbon dioxide-containing magnesium·sodium-hydrogen carbonate spring" stand out. I did not find one named "simple carbon dioxide spring", though the table as I extracted it was incomplete.

## Where the water comes from

The public material is thinner than the folklore, and mostly regional.

- **Carbon dioxide.** The Japan Meteorological Agency lists carbon dioxide among the main components of volcanic gas, but does not say carbonated springs get their gas from volcanoes. A 2010 conference abstract by researchers including the Geological Survey of Japan says salty and carbonated groundwater is widespread even away from volcanoes, and that deep carbon dioxide across Shikoku may come from marine carbonates in the subducted plate. That is a "may" in an abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper. I could not find an official source on where Nagayu's gas comes from.
- **Chloride, from old seawater.** The ministry's guidelines say seawater-origin onsen, the "fossil water type", have become common with deep drilling, and that such water has far less sulphate than today's sea. They also warn analysts **not to classify present-day seawater as onsen** in cold salty wells right on the coast. Tokyo's environment bureau says deep wells in the wards and eastern Tama reach water above 25°C carrying sodium chloride from seawater trapped in the strata, which qualifies as onsen.
- **Chloride, from volcanoes.** The same Tokyo page says islands such as Ōshima, Miyakejima and Hachijōjima have many volcanic onsen rich in sodium chloride, some above 80°C.
- **Chloride, from deep below.** The Geological Survey's 2004 page describes "Arima-type" water across Kinki: some boils up far from any volcano, some is over twice as salty as seawater, and though its isotopes resemble magmatic gas, current volcanology says magma is very unlikely there. A 2010 abstract says it is thought to come from dehydration of altered basalt in the subducting plate. These are regional studies, not a rule for all chloride springs.
- **Iron, and why it changes.** The guidelines say iron is mostly Fe²⁺ just after the water emerges, but in acidic water exposed to air for a long time, oxidation increases Fe³⁺; in a bath, over half can be Fe³⁺. The association says the iron oxidises in air and turns reddish-brown. I found no official source on where the iron in the ground comes from.

## The official indications and contraindications

The ministry's posting standard of 1 July 2014 lists these. They are reproduced as what the standard lists, not as a promise; the same standard describes spa therapy as easing symptoms and restoring health rather than curing disease, usually over two to three weeks, and best under a doctor.

| Type | Bathing (official list) | Drinking (official list) | Type-specific contraindications (bathing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Cuts, peripheral circulation disorders, sensitivity to cold, autonomic instability | Weak digestive function | None listed |
| Chloride | Cuts, peripheral circulation disorders, sensitivity to cold, depressive states, dry skin | Atrophic gastritis, constipation | None listed |
| Iron | None listed | Iron-deficiency anaemia | None listed |

Two drinking rules touch these types.

- **Sodium.** For water containing sodium ion, drinking more than (1,200 ÷ A) × 1,000 mL a day, where A is the mg of sodium per kilogram, is contraindicated for conditions needing salt restriction, such as kidney failure, heart failure and high blood pressure. It applies to any water with sodium, not only chloride springs.
- **Free carbon dioxide.** The standard says a drinking amount is shown by a separate method; the ministry's booklet gives (1,000 ÷ A) × 1,000 mL per drink.

## Bathing in them

### Carbon dioxide springs, bubbles and temperature

The ministry's booklet says small bubbles stick to the body, and that carbon dioxide is absorbed through the skin, with heat-retention and circulation effects "known". That is the booklet's wording; I did not find a separate official explanation. The association says the water can lose its gas when heated.

Are carbonated springs always lukewarm? I found no official source saying so, or giving a reason. The ministry's plan gives Nagayu's sources as 25°C to 52°C. A 2022 Oita prefecture tourism article puts one Nagayu bath at 32°C against a typical 40°C, and says that suits people who dislike hot water. The ministry's English booklet says unstable components such as carbon dioxide change easily after the water emerges. The posting standard has no bathing precaution specific to carbon dioxide springs.

### Chloride springs, salt on the skin

The booklet calls the chloride spring the "so-called 'onsen of heat'" and says salt clinging to the skin gives heat-retention and circulation effects; again, the booklet's wording.

The posting standard's general advice is not to rinse off, but to towel dry, keep warm and rest about 30 minutes. People with sensitive skin are better off rinsing after strong water, with acidic and sulphur springs given as examples, or where chlorine is used. Chloride springs are not in the examples. Locally, the Noboribetsu tourism association's English page says soap does not lather well in its salt springs and that people with weak skin should avoid long soaks; that is one association about one place.

### Iron springs and colour

The ministry's Japanese booklet says iron springs turn **gold** in air. Its English booklet, the same publication, says **red**. The association says **reddish-brown**, and Kobe city calls Arima's Kinsen "brown". The Noboribetsu association says its iron water is clear when it comes out and turns brownish as the iron oxidises. I have not picked one colour.

On towels and clothes, I found no warning from the ministry, a prefecture or a city; only Noboribetsu's note that white towels turn reddish.

## Places named in official sources

Only what an official or association source states; no facility is assessed here.

- **Nagayu, Ōita.** The ministry's April 2026 plan gives the main types as hydrogen-carbonate and carbon dioxide springs, sources at 25–52°C, and a drinking culture. It says Nagayu was called **"one of Japan's leading carbonated springs"** for its concentration, flow and temperature. A 1991 Oita prefecture publicity piece, reprinted on the prefecture's site, says that at 1,200 ppm it "is said to be Japan's best"; it gives no basis, and I could not find the claim on the Taketa city site.
- **Arima, Hyōgo.** Kobe city lists the Tansan source as a "carbon dioxide-containing, iron simple cold mineral spring" at 18.3°C. For the brown Kinsen, the Tenjin and Uwanari sources are "iron-containing sodium-chloride strong salt high-temperature onsen" at 99.4°C and 100.5°C. The association names Arima as an iron spring example.
- **Yugawara, Kanagawa.** The town gives "sodium·calcium-chloride·sulphate onsen", weakly alkaline and hypotonic.
- **Kinosaki, Hyōgo.** The tourism association gives the six public baths a shared "sodium·calcium-chloride high-temperature spring", on an undated page.
- **Shirahama, Wakayama.** The town lists one source for its Muro-no-yu bath as a sulphur-containing sodium chloride strong salt spring at 74.0°C.
- **Atami, Shizuoka.** The association names it as a chloride spring example.

## Local and elsewhere

- **City onsen are legal onsen.** Tokyo's fossil water qualifies; it is the Act's criteria that count, not the location.
- **"Carbonated spring" claims are checked.** The consumer agency's casebook includes a 4 June 2013 order over an accommodation that described its water as a carbonated spring, implying a therapeutic spring; the bath water was not one.
- **Artificial carbonated baths are not defined.** I found no official definition or concentration standard. A health ministry-funded research report for fiscal 2020 notes bathhouses increasingly installing machines that make carbonated water, which is weakly acidic at around pH 5. That is a disinfection study, not a standard.
- **The 1,500 ppm figure is about air.** The health ministry's public bath hygiene guidelines, as revised in September 2019, set carbon dioxide in bathroom air at 1,500 ppm or less, a ventilation rule, not a measure of the water. The guidelines were revised again in December 2020; I did not check whether that figure changed.
- **Bath salts are a different regime.** They are quasi-drugs; the 2015 approval standard does not mention onsen. A 1988 voluntary standard told makers not to use spring-type expressions or claim ingredients such as carbon dioxide gas directly improve circulation. I could not confirm whether it is still in force.

## What people get wrong

- **"A carbonated spring is 250 ppm."** That is the onsen threshold. The carbon dioxide spring starts at 1,000 mg/kg.
- **"Carbonate spring" means fizzy.** In the ministry's English booklet it is the hydrogen-carbonate type, defined by an ion.
- **"Nagayu is officially Japan's number one."** The ministry's plan says one of Japan's leading; "Japan's best" is a 1991 "said to be".
- **"Carbonated springs are always lukewarm."** Nagayu's sources run 25–52°C.
- **"Seaside onsen are just seawater."** The guidelines tell analysts not to class present-day seawater as onsen.
- **"Iron springs are red."** Gold, red and reddish-brown all appear in official or association text.
