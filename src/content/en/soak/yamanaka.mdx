Yamanaka Onsen is a hot spring town in Kaga city, in the south-west of Ishikawa prefecture, set in a gorge of the Daishōji river called Kakusenkei. Like its neighbour in the same city, [Yamashiro](/en/soak/yamashiro/), it is reached by bus from Kaga Onsen station on the Hokuriku shinkansen. It has two founding stories, a visit by the poet Matsuo Bashō, a lacquerware trade, and a public bath whose men’s and women’s sides are in separate buildings. This page uses the Yamanaka Onsen tourism association, Ishikawa prefecture’s tourism site, Visit Kaga (the Kaga city tourism association’s English site), the trade ministry’s list of traditional crafts and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, each is given with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For how to read a spring’s name and the analysis sign, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### Two finders

The tourism association’s page about the town tells two stories, and does not join them.

- **Gyōki.** About 1,300 years ago, it says, the Nara-period monk Gyōki is said to have found the spring. He carved a Yakushi Buddha, the healing Buddha, from a log and built a small shrine to guard the water. Ishikawa prefecture’s site also says Gyōki found the spring 1,300 years ago.
- **Hasebe Nobutsura.** In the Jishō years at the end of the Heian period, the association says, Hasebe Nobutsura, a steward from Noto, saw a white heron healing an injured leg in a small stream in the shade of the hills. He dug there, a Yakushi image about five *sun* tall appeared, and hot water came up. He opened twelve inns, and this is handed down as the beginning of Yamanaka’s inns.

Visit Kaga’s English page adds a third shape to the story: Gyōki (668–749), guided by the Yakushi Buddha, found the water; soon afterwards war left it abandoned and forgotten; and centuries later people came back and the town took its present form. The association’s Japanese page does not say this. The three are given here as they are told; this page does not choose.

The "1,300 years" is the association’s and the prefecture’s phrase, attached to the Gyōki story.

### Bashō

The association says that on the 27th day of the 7th month of Genroku 2, Matsuo Bashō came to Yamanaka with his disciple Sora, during the journey of *Oku no hosomichi*, and is said to have stayed nine days, visiting the Yakushi hall, bathing and enjoying the scenery. Ishikawa prefecture gives the same date and the same nine days, also as "it is said". The date is given here in the Japanese calendar, as the official pages give it.

According to the association and the prefecture, Bashō praised Yamanaka’s water as one of the *Fusō no san meitō*, the three famous springs of Japan, together with Arima and Kusatsu, and wrote the verse *Yamanaka ya / kiku wa taoraji / yu no nioi* (山中や 菊は手折らじ 湯の匂ひ). That trio is Bashō’s praise as the association and prefecture report it, not an official selection. The association’s page also calls Yamanaka "one of Japan’s three great springs" without saying who chose them.

The association says the Bashō hall, the Bashōdō, was built in October of Meiji 43 and stands where Bashō and Sora are known to have parted.

### Lacquer and porcelain

- **Yamanaka lacquerware.** The association says it has come down from the Azuchi-Momoyama period and made Yamanaka one of Japan’s main lacquerware centres. On the trade ministry’s list of nationally designated traditional crafts (244 items as of 27 October 2025), Yamanaka lacquerware is listed for Ishikawa, designated on 10 May 1975. Kutani ware was designated the same day.
- **Kutani ware.** The association says the old Kutani porcelain is held to have begun about 300 years ago, when Maeda Toshiharu, first lord of the Daishōji domain, had his retainers build a kiln at Kutani village, upstream of Yamanaka. It vanished after some fifty years, and the reasons are disputed. Ko-Kutani ware dates from the 17th century, the association adds. Placing the origin near Yamanaka is the association’s account; the trade ministry’s list does not name a town.

### Yamanaka-bushi

The association calls the folk song *Yamanaka-bushi* "one of Japan’s three great folk songs". It does not say who counts them.

## The water

### What the sources say

- **The tourism association**, for the Kiku-no-yu: a simple spring, hypotonic, weakly alkaline, high-temperature, at 45.2°C. It does not give the year or the source the figures come from.
- **Visit Kaga (English)**: "sodium and sulfate-rich waters".

These do not match, and neither source explains the difference. In the environment ministry’s table of old and new names, the old "simple spring" is now "simple spring" or "alkaline simple spring". For what a simple spring is, see [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/).

Official sources do not give the number of sources, the flow, the pH or how the water forms. Yamanaka is not on the environment ministry’s list of national health resort hot springs; Ishikawa’s only entry is the Hakusan onsen district.

## How the town works

### The Kiku-no-yu

The town’s public bath, or *sōyu*, is the **Kiku-no-yu**, named after Bashō’s verse. The association says:

- It is built in a Tenpyō-period style and stands where a shared bathhouse has stood since the spring was found.
- The men’s bath and the women’s bath are in **separate buildings**. The association calls this "rare in Japan"; that is its phrase, and it gives no comparison.
- The men’s bath is deep. The women’s has deep and shallow parts. The Yamanaka-za theatre stands next to the women’s bath.
- Hours are **7:00 to 22:00**, closed on the **second and fourth Tuesday** of each month, with occasional maintenance closures (as checked on 13 October 2026).

Visit Kaga adds that the bath has stood on the same site for 1,300 years, that the men’s bath has tiles showing Yamanaka’s history, that there is a footbath outside the entrance to the women’s bath, and that you can make onsen eggs in the pond in the square. It describes a large shared tub and showers.

The association lists prices for adults and children, with half price for people staying in the town, but does not say whether tax is included, so no figures are given here.

### Yuge Kaidō and the Yamanaka-za

The main street, **Yuge Kaidō** ("steam street"), runs about 600 m from the Kiku-no-yu, around the Hasebe shrine, to near the Kōrogi bridge, the association says. It has galleries of lacquerware and Kutani ware, cafés and croquette shops. The **Yamanaka-za**, opened in 2002, stages the *Shiki no mai* dance to Yamanaka-bushi and has tourist information. The **Ashiyu** footbath sits beside a drinking fountain the association connects with Bashō.

### The Kakusenkei gorge

The association calls Kakusenkei "the finest gorge scenery in Hokuriku"; again, no one is named as having judged it. A 1.3 km riverside path runs from the **Kōrogi bridge** to the **Kurodani bridge**, by way of the **Ayatori bridge**, a 94.7 m footbridge. From April to November there is a riverside seating platform, the *kawadoko*.

The Kōrogi bridge is built entirely of Japanese cypress. The association gives two explanations of its name: *kōrogi* as "a dangerous path", or the crickets (*kōrogi*) that sing on autumn nights. The fourth bridge on the site was finished on 12 October 2019.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos

The tourism association’s pages do not mention tattoos, and official sources do not give a rule for the Kiku-no-yu. Visit Kaga’s undated English FAQ says that people with tattoos were not allowed in the past, that many public baths have begun to accept tattooed visitors from overseas, and that a cover sticker can make things more comfortable. It does not name Yamanaka’s bath. Ask before you go in.

### Manners

JNTO’s general advice: leave your clothes, swimwear included, in the changing room; wash and rinse before getting in; keep your small towel out of the water; tie up long hair; do not swim or splash; leave your phone outside. It says some traditional baths refuse tattoos but this is starting to change, and suggests tattoo-friendly places or a private bath.

### Compared with city baths

No official Yamanaka source compares its water with baths in the city. For what the notice at any bath tells you about added water, heating and recirculation, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

- **Seafood.** The association says the Sea of Japan is close, and fish and shellfish come from Hashitate fishing port in Kaga: snow crab and the female *kōbako* crab in winter, yellowtail and sweet shrimp.
- **Kaga kani gohan.** Ishikawa prefecture’s tourism site describes this crab-rice set as a Kaga city dish created in 2015, ahead of the Hokuriku shinkansen. Its rules include a whole *kōbako* crab, Yamanaka lacquerware for the tray and soup bowl, Kutani ware for the small dishes and the saucer for Kaga *bōcha* tea, five small side dishes and miso soup. It is a dish of Kaga city as a whole, not of Yamanaka alone, but it is where the town’s lacquer and the food meet on an official page.
- **Street food.** The prefecture’s site has a blog-style article on eating along Yuge Kaidō: croquettes and onsen eggs.
- **The farm ministry.** Its list of 30 Ishikawa dishes does not mention Yamanaka. The nearest is *kishizu*, a vegetarian dish eaten at memorials and Hōonkō in Daishōji, in the Kaga city area. The ministry says *jibuni* is eaten from a special wide, shallow lacquer bowl, but does not connect it with Yamanaka lacquerware.

For the region’s food as a whole, see [Hokuriku local food](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-hokuriku/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not given here: the CAN BUS publishes its fares without a tax note.

- **By shinkansen to Kaga Onsen.** The Yamashiro Onsen tourism association gives 16 minutes from Kanazawa at the fastest, 2 hours 43 minutes from Tokyo at the fastest, about 2 hours from Osaka or Nagoya and about 1 hour 40 minutes from Kyoto, changing at Tsuruga for the last three. Its page has no date.
- **Kaga Onsen station to Yamanaka.** Ishikawa prefecture gives about **30 minutes** by the Hokutetsu Kaga bus on its Onsen–Yamanaka line. Visit Kaga also lists a "Yamanaka Route" bus linking Yamashiro and Yamanaka.
- **CAN BUS.** Kaga’s sightseeing bus starts at Kaga Onsen station and runs three routes: mountain, coast and Komatsu airport. It offers single rides and a one-day digital ticket.
- **By air.** Komatsu airport is about 1 hour from Narita or Haneda, according to the Yamashiro association.
- **By car.** About 20 to 25 minutes from Kaga interchange on the Hokuriku expressway, says the prefecture.

Buses change during festivals. For the Koikoi festival of 18 to 21 September 2026, the association posted that route buses would stop at the Yamanaka bus terminal and that the spa streets would be closed to traffic. Check the association’s notices before you travel; its latest, on 2 October 2026, carried no disaster or closure notice.

## What people get wrong

- **"Take the Kyūtoran bus."** The official Kaga pages checked for this guide do not use that name (キュートらん). The sightseeing bus is the CAN BUS, and the route bus to Yamanaka is Hokutetsu Kaga’s.
- **"Gyōki founded Yamanaka, full stop."** The association also tells the story of Hasebe Nobutsura and the heron, and Visit Kaga has the spring lost and found again.
- **"Yamanaka is officially one of Japan’s three great springs."** The *Fusō no san meitō* is Bashō’s praise, as the association and the prefecture report it. The association’s "three great springs" names no one who chose them.
- **"The water is a sodium sulphate spring."** That is Visit Kaga’s English description. The association gives a simple spring for the Kiku-no-yu.
- **"The Kiku-no-yu is open every day."** It closes on the second and fourth Tuesday.
- **"Tattoos are fine in Kaga."** Visit Kaga says many baths have begun to accept them, in general; nothing official covers the Kiku-no-yu.
- **"The water cures things."** This page makes no health claims. The official indications by spring type are in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

Other Hokuriku spa towns on this site: [Yamashiro](/en/soak/yamashiro/), also in Kaga; [Wakura](/en/soak/wakura/) on Noto; and, in Niigata, [Tsukioka](/en/soak/tsukioka/) and [Echigo-Yuzawa](/en/soak/echigo-yuzawa/).
