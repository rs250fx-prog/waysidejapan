Higashiyama Onsen is a spa town in Aizu-Wakamatsu, Fukushima, with inns lining both banks of the Yukawa river about ten minutes by car from the city centre. It grew as the Aizu domain’s place for curative stays, and its official pages lean on the city’s samurai history, on a monk’s legend and on the painter-poet Takehisa Yumeji. This page uses the Aizu Higashiyama Onsen tourism association, the Aizu-Wakamatsu tourism bureau (the city’s destination management organisation), the Fukushima prefectural tourism body, the farm ministry and the Cultural Agency. Where they disagree, each is given with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the sulphate and simple springs named below, see [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). For reading the sign at any bath, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### The association’s figures

The tourism association’s history page gives:

- **Spring type**: sulphate spring; in full, a **calcium-sodium sulphate-chloride spring**.
- **Flow**: about **1,500 litres a minute**.
- **Source temperature**: **50°C to 60°C**.

It gives no year and names no source, so these are round figures of unknown date. Its English page calls the water "sodium-sulphate hot springs".

### Three ways of naming it

The official pages describe the water three ways:

- **The tourism association**: a sulphate spring, as above.
- **The Aizu-Wakamatsu tourism bureau**: a silky simple spring.
- **The Fukushima prefectural tourism body**: *gan-bōshō shokuen-sen*, a salt spring containing Glauber’s salt, which is the form of the older spring names.

None gives an analysis date or a named source, so this page cannot say which is current. If the type matters to you, read the analysis posted at the inn where you bathe. Official sources do not give a pH or a total number of sources for Higashiyama.

### Where the heat comes from

Official sources do not say where Higashiyama’s heat comes from, or how it relates to the Aizu basin or the hills to the east. The association notes only that Seaburi-yama (870 m), the hill behind the town, separates the Aizu basin from Lake Inawashiro. That is geography, not an explanation of the water.

### Not a health resort

Higashiyama does not have a plan as a national health resort hot spring on the environment ministry’s list.

## Where it came from

### Gyōki and the three-legged crow

The tourism association says the spring is said to have been found by the monk Gyōki about 1,300 years ago, guided by a three-legged crow. On the same page it says the spa opened "in the late eighth century, or in the Tenpyō era". The Fukushima prefectural tourism body gives the Tenpyō era (729–749), and the tourism bureau "about 1,300 years ago". The association’s English page also gives both "late 8th century" and the Tenpyō era.

The Tenpyō era falls in the first half of the eighth century, so the association’s two dates do not agree. This page says only that Gyōki is said to have found it.

The association also tells the legends of Princess Chiho and of how the Fushimi-ga-taki waterfall got its name. They are presented as legends, not history.

### The domain’s spa

In the Edo period, say the association and the prefectural tourism body, Higashiyama flourished as the Aizu domain’s *tōji-ba*, its place for curative stays, and grew into the *okuzashiki*, the secluded retreat, of Aizu-Wakamatsu. Both connect it with Ohara Shōsuke, who appears in an Aizu folk song.

### "One of the three pleasure resorts of Ōu"

The association, the tourism bureau and the prefectural tourism body all say Higashiyama is counted among the *Ōu san-rakukyō*, the three pleasure resorts of the old north-east; the association’s English page says "listed among the top three onsen towns of old Tohoku". None names the other two or who did the counting, so treat it as a traditional title.

### Takehisa Yumeji and other visitors

The Fukushima prefectural tourism body says the painter and poet **Takehisa Yumeji** loved Higashiyama and stayed **three times**. It says portraits of women he painted while here are still kept at an inn and shown to the public free of charge, and that a stone carved with his song *Yoimachigusa* stands in the middle of the town. The tourism association says Yumeji, Yosano Akiko and other writers and artists were fond of the place. Whether the inn still shows the paintings can change; check before you go.

The prefectural tourism body lists Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the Shinsengumi vice-commander **Hijikata Toshizō** and the Byakkotai among those who visited. That is all it says; it does not say why Hijikata came. The association and the tourism bureau do not mention him.

## How the town works

### The river and the setting

The prefectural tourism body describes inns standing along both sides of the Yukawa river. The tourism bureau and the association put the town about ten minutes by car from central Aizu-Wakamatsu. The association’s front page calls Aizu-Wakamatsu "the town of the Byakkotai" and points visitors to nearby sights such as Iimoriyama, Tsuruga castle and the Aizu Bukeyashiki samurai residence.

### Day bathing at the inns

The association’s page of "inns where you can enjoy a day at the onsen" lists each inn’s hours and fees. It warns that hours differ from day to day and that day bathing may be stopped without notice. The page does not say whether the fees include tax, so they are not given here. Official sources do not describe a public shared bath or a footbath for Higashiyama.

### Geisha

The association’s site has a section on the **Higashiyama geisha**. This page has not looked into the details; see the association’s site.

### How many inns

The association lists 13 inns on its Japanese pages and 12 in English. Official sources do not give a single count, so this page does not.

## Bathing there

### Tattoos and English

The association’s English page covers only the founding legend and an outline of the water. Aizu-Wakamatsu city’s English site links to it. Neither says anything about how to bathe or about tattoos, and official sources do not give a tattoo rule for Higashiyama. More in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn in advance.

### Compared with city baths

No official Higashiyama source compares its water with baths elsewhere, and the sources do not say whether the inns’ baths are free-flowing or recirculated. Read the notice at the inn. For what it tells you, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Food

**What the association lists.** Its food and shopping page introduces shops that serve sauce katsudon, an old *yōkan* (bean jelly) maker that says it began when spa guests asked for something to take home, and places serving Aizu local dishes, horse-meat sashimi and river-fish sashimi. These are shop listings. Prices are not given because the page does not say whether they include tax.

**Sauce katsudon.** It is in neither the farm ministry’s 30 dishes for Fukushima nor the Cultural Agency’s Fukushima 100-year foods. Official sources do not link it to the spa beyond those shop listings.

**Kozuyu.** The farm ministry places this Aizu dish in the Aizu area, excluding Minami-Aizu, and the Cultural Agency made it a 100-year food. But Higashiyama’s association pages do not mention it, and the tourism bureau’s feature on Aizu local food, kozuyu and carp simmered in sweet soy, does not mention the spa. So it is an Aizu dish you can look for here, not Higashiyama’s own. More in the [Tōhoku local food guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-tohoku/).

**Kitakata ramen.** The tourism bureau’s food list says one Kitakata ramen maker sells its souvenir packs at hotels in Higashiyama and Ashinomaki onsens. That describes a company’s outlets, not a Higashiyama speciality. See [Kitakata ramen](/en/eat/ramen-kitakata/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page; the association and the bus company give different amounts for the loop bus, and neither says whether tax is included.

- **From Tokyo.** By Tōhoku shinkansen from Tokyo to Kōriyama, about 1 hour 22 minutes, then the Ban-etsu West line rapid to Aizu-Wakamatsu, about 1 hour 5 minutes, not counting changes (the tourism bureau). The bureau also gives a route via Niigata on the Jōetsu shinkansen: about 2 hours, then about 2 hours 22 minutes.
- **From Aizu-Wakamatsu station.** About 20 minutes by the bus bound for Higashiyama Onsen, or 10 to 15 minutes by taxi (the tourism bureau).
- **By loop bus.** The town’s loop buses, Haikara-san and Akabe, run from Aizu-Wakamatsu station by way of Aizu Bukeyashiki to Higashiyama Onsen in 35 to 40 minutes (the association). They are a different, longer route from the direct bus, so the times do not conflict.
- **By car.** About 35 minutes from the Aizu-Wakamatsu interchange on the Ban-etsu expressway (the prefectural tourism body).

The times above are the sources’ round figures, not a timetable. For other spa towns in Fukushima and Tōhoku, see [Iizaka](/en/soak/iizaka/), [Akiu](/en/soak/akiu/), [Zao](/en/soak/zao/), [Ginzan](/en/soak/ginzan/) and [Naruko](/en/soak/naruko/).

## What people get wrong

- **"Higashiyama has a shared bath called Taki-no-yu."** In official sources, Takinoyu is the name of the district in the association’s address and of an inn; no public bath of that name is listed.
- **"Hijikata Toshizō came here to heal his wounds."** The prefectural tourism body says only that he visited.
- **"The spring opened in 729" or "in the late eighth century".** The association gives both on one page; the founding is a legend.
- **"It is officially one of the three best spas of Tōhoku."** *Ōu san-rakukyō* is a traditional title with no named selector.
- **"Kozuyu is Higashiyama’s dish."** It is an Aizu dish; no official page ties it to the spa.
- **"The water is a sulphate spring" (or "a simple spring").** The official pages disagree, and none gives a date.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so.
