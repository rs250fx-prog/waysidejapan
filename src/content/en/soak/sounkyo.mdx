Sounkyo Onsen is in the town of Kamikawa, in Daisetsuzan National Park. Its tourism association calls it the largest onsen town in the Daisetsuzan mountains, and in winter it builds the Ice Fall Festival. The gorge itself has an official explanation, from the Japan Meteorological Agency; the name has a well-documented history, from the National Diet Library and an environment ministry ranger’s account; the water, oddly, has almost none that I could find. This page sets out what each source says and marks the gaps. It does not rate any inn or bath.

For how Hokkaido’s onsen towns compare, see the [Hokkaido overview](/en/soak/onsen-hokkaido/). For how to read the analysis posted at a bath, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### The gorge and the volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes Taisetsuzan (Daisetsuzan) as more than about 20 stratovolcanoes and lava domes of andesite and dacite. In the middle is a small caldera, Ohachidaira, 2 km across, formed about 38,000 years ago. The pyroclastic flow that made it had a volume of about 8 km³, and the agency says that **at Sounkyo and Tenninkyo the deposits from that flow are up to 200 m thick, welded and broken into columnar joints**. The latest phreatic (steam-driven) eruption was about 250 years ago or later. The agency keeps Taisetsuzan under constant observation.

That is the agency’s explanation of the gorge walls. It does not mention hot springs, and I found no official source that says where Sounkyo’s water comes from. So I do not tie the water to the volcano.

### The Ainu

The tourism association says the first people to know Sounkyo were the Ainu, who are said to have passed along the Ishikari river when hunting and followed brown bears and other game up into the high country of Daisetsuzan. It says the Ainu called it *Kamuy-mintar*, "the garden where the gods play". Both are given in the "it is said" form.

The association’s festival page mentions that in 2018 the story "Kamikawa Ainu living with Kamuy" was recognised as Japan Heritage, and that the main ice sculpture draws on this. Japan Heritage is a scheme of the Agency for Cultural Affairs; I have not read the agency’s own page, so this is the association’s statement.

### Early records of the springs

An article on the environment ministry’s Hokkaido office site, written by one of its rangers and quoting the Kamikawa town history, gives these steps:

- **The 1850s.** Matsuda Ichitarō, a low-ranking samurai posted to Ishikari, wrote that there were "hot springs in several places on the bank of the great river".
- **For about half a century** the records say little, until the Meiji 30s (from 1897).
- **1900.** Development of the springs began. The town history also gives the contents of a mineral spring certificate from that year.
- **Around 1915**, the springs were known by the surnames of their operators, such as Shioya Onsen and Kunisawa Onsen.

The article is a staff contribution, though published on the ministry’s site, and I have not read the town history itself. None of this links the old records to a current spring name.

### How it got its name

The National Diet Library’s column on how Sounkyo became a famous sight gives the fullest account:

- Before the 20th century the gorge seems to have been called *Nisei*, an Ainu common noun meaning a gorge, the library says, citing Yamada Hidezō.
- **1872.** Takabatake Toshiyoshi of the Hokkaido Development Commission named the area near Ōbako and Kobako "Hakogawa".
- **1910.** Ōta Ryūtarō, head of Aibetsu village, named it "Reizan Hekisui" and "Reizankei". The library notes that Shioya Tadashi, adopted son of the first operator of Shioya Onsen, seems to have been closely involved.
- **1921.** Ōmachi Keigetsu visited and named it Sounkyo, after a nearby settlement called Sōunbetsu. The library says the Ainu *Sōunpetsu* means "a river with waterfalls".
- The spring was first called Shioya Onsen after its discoverer, and became Sounkyo Onsen once the name Sounkyo took hold.
- **1927**, chosen among the Hundred Views of Japan; **1934**, part of Daisetsuzan National Park; **1967**, the Kurodake ropeway and lift opened.

The ministry ranger’s article dates Ōta’s naming to around 1911, the library to 1910. Both agree on Ōmachi Keigetsu in 1921. I give both years.

## The water

I could not find an official spring name, number of sources, flow, temperature or pH for Sounkyo Onsen as a town. I looked on the tourism association’s top and stay pages, the environment ministry’s Hokkaido office and Kamikawa town’s pages. One day-bathing facility gives a description of its own water, but that is one facility, not the town, so I do not use it.

What the association does say is that Sounkyo has "the largest onsen town in the Daisetsuzan range". The way to find out what is in the water is the analysis that every public bath has to post; how to read it is in the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## How the town works

### The Ice Fall Festival

The association’s festival page, which says the 2026 festival has ended, gives:

- **51st festival:** 24 January (Saturday) to 8 March (Sunday) 2026, at a special site in Sounkyo Onsen, Kamikawa.
- **Hours:** 17:00 to 21:30 on weekdays, 11:00 to 21:30 on Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, last entry 21:15.
- **52nd festival:** 23 January (Saturday) to 7 March (Sunday) 2027.
- **What is on:** taiko drumming, Ainu dance and a rice-cake scatter on the stage, an ice slide, ice-climbing to try, and an ice bar on weekends and holidays.

The festival asks for a contribution towards costs. The page does not say whether the amount includes tax, so I do not give it.

The association’s top page still carries an older event-calendar entry with different dates for the 51st festival. I follow the festival’s own page. The same page also holds notes from past years, such as a cancellation under a Hokkaido state of emergency, so only the dates under its main heading are current.

## Getting there

Checked on 12 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

**By bus from Asahikawa.** The association gives about 110 minutes from Asahikawa station by Dōhoku Bus. JNTO, on an older page, gives about 2 hours by direct bus, one or two an hour. The association says bus times changed from July 2026 and may change without notice.

**By train and bus from Sapporo.** The association gives about 1 hour 25 minutes by limited express from Sapporo to Asahikawa, about 45 minutes on to Kamikawa, and about 35 minutes by bus from Kamikawa station to Sounkyo. There is no railway to Sounkyo itself. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation gives about 30 minutes by bus from JR Kamikawa station. I give both.

**From Asahikawa Airport.** The association lists no direct service, and says taxis do airport transfers.

**By car.** The association gives about 205 km from Sapporo and about 66 km from Asahikawa by Route 39. Hokkaido’s tourism organisation gives about 25 minutes from Kamikawa-Sōunkyō interchange on the Asahikawa-Monbetsu road.

**In winter, for the festival.** The festival’s access page gives:

- **By car:** 25 km, about 30 minutes, from Kamikawa-Sōunkyō interchange by Route 39. There is a free car park at the site.
- **By train:** from JR Kamikawa station, take the Dōhoku bus from the Kamikawa Mori no Terrace bus stop in front of the station, get off at Sounkyo Onsen, and walk about five minutes. The timetable is a PDF that I did not open.

The page says nothing about winter road closures. The festival page also links to the Attaka Onsen-gō from Asahikawa Airport and the Hyōbaku Yumeguri Liner from the north exit of Sapporo station; I did not open those links, so I cannot say what they offer.

## What people get wrong

- **"The Ice Fall Festival starts in November."** An old entry on the association’s top page gives November dates. The festival’s own page gives 24 January to 8 March 2026, and 23 January to 7 March 2027 for the next one.
- **"The water comes from the volcano."** The weather agency explains the gorge walls through Daisetsuzan’s pyroclastic flow, but says nothing about the springs.
- **"Sounkyo is an Ainu name."** It was given by Ōmachi Keigetsu in 1921, after a settlement called Sōunbetsu. The National Diet Library says the Ainu *Sōunpetsu* means a river with waterfalls.
- **"Reizan Hekisui was 1910" (or 1911).** The library says 1910, the ministry ranger’s article around 1911.
- **"There is a train to Sounkyo."** The line stops at Kamikawa; the last stretch is by bus or taxi.
- **"Sounkyo’s water type is well known."** I could not find an official town-wide spring name.
- **"The water treats a condition."** This page makes no health claims.
