*Gensen kakenagashi* — roughly, "source water, poured and let flow" — is one of the most prized phrases on a Japanese onsen sign or website. It is also one of the least explained in English. I could not find a JNTO, tourism agency or environment ministry guide for visitors that sets out the difference between free-flowing and recirculated water. This page tries to: what the term means, who defines it (no one in law, as far as I could find), what an onsen is actually obliged to tell you about its water, and why some baths are filtered and chlorinated. It does not grade or rank any bath.

## What it is

### The idea

Water from the spring is fed into the bath, overflows, and is not used again. That is the core of every definition below. The opposite is a recirculating bath (*junkan*), where water is drawn out, usually filtered, and returned.

### No legal definition

As far as I could check, there is no official definition of *gensen kakenagashi*. The Hot Springs Act requires onsen to post certain information, and its enforcement regulations list what — and "free-flowing" is not one of the items. The environment ministry’s overview of the Act says nothing about free-flowing water, recirculation, disinfection or Legionella. Japan’s consumer agency and Fair Trade Commission have taken action over onsen advertising, but over water that was not onsen, or not a therapeutic spring, rather than over the meaning of *kakenagashi*. When the government discussed what onsen should post in 2005, the phrase came up, but no definition was set.

This is a limited finding: I read the laws in summary, not word for word, and absence is hard to prove. But I found nothing that defines it.

### Who does define it

Two non-legal definitions exist.

**The Japan Hot Spring Association**, a private industry body, published a glossary on 11 December 2015 with three levels:

| Term | What the association says |
|---|---|
| *Onsen kakenagashi* (onsen fully discharged) | Fresh water is fed in continuously and allowed to overflow, and the overflow is not reused. The fresh water is onsen water. Water may be added or the water heated, but if so the operator must say so and why |
| *Gensen kakenagashi* (source fully discharged) | As above, but because it is an emphatic claim that suggests something closer to the natural state than plain *kakenagashi*, no water may be added |
| *Gensen 100% kakenagashi* | As above, and because it suggests something closer still to the natural state, neither added water nor heating is allowed |

The page does not mention chlorine, disinfection or sterilisation. Under the association’s terms, "100% source water" is about dilution, heating and reuse — not about whether the water is disinfected. I have not checked word for word how the association treats heating at the middle level.

**Beppu city**, in its onsen guide, uses four terms: *gensen* (the source is on the site), *hikiyu* (piped in from a source elsewhere), *kakenagashi* (spring water sent into the bath without controlling the volume, passing through once) and *junkan* (the supply is adjusted and the drained water filtered and reused). So a Beppu listing might read "source, free-flowing", "piped, free-flowing" or "piped, recirculated". The page does not say these terms rest on any law or bylaw; they are the city’s guide information.

Oita prefecture also points to a voluntary "onsen chart" (*onsen karute*) run by a Beppu quality-assurance association and certified by the city tourism association. It shows things the law does not require, such as whether the water is recirculated, heated or diluted and by how much, whether additives or disinfectant are used, the bath volume, how much spring water goes in, how often the water is changed, and Legionella test results.

I found no national or local government certification scheme for *kakenagashi*, and could not confirm whether the Japan Hot Spring Association runs one now.

## The rules

### What the sign must say

The Hot Springs Act (Article 18) requires anyone offering onsen water to the public to post, somewhere easy to see, the water’s components, contraindications, precautions for bathing or drinking, and other information set by ministry ordinance. The analysis is done by a registered body, every ten years according to the ministry’s overview, and the sign is updated within 30 days of a new result.

Since 24 May 2005 the ordinance (now Article 10 of the enforcement regulations) has also required four more things, each only **if the operator does it**:

1. **Adding water** (*kasui*) — that it does so, and why.
2. **Heating** (*kaon*) — that it does so, and why.
3. **Recirculating** (*junkan*) — that it does so, including whether it filters, and why.
4. **Adding bath additives or disinfecting** (*shōdoku*) — the name of the additive or the method of disinfection, and why. Additives you can obviously see, such as sweet-flag (*shōbu*) leaves or yuzu, are excluded.

The 2005 wording above is from the ministry’s leaflet of the time; I confirmed in summary that the current article has the same four items, but not word for word. Not posting, or posting something false, carries a fine of up to ¥300,000.

### How the ministry explained it

The environment ministry’s 2005 leaflet for operators adds detail that is useful when you are standing in front of the sign.

- **Example reasons.** For adding water: the source is very hot, the water is strongly acidic, to make up for a shortage of supply, or only in hot weather. For heating: to keep a suitable bathing temperature, for hygiene because there is a storage tank, or only in cold weather. For recirculation: hygiene, or protecting the spring and hygiene. For disinfection: hygiene, using chlorine (or ozone, ultraviolet light, silver ions and so on), or to meet the prefecture’s bathhouse bylaw. These are examples; the operator writes its own.
- **Where.** Somewhere guests can easily see, preferably the changing room. If baths are treated differently, a plan should show which notice goes with which bath, and a facility with both onsen and non-onsen baths should make the difference clear.
- **What is not covered.** Guests topping up a bath themselves. Disinfection other than chlorine, such as ozone, *is* covered if used.
- **Saying "none".** A facility that does none of the four does not have to post anything new. Adding a line saying so is optional.

That last point is the one to remember. The duty is to post what *is* done. A sign with no line about heating does not prove the water is unheated. The ministry also encouraged — but did not require — extra information such as how much water is added, whether it is tap or well water, the source’s flow and pH, and how often the bath is cleaned and refilled. A 2005 government subcommittee considered making the dilution ratio compulsory and decided against it, because the effect on the water’s components did not differ much and the figures vary.

## Why some water is recirculated and chlorinated

### The hygiene framework

Under the Public Bath Act a bathhouse needs a prefectural licence, and the operator must take the hygiene measures that each prefecture sets by bylaw. The national government provides the model: the health ministry’s guidelines for public baths (most recently revised on 10 December 2020) and its 2003 technical guidance on preventing Legionnaires’ disease. I did not check any individual prefecture’s bylaw, or how inns covered by other laws are treated.

What those national documents say, in outline:

- **Change and clean.** Bath water should in principle be fully changed and the bath cleaned every day; where that is difficult, at least once a week. Filters should be backwashed at least weekly to remove biofilm.
- **Chlorine.** Where chlorine is used, the free residual chlorine should usually be around 0.4 mg/L and not above 1 mg/L, under the 2020 guidelines. The 2003 guidance gives 0.2–0.4 mg/L and a maximum of 1.0 mg/L. The two documents differ; I have not confirmed which figure is applied today.
- **Legionella.** None should be detected (under 10 cfu per 100 mL) in the bath water or the source water. Testing is at least once a year for baths without filters or changed daily, twice a year for baths used day after day, and four times a year if they are not chlorinated.
- **Tanks and pipes.** Storage tanks should be kept at 60°C or above. The 2003 guidance says biofilm in filters and pipes of baths used continuously is what lets bacteria grow.

### Why chlorine is not always used

The guidelines recognise that chlorine does not suit every spring: some low-pH water gives off toxic gas with it, some water rich in organic matter makes dosing difficult, and in some baths so much spring water flows in that a residual level cannot be held. In those cases the guidelines say to change the water completely every day and clean and disinfect the bath and any pipes thoroughly. For high-pH water, chlorine alone needs testing to show it works, a slightly higher level, or monochloramine instead. They also say the interaction between chlorine and onsen components should be studied beforehand, and that some standards need not apply to onsen or well water where meeting them is difficult and there is no hygiene risk.

Free-flowing baths are not exempt from care. The guidelines note that they too may have storage tanks or complex piping that need attention.

### Neither is officially better

The environment ministry’s draft on labelling, published for comment in 2005, set out both sides: recirculation and filtration help hygiene and protect the spring, but volatile components are easily lost and filtering can leave iron and other components on the equipment, changing the water. Water added or heated can also change it.

The subcommittee’s answer to public comments that year was plain. The bathing conditions depend on the nature of the spring, the number of bathers and how the bath is maintained, and neither recirculating filtration nor free-flowing is, in its words, a method that is unambiguously superior to the other. Properly maintained filtration, it added, is an important means of protecting spring resources and keeping the bath hygienic. One published comment asked that free-flowing water not be praised one-sidedly while filtration was disparaged, since free-flowing use can exhaust springs.

## Reading the sign

- **Find the analysis sheet** — the ministry suggests the changing room as the place for it. The type, temperature and pH are explained in the separate piece on onsen water types.
- **Look for the four items.** Added water, heating, recirculation (and filtration), additives or disinfection, each with a reason. Lines like "heated only when the weather is cold" are normal; the ministry’s own example reads much like that.
- **Check which bath.** Where baths are treated differently the notice should say which is which, and some facilities have baths that are not onsen water at all.
- **Do not read silence as "none".** If a point is not mentioned, the honest reading is "not stated".
- **Treat "100%" claims carefully.** In a 2003 study summarised for a 2004 ministry council, the Fair Trade Commission said that calling water "100% source" or "100% natural onsen" when it is diluted, heated or filtered risks misleading customers. It added that a fair number of customers take "natural onsen" (*tennen onsen*) to mean water with none of these, so a "natural onsen" claim should come with information about them, and that any indications shown should say whether they are based on the source or on the bath water.

The public cases are about something more basic. In 2004 the Commission warned a bathhouse that advertised "natural onsen" for heated well water. In 2015 the consumer agency ordered an inn operator to correct advertising that presented its baths as onsen water when only two types of bath actually used onsen water. The agency’s 2016 casebook lists further examples — a sports club’s "natural radium onsen" made from heated tap, well and industrial water, a lodging where heated well water was described as "free-flowing (partly recirculated)", and a ryokan’s "high-quality onsen from 1,300 m underground" that was heated tap water. In each case the problem was water presented as onsen that was not, or as therapeutic when it was not. None was a ruling on what *kakenagashi* means.

## In English

The English guides I checked do not explain any of this. Neither of the two JNTO onsen pages I checked mentions free-flowing water, recirculation, filtration, chlorine or added water. The environment ministry’s 2019 English booklet on onsen use covers spring types, indications and bathing precautions, but not free-flowing water, recirculation or disinfection. JNTO and a Hokkaido transport bureau guide explain *kake-yu* — pouring water over yourself before getting in — which is a different thing from *kakenagashi*.

Where the phrase does appear in English, it is in local descriptions. In the tourism agency’s database of multilingual commentary, written by local councils, Nozawa Onsen’s public baths are described as fed by local springs that flow continuously, and its Furusato no Yu as a *gensen kakenagashi* bath, without a definition (both FY2018). A text for a footbath in Ureshino (FY2020) calls it pure, free-flowing spring water and says adding cold water would count as an additive and could cost it the label. That is a local description, and it does not cite a rule.

## What people get wrong

- **"Kakenagashi is a legal grade."** No law or ministry document I checked defines it. The best-known definition is a private association’s.
- **"100% source water means no chlorine."** The association’s three levels deal with dilution, heating and reuse. Disinfection is not part of them.
- **"No notice means nothing is done."** The duty is to post what is done. Saying "none" is optional.
- **"Recirculated or chlorinated water is bad water."** The official position is that neither method is unambiguously superior, and filtration and disinfection are hygiene tools that the national guidelines describe in detail.
- **"Free-flowing needs no hygiene work."** The guidelines say free-flowing baths can have storage tanks and pipes that need management, and where chlorine cannot be used they call for changing the water every day.
- **"Kake-yu and kakenagashi are the same."** One is what you do before bathing; the other is how the bath is supplied.
- **"The famous labelling scandals were about kakenagashi."** The enforcement cases were about water that was not onsen, or not therapeutic, being presented as if it were.
