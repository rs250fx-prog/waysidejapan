Yuda Onsen is a hot spring district inside Yamaguchi city, the prefectural capital, a short train ride from Shin-Yamaguchi. Unlike a valley or seaside onsen town, it sits in one of the prefecture's main nightlife and dining areas, and Yamaguchi's tourism site suggests staying at a business hotel and going to the onsen after work. Its mascot is a white fox, from the story of how the spring was found. This page covers the water and why it is hot, the stories and dates of its beginning, the footbaths and day baths, food and the way in. It uses the Yuda Onsen inns' cooperative, Yamaguchi's tourism site, Yamaguchi city's tourism site, the Kitsune no Ashiato visitor centre's site, the prefecture's environmental health research centre, a 2016 conference abstract and the farm ministry. Where they disagree, I give each. It does not rate any bath or inn.

What "alkaline simple spring" means is in the guide to [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/). For reading the analysis sign, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### What the cooperative says

The inns' cooperative gives an **alkaline simple spring**, with **2,000 tonnes a day** rising and a top temperature of **72°C across its seven sources**. Its posted analysis is for a source called "Yuda Onsen Mix", at 63.6°C and pH 9.14, with 0.596 g/kg of dissolved residue and a bath temperature of about 41°C. **The analysis does not give a year.** The cooperative says that in 1984 a system was set up to collect water from each source into storage tanks and pipe it to the inns and hotels.

Yamaguchi's tourism site gives the same type and "about 2,000 tonnes a day". It says the Yunoya building lets you see, up close, a source pumped from about 500 metres down, and that it has a drinking fountain.

### Hot for Yamaguchi

The cooperative says that non-volcanic sources mostly stay below 42°C, but that Yuda, though non-volcanic, is above 70°C, the highest in the prefecture.

Two official sources help put that in context.

- **The environment ministry's onsen use table** (end of March 2025) gives Yamaguchi 404 sources, of which 276 are below 25°C, about seven in ten, and only 20 are 42°C or above. That is the prefecture as a whole; the reading is mine.
- **A paper by the prefecture's environmental health research centre**, covering the springs it analysed from 1951 to 2004, counts 479 sources in the prefecture, with Yamaguchi city having the most, 139 (29%). It gives the prefecture's highest temperature as **73.4°C, in Yamaguchi city, in 2003**, and finds 47 of the prefecture's 48 sources at 42°C or above in the central area.

The paper fits the cooperative's claim, but it says "Yamaguchi city", not Yuda, and its figures go up to 2004.

### Where the heat comes from: two accounts

**The cooperative's account.** Citing research at Yamaguchi University's graduate school, without naming it, the cooperative says groundwater flows widely along active faults into rift-like zones, passes through heavily fractured intrusive rock to a deeper heat source, and rises again near the surface. It frames this as what "may be" the secret of the volume and the heat.

**A conference abstract.** At the 2016 meeting of the Japan Geoscience Union, Nishiyama, Tanaka (Yamaguchi University), Suzuki (Central Research Institute of Electric Power Industry) and Takahashi (AIST) reported on Yuda's water. In summary:

- The water is all of the sodium chloride type, and its oxygen and hydrogen isotopes suggest it **began as rain**.
- Its lithium-chlorine ratio makes a role for present-day seawater unlikely.
- Its carbon isotopes point to carbon from outside the soil, close to magmatic values, while its helium and lithium isotopes show **no supply from the mantle or other deep sources today**.
- The authors conclude that **a volcanic fluid, or a deep fluid from the subducting slab**, was added to the rainwater-derived water, which then stayed under Yuda long enough to exchange isotopes with the rock.
- They estimate the highest temperature the water has experienced at about 70°C, close to the present temperature, and think it **rose quickly through fractured intrusive rock**, a dyke about a metre wide among them.

The two agree on one point: fractured intrusive rock as the way up. "Active faults and rift zones" do not appear in the abstract, and I could not find the source the cooperative cites. The cooperative says "non-volcanic"; the abstract speaks of a possible volcanic or slab-derived deep fluid. Both are an industry body's and a conference presentation's accounts, not a public body's, and I found no official explanation. So I do not call Yuda simply volcanic or non-volcanic.

### "Bihada no yu"

The cooperative and the prefecture's tourism site call Yuda a *bihada no yu*, a "bath for beautiful skin", on the grounds of its alkalinity. No body is named as awarding it, and the environment ministry's spring types have no such class. I treat it as their own description.

Yuda is not on the environment ministry's list of designated health resort onsen areas; Yamaguchi's entries are Tawarayama and Mitsuo.

## Where it came from

### The white fox

The story is the same in outline everywhere. A wounded white fox came every night to soak its leg in a temple pond. When the priest dug out the pond, warm water came up, and with it a **golden statue of Yakushi Nyorai**. The prefecture's tourism site gives this as one theory of the origin ("there is also a theory"), and adds a tradition that the statue was stolen by thieves and came back. The cooperative adds that the fox's home was Gongen-yama, where the 24th head of the Ōuchi clan, Ōuchi Hiroyo, enshrined the three Kumano deities.

### Which century

The dates do not agree, even within one site.

- **Yamaguchi's tourism site**: a spring said to have more than 800 years of history, whose beginning is traditionally traced to the **Muromachi period**.
- **The cooperative's history page**: the spring goes back to the time of **Ōuchi Yoshioki**, the 30th head of the clan (the early 16th century).
- **The cooperative's "about Yuda" page**: an onsen that has existed for about 810 years, **since 1200**.

I give all three and choose none.

### The end of the Edo period

The tourism site and the cooperative both say that Takasugi Shinsaku, Itō Hirobumi, Saigō Takamori, Ōkubo Toshimichi, Sakamoto Ryōma and others met at Yuda to plot. The cooperative says a bath built at the end of the Edo period is called the "Ishin no yu", the Restoration bath, is said to have been used by these men and Ōmura Masujirō, and is open to anyone. That bath is in a private inn, so I do not name it. Who actually bathed there is tradition, and I have not checked it against historical records.

### Inoue Park

Yamaguchi city's tourism site says Inoue Park is on the site of the birthplace of **Inoue Kaoru**, one of the Meiji statesmen. In the last years of the shogunate it also lodged Sanjō Sanetomi and the other court nobles who fled Kyoto. The park has the Kaentei, a 2015 reconstruction of the building used for about half a year as their lodging (free to visit, 10:00 to 17:00 from April to October and 10:00 to 16:00 from November to March; closed Wednesdays, 13–15 August and 28 December to 3 January). There are also a poem stone for Nakahara Chūya, a haiku stone for Taneda Santōka and a footbath (10:00 to 22:00). The park was renamed Takada Park in the early Shōwa period and given back the Inoue name in 2012.

The cooperative adds that the poet Nakahara Chūya was born here in 1907 and that Santōka was a regular visitor.

## How the town works

### Free footbaths

Yamaguchi's tourism site lists **six free footbaths**: in front of Yuda-Onsen station, in Inoue Park, in front of the tourist information office, on Yunoka-dōri, on Yunomachi-dōri, and at Yuda Onsen Konkon Park. From the station one you can sometimes see the SL Yamaguchi steam train pass; the Yunoka-dōri one has a hand bath too. The cooperative's list gives the hours as 10:00 to 22:00 (8:00 to 22:00 on Yunoka-dōri), free, and says the hand bath, added in 2019, is the only one in Yuda.

### Kitsune no Ashiato

Kitsune no Ashiato, "the fox's footprints", is the town's visitor centre, with three footbaths, a café and sake tasting. The footbaths cost **¥200 for adults and ¥100 for primary and junior high school children**; the prefecture's tourism site states that all prices include tax (checked 13 October 2026). The tourism site gives opening hours of 9:00 to 21:30, closed on the first and third Wednesdays. But the centre's own site has a notice of **new opening hours and closing days from 1 January 2026**, and the cooperative's list gives 8:00 to 22:00. Check the centre's site for the current hours.

### Yuda Onsen Konkon Park

Opened on **1 June 2025**, Konkon Park has two day baths, Sora no Yu and Mori no Yu, a private bath and a free footbath (10:00 to 21:00). The baths open 6:00 to 20:00, last entry 19:30, and close **every Tuesday**. Fees differ for Yamaguchi city residents and others, and are shown without a tax note, so I do not give them. The park's site posted on 5 October 2026 that the hot spring in both baths was paused and then resumed, so check before you go.

### Day bathing

The Kitsune no Ashiato site's Q&A says there are **four** places in Yuda where visitors can bathe without staying. I found **no communal public bath** in the descriptions by Yamaguchi city, the cooperative or the prefecture's tourism site; the "Ishin no yu" is an inn's bath. The same Q&A puts the number of places to stay at about 30, from onsen inns to public lodges and business hotels.

### Foxes everywhere

The tourism site lists a white fox statue 8 metres tall and weighing 3 tonnes in front of the station, six fox postboxes around the town, six fat beckoning foxes on the Yunomachi shopping street and fox manhole covers. The national tourism organisation also features the statue.

## Bathing there

- **English and tattoos.** The Kitsune no Ashiato site's English page and the national tourism organisation's Yamaguchi page say nothing about tattoos or bathing manners. I found no official rule. Ask each bath or inn.
- **Compared with city baths.** I found no official Yuda source that compares its water with other baths. The cooperative says water from its sources is collected in tanks and piped to the inns; the posted sign at each bath says whether it is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected. See the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## What it is eaten with

Yamaguchi's tourism site, in its Yuda feature, lists kawara soba, fugu, Chōshū chicken, *bari-soba*, chicken-chicken-gobō and the sweet *uirō* as local food.

- **Chicken-chicken-gobō.** Fried chicken and burdock. The farm ministry lists it for the whole prefecture and says it began around 1995 from a school-lunch recipe contest. Its Yamaguchi story says the dish was **born in Yamaguchi city** and spread through the prefecture, and that *uirō* has been loved in Yamaguchi city since the Muromachi period. On Yuda's side, a "chicken-chicken-gobō gathering" is announced for 17 October 2026 at Konkon Park, organised by the Yamaguchi city destination campaign committee. The ministry does not name Yuda Onsen, so I say both name the dish and the city.
- **Fugu.** The farm ministry places fried fugu in the Shimonoseki and Hagi areas, and its Yamaguchi story treats fugu as food of the Japan Sea side, putting Yamaguchi city in the central, Inland Sea area. The link between Yuda and fugu is the tourism site's alone.
- **Kawara soba.** It was born at Kawatana Onsen in Shimonoseki. The tourism site and the national tourism organisation list it as Yamaguchi food you can eat around Yuda. More in the [Setouchi local dishes guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-setouchi/).

## Getting there

Checked 13 October 2026; most pages carry no date. Fares are not on this page.

- **By train.** The Kitsune no Ashiato Q&A gives Shin-Yamaguchi to Yuda-Onsen station on the JR Yamaguchi line in **18 minutes by local train or 9 minutes by limited express**, then 10 minutes on foot to the town. The prefecture's tourism site says "about 20 minutes by train" in one place; its town page gives about 15 minutes by car or about 40 minutes by bus from Shin-Yamaguchi. The figures differ partly because some include walking or waiting.
- **By car.** About 15 minutes from Yamaguchi or Ogōri interchange on the Chūgoku expressway.
- **By air.** Yamaguchi Ube airport, 1 hour 30 minutes from Haneda, then about 40 minutes by car.
- **Onward.** From the Yuda Onsen-dōri bus stop, about an hour to Hagi and about 40 minutes to Akiyoshidai and Akiyoshi-dō.

I found no official times from Yamaguchi station, Izumo-shi or Yonago. For the other spring towns in this region, see [Kaike](/en/soak/kaike/) on the Tottori coast, [Yubara](/en/soak/yubara/) in Okayama, and the [Misasa](/en/soak/misasa/) and [Tamatsukuri](/en/soak/tamatsukuri/) guides.

## What people get wrong

- **"Yuda is officially the hottest spring in Yamaguchi."** The cooperative says so; the prefecture's research centre names Yamaguchi city, not Yuda, with data to 2004.
- **"It is non-volcanic, full stop."** That is the cooperative's word. A 2016 conference abstract describes a possible volcanic or slab-derived deep fluid.
- **"The spring is 800 years old."** One source says so; others give 1200, the Muromachi period, or the time of Ōuchi Yoshioki.
- **"There is a public bathhouse like other onsen towns."** I found none described; there are four day-bathing places and the footbaths.
- **"Fugu is Yuda's dish."** The farm ministry ties fugu to Shimonoseki and Hagi.
- **"Kawara soba comes from here."** It comes from Kawatana Onsen; you can eat it in Yuda.
- **"Bihada no yu is a certification."** It is a description, not an official spring class.
