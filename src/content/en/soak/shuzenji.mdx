Shuzenji is a spa town in a valley along the Katsura river, in Izu city, Shizuoka, on the Izu peninsula. Its founding spring sits in the river and you cannot bathe in it; the town grew up around a temple with a legend about the monk Kūkai, and it is also where, by tradition, a shogun was killed in the bath. This page uses the spa town’s official site, Izu city, the temple, Shizuoka prefecture, the environment ministry, the geopark and JNTO. Where they differ, each is given. It does not rate any bath or inn.

One spelling point first. The temple is written 修禅寺, with the character for Zen; the place is 修善寺. Both read *Shuzenji*. The spa town’s official site warns that more and more people mistake one for the other. On this page, "Shuzen-ji" means the temple and "Shuzenji" the town.

For spring types and temperature bands, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/); for the notices about added water, heating and recirculation, see the piece on [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/). Nearby on the east coast are [Atami](/en/soak/atami/) and [Itō](/en/soak/ito/).

## Where it came from

### Kūkai and the Tokko no Yu

The founding story is a legend, and the sources tell it the same way. Izu city’s tourism site says Kūkai, the monk also known as Kōbō Daishi, saw a boy washing his sick father in the Katsura river, was moved, and struck a rock with his *tokko*, a ritual implement, so that a healing spring rose. The father bathed and recovered, and from there, the story goes, bathing as a cure spread. The spa town’s official site and the temple tell the same story and give 807 for the founding of the temple. The temple cites the *Izu Fudoki* for this; this page has not checked the *Fudoki* itself.

The temple also says, separately, that Kūkai trained at the inner sanctuary, Okunoin, in 791, aged 18. It adds a note on reading: the spring is *Tokko no Yu*, while the implement is usually read *dokko*.

### Minamoto no Yoriie

The second story belongs to the Kamakura period. The temple says that Minamoto no Yoriie (1182–1204), who became the second shogun after his father Yoritomo died, lost his post in a struggle between the Hōjō and Hiki clans and was sent to Shuzenji, and that he is said to have been attacked in the bath by assassins from the Hōjō family and killed.

The sources word this differently:

- **The temple** says it "is told" that Hōjō assassins killed him while he was bathing.
- **The spa town’s sightseeing page** states it outright: he was assassinated in the Hako-yu at the hand of Hōjō Tokimasa.
- **The spa town’s hot spring page** calls the Hako-yu the legendary spring in which Yoriie is said to have been bathing.

This page treats it as tradition, and follows the temple in saying Hōjō assassins rather than naming one man. The spa town’s site adds that Hōjō Masako built the Shigetsuden hall to pray for her son, and that it is said to be the oldest wooden building in Izu. Both the temple and the spa town’s site say Minamoto no Noriyori, Yoritomo’s brother, is said to have been confined at Shuzenji too.

### Hōjō Sōun

After war and fire, the temple was rebuilt by Hōjō Sōun and became a Sōtō Zen temple. The temple’s site and the spa town’s site give different years for the fire and the rebuilding, and in one of them the era names and Western years do not match each other. This page gives no year, only that Hōjō Sōun rebuilt it after war and fire.

### "The oldest in Izu"

Izu city’s tourism site says Shuzenji "is said to be" the oldest spa town in Izu; the spa town’s official site says it is "also said to be" the oldest in Izu. These are the sites’ own words, not a ranking with anyone named as choosing it. No official page calls it the oldest in Japan.

### Writers

The spa town’s site names Natsume Sōseki, whose Chinese poem gave the Gyōkūrō tower its name; Shimaki Kensaku, for the short story *Akagaeru* ("Red frog"); and Okamoto Kidō, for the play *Shuzenji Monogatari*. The temple says Kidō wrote the play after seeing a mask of Yoriie, and that he stayed in 1908. The temple and the geopark also name Akutagawa Ryūnosuke.

### The volcano

The Izu Peninsula Geopark says the spa town lies in a valley at the foot of the Daruma volcano, which erupted repeatedly until about 500,000 years ago. Along the Katsura river, it says, there are deposits from undersea volcanoes, layers that sat beneath the Daruma volcano and have been exposed by erosion, and Shuzenji’s water rises from inside these layers.

The geopark’s page on the temple’s Okunoin describes Mount Daruma as a large land volcano active from about 800,000 to 500,000 years ago, part of a "Daruma volcano group" with the Ōsezaki and Ida volcanoes. The two pages name it slightly differently. No official source ties Shuzenji’s water to the Amagi volcano or to the East Izu volcano group.

## The water

### The prefecture’s survey

Shizuoka prefecture’s hot spring survey, as of 1 February 2023, has a row headed "Shuzenji" within Izu city. It counts **89** sources, but that total includes sources no longer in use, dry or buried. **17** are in use, all pumped, with a total flow of about **2,065 litres a minute** and an average temperature of about **62.1°C**.

The table does not say whether "Shuzenji" means an administrative district or the spa town, so this page does not call these the spa town’s figures. The same table has Atami at 522 sources. How many a place has depends on where its edges are drawn, so this page does not rank them. The table has no column for spring type.

### Central distribution

An environment ministry case study on using hot spring heat describes how the spa town is supplied. Water from **six** sources is gathered into a tank at a first distribution station and sent on through a second, a centralised system that began in June 1981 and is run by the Shuzenji Onsen business cooperative. It gives the water as a **simple spring** at **60.1°C**. The PDF does not say when the figures were taken; its file data dates from 2019.

The six sources here and the prefecture’s 17 in use are not the same set: one is what the cooperative distributes, the other a district row in a survey. Official sources, national, prefectural or city, give no table of spring types for Shuzenji. What a simple spring is, and why it can still be hot, is in the guide to [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/).

## How the town works

### The Tokko no Yu

The Tokko no Yu rises by the Katsura river and is called the spring where Shuzenji Onsen began. It is still set in natural stone. The spa town’s official site is clear that you **cannot bathe in it or use it as a footbath**, because it is not a bath under the law. It has no closing day and no parking.

### Two free footbaths

The spa town’s hot spring page lists two footbaths, both free:

- **Kawara-yu**, in the small park in front of the Tokko no Yu, for six people at a time. It is named after a shared bath of that name that once stood nearby.
- **River Terrace Sugi no Yu**, in the Tokko no Yu park, a Japanese-modern terrace with tables, for ten at a time. It takes its name from Sugi no Yu, one of the old outdoor baths.

Izu city’s tourism site points visitors from the Tokko no Yu to these two.

### The Hako-yu

The spa town’s site says that there were once seven *soto-yu*, baths outside the inns, along the river, and that between 1945 and 1954 only the Tokko no Yu was left. The **Hako-yu** was built to bring back a walk between outdoor baths, and is named after the spring linked to Yoriie. It has an indoor bath only. The site gives hours of 10:00 to 21:00, closing on irregular days, and on the second Thursday of each month, except August, it opens from 15:00 for maintenance.

The fee table has separate rates for adults, children and residents, but does not say whether tax is included, so this page does not give a price. Beside it stands the **Gyōkūrō**, a 12 m lookout tower, free to enter.

### The bamboo path

The *Chikurin no Komichi*, "bamboo grove path", follows the Katsura river on from the Tokko no Yu, with round bamboo benches along the way, lit up until 23:00. The spa town’s site says it won a top urban landscape award in fiscal 1997, without saying who gave it.

### Shuzen-ji temple

The temple is Sōtō Zen. Its own site gives entry to the grounds as free, the gate as open from 5:00 to 17:00, and the office and treasure hall as open 8:30 to 16:30. The spa town’s site notes that the water at the temple’s purification basin runs hot from a dragon’s mouth.

## Bathing there

Official sources give no English guidance for Shuzenji on tattoos or how to bathe, and no comparison of its water with day spas or bathhouses in the cities.

- **Language.** The spa town’s official site has no English pages of its own, only a link to Google Translate. English text on Izu city’s tourism site has not been checked.
- **Tattoos and manners.** The spa town’s Japanese pages on its baths and footbaths say nothing about tattoos. The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn or bath.
- **Compared with city baths.** What the ministry’s case study does describe is how the water reaches the town: gathered from six sources and distributed from a central station.

## What it is eaten with

**Wasabi.** JNTO’s page on Shuzenji Onsen says the area is known for wasabi and its many wasabi products. A separate JNTO article on the *tatami-ishi* method of growing wasabi in Izu, which it describes as a UN-recognised world agricultural heritage system, ends by suggesting a visit to Shuzenji Onsen. That is the only official source that ties the spa town itself to wasabi.

The farm ministry’s list of Shizuoka’s local dishes has *wasabi-zuke*, pickled wasabi. It names Izu city among the areas where it is passed down, together with Shizuoka city, Gotemba and Oyama, but does not mention Shuzenji. It says wasabi growing began at Utōgi in Aoi ward, Shizuoka city, that the method reached Izu in the mid-18th century, and that the *tatami-ishi* system was developed in Naka-Izu. Shizuoka prefecture’s food site, under Izu city, mentions the plentiful water from the Amagi range and wasabi, suggests *wasabi-don* (rice with grated wasabi), and also lists wasabi-zuke, salt-grilled *ayu* (sweetfish) and *Izu-shika*, local venison. For the dish across Shizuoka, see [local food of Chūbu](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/).

**Shiitake.** The spa town’s sightseeing page lists *Shiitake no Sato*, where you can pick shiitake mushrooms and have a barbecue. This comes only from the spa town’s own site.

These times have not been checked against JR East’s, the bus company’s or the Izuhakone Railway’s own pages, and the sources used here give no official time from Shinjuku or Hakone.

## What people get wrong

- **"You can bathe in the Tokko no Yu."** You cannot, and it is not a footbath either. Use Kawara-yu or the River Terrace Sugi no Yu next to it.
- **"Shuzenji is the oldest onsen in Japan."** The official sites say it is said to be the oldest in Izu. No official page says Japan.
- **"Kūkai founded the spring in 807."** That is the legend, as the temple and the city tell it. This page has not checked the *Fudoki* the temple cites.
- **"Yoriie was certainly killed in the Hako-yu."** The spa town’s sightseeing page says so; its hot spring page calls it a legend, and the temple says it "is told".
- **"The temple and the town are spelt the same."** The temple is 修禅寺, the town 修善寺.
- **"Shuzenji’s heat comes from the Izu volcanoes that are active today."** The geopark places the springs in old undersea deposits at the foot of the Daruma volcano. No official source links them to Amagi or the East Izu group.
- **"Wasabi is officially Shuzenji’s dish."** The only official source that ties the spa town to wasabi is JNTO. The farm ministry names Izu city, not Shuzenji.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide.
