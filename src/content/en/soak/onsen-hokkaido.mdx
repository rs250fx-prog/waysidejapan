Hokkaido is the prefecture with the most onsen areas in Japan, on the environment ministry's own figures. It is also where you can see, more plainly than almost anywhere, a hot spring town sitting next to the volcano its water is said to come from. But "Hokkaido is volcanic, so its onsen are volcanic" is a sentence I could not find an official source for. This page sets out what the figures say, which towns explain their water through a volcano and which do not, and how the main towns began. The individual town guides go deeper; for how to read a spring's name and the sign on the wall, start from the onsen overview.

## Where the hot springs are

The environment ministry publishes a yearly table of hot spring use by prefecture. The latest, for fiscal 2024 (as of the end of March 2025), gives Hokkaido:

- **226 onsen areas.** The ministry counts a place as an onsen area when it has somewhere to stay.
- **2,249 springs** in total. Of those in use, 405 flow naturally and 879 are pumped; another 482 natural and 483 pumped springs are not in use.
- **740 places to stay**, with room for 124,348 guests.
- **13,579,988 overnight stays** in the year. The ministry marks this figure as a reference value.
- **437 public baths** using hot spring water.

The table has no ranking column. Comparing its prefecture figures: Hokkaido has the most onsen areas (226, ahead of Nagano's 193 and Fukushima's 122) and the most overnight stays (ahead of Shizuoka's 10,576,818). On springs it is third, behind Oita (5,094) and Kagoshima (2,735). The national totals are 2,839 onsen areas and 27,899 springs.

A count of onsen areas is a count of places with lodging, not a measure of quality. I would not read more into it than that.

## Why here

Some of the answer is volcanoes, and the official sources say how much.

**The volcanoes.** The Sapporo regional office of the Japan Meteorological Agency says Hokkaido has 31 active volcanoes, 11 of them in the Northern Territories. It watches nine around the clock with instruments: Meakandake, Tokachidake, Tarumaesan, Usuzan, Hokkaido-Komagatake, Atosanupuri, Taisetsuzan, Kuttara and Esan. That page does not talk about hot springs.

**Noboribetsu and Kuttara.** The agency describes Kuttara as a stratovolcano that, roughly 80,000 to 45,000 years ago, erupted repeatedly with pyroclastic flows and formed a round caldera 3 km across. Noboribetsu volcano is a post-caldera volcano on its western foot. Hiyoriyama is a lava dome that still gives off steam and hot water, and Jigokudani ("Hell Valley") and Oyunuma are a group of craters made by steam explosions. The latest eruption is estimated at about 200 years ago. The agency's page does not mention Noboribetsu Onsen by name.

The town's tourism association makes the link itself: it says much of Noboribetsu's water comes from Jigokudani, which it describes as formed by volcanic activity about 10,000 years ago. That date is the association's; the agency's page does not give one for the craters.

**Toya.** The Lake Toya Onsen tourism association says the hot water of Toyako Onsen and Sobetsu Onsen came up in an eruption in 1910, and that the town was born from it. During that eruption, it says, a seismometer was taken to an erupting volcano for the first time in the world, which is why the area is called "the birthplace of modern volcanology". The page I read does not name the volcano, so neither do I.

**Kawayu.** The Lake Mashu tourism association says the water is heated by magma under Mt Io (Atosanupuri) in the middle of the Kussharo caldera, flows through the rock underground and comes up where the town stands. It calls Kawayu "a famous spring born from Japan's largest caldera". The name Atosanupuri, it says, means "bare mountain" in Ainu.

**Jozankei.** Jozankei's own pages do not explain how its water rises, and do not connect it to a volcano. I have not filled that gap.

I could not find a ministry or prefectural count of which spring types are most common in Hokkaido, and I could not open the geological survey material that would map volcanoes to hot springs across the island. So this page does not describe a Hokkaido-wide pattern.

## The towns

These are sketches, not guides. Each town's own page is the place to go further.

**Noboribetsu.** The association says Noboribetsu is famous for having "as many as ten" kinds of spring water and lists them: sulphur, salt (chloride), alum, Glauber's salt (sulphate), green vitriol, iron, sodium bicarbonate, radium and simple springs, plus one name I could not interpret. Several of these are older names: salt spring is today's chloride spring, Glauber's salt spring is a sodium-sulphate spring, bicarbonate of soda spring is a hydrogen-carbonate spring, and iron spring is today's iron-bearing spring. "Radium spring" is not one of the ministry's official categories. So the ten is the association's count, and it is not the same thing as the ministry's ten categories.

On history the association gives two starts and does not choose between them. In 1857 Okada Hanbei, a merchant from Ōmi, built a lodging hut for bathers while mining sulphur in Jigokudani. In 1858 Takimoto Kinzō, a carpenter from Edo whose wife had a skin complaint, built a bath hut and, with permission to keep the spring, opened an inn; the association calls him the founder of bathing here. A Yakushi (medicine Buddha) hall followed in 1861. This site does not choose which counts as the opening.

**Karurusu**, near Noboribetsu, has its own dates on the same timeline: a spring found by Hino Aiki in 1886, a road cut by Hino Kyūkitsu and the spring opened in 1897, and "opened" again in 1899, with no explanation of the difference. The association says the water was known to the Ainu as a medicinal spring long before.

**Jozankei.** The association describes the water as a sodium chloride spring, neutral, hypotonic and high-temperature, colourless with a mild saltiness. It gives 56 sources, a flow of 8,600 litres a minute and a temperature at the source of 60 to 80°C, which appear to be figures for the whole town. The founder is said to be a mountain ascetic monk, Miizumi Jōzan, who opened the spring in Keiō 2; the association's home page counts 160 years since. It is "said" that he reached the source with Ainu guides.

**Kawayu.** The association names the water an acidic, sulphur-bearing, iron(II)-sodium-sulphate-chloride spring of the hydrogen sulphide type, and says it is one of the few volcanic spring types in Japan. About its history it says only that the town has more than a hundred years behind it.

**Sounkyo.** The Sounkyo tourism association says the Ainu were the first to know this place, passing along the Ishikari river while hunting, and called it Kamuy-mintar, "the garden where the gods play". Its pages do not tell the history of the spring.

**Akan.** The Akan tourism association says Ainu tradition is strong on the lakeshore and that visitors can take part in activities at Akan Ainu Kotan. I could not find the history or the water type of the spring on its pages.

**Tokachigawa.** The association calls its water a "moor onsen" that rises from a peat layer and has been chosen as Hokkaido Heritage. It does not give a spring name, and I could not find an official definition of a moor spring as a category.

Hokkaido's tourism organisation describes some waters in its own words: "rare tea-brown waters" at Teshio and "exceptionally rare oil-bearing waters" at Toyotomi. Those are promotional descriptions. I could not find how the official classification treats oil in spring water, so I have not placed Toyotomi in a category.

**National health resort onsen.** The environment ministry designates a small number of onsen areas as *kokumin hoyō onsenchi*, places it considers suitable for health and recuperation. Its list shows five in Hokkaido: Karurusu, the Niseko onsen district, Ashibetsu, Naganuma and Toyotomi. The dates beside them on the list are when each area's plan was drawn up or revised, not when it was designated, and I could not find the designation years. Towns that are missing from this list are simply missing from it; that is all the list says.

## How people use them

**In winter.** The tourism organisation's winter page features an open-air bath and footbath on the ice at Lake Shikaribetsu Kotan in Shikaoi, a village it describes as appearing on the frozen lake each winter; a night walk through the streets of Noboribetsu; and a plan at the roadside station Tappu no Yu in Shinshinotsu that pairs a bath with smelt fishing. Sounkyo holds its Ice Fall Festival in winter; the festival's own page gave the 51st as 24 January to 8 March 2026 and the 52nd as 23 January to 7 March 2027 (checked 12 October 2026). An older date range still shown on the association's top page is out of date. More is in the [Sounkyo guide](/en/soak/sounkyo/). Akan offers frost flowers and walks on the frozen lake. I could not find official descriptions of snow-viewing baths, and I have not used the phrase.

**With food.** Tokachigawa's tourism association lists *butadon*, a bowl of grilled pork over rice, among the town's local food. The farm ministry's local-food database says butadon is thought to have begun in the early Shōwa era at a diner in Obihiro that put charcoal-grilled pork under a sauce like the one used for grilled eel, that Obihiro is said to be its birthplace, and that it is handed down mainly in the Tokachi area. The ministry does not mention Tokachigawa Onsen, so the honest link is that you can eat a Tokachi dish near a Tokachi onsen, not that the onsen invented it. For the other towns on this page I could not find a dish that both the town and the ministry name.

**Reading the sign.** I found no Hokkaido source that explains how bathing in an onsen town differs from a day spa in a city. What applies everywhere is the national rule that a bath open to the public must post its water's analysis and say whether, and why, the water is diluted, heated, recirculated or disinfected. Jozankei being about 50 minutes by car from Sapporo is a fact about distance, not about the water.

## Getting there

These are the times the towns' own pages give, checked on 10 October 2026. Every page warns that timetables change, so check the operator before you travel. Fares are not on this page.

- **Noboribetsu** — From Sapporo by JR limited express about 1 hour 10 minutes, or by intercity bus about 1 hour 20 minutes. From New Chitose Airport by reserved direct bus about 1 hour to 1 hour 15 minutes. From Hakodate by limited express Hokuto about 2 hours 25 minutes. Some air and ferry details on the page are marked as of November 2021.
- **Jozankei** — By car about 50 minutes from Sapporo. There are buses from Sapporo; check times with the operator, as the association notes that some connections from Makomanai station do not work.
- **Toya** — From New Chitose Airport by JR limited express about 1 hour 30 minutes to Toya station, then about 20 minutes by bus or taxi. From Sapporo station by reserved Donan bus about 2 hours 45 minutes.
- **Sounkyo** — From Asahikawa station by Dohoku bus about 110 minutes. From Sapporo, limited express to Asahikawa about 1 hour 25 minutes, on to Kamikawa about 45 minutes, then bus about 35 minutes. Bus times changed in July 2026 and may change without notice.
- **Akan** — From Kushiro Airport by Akan bus or the Akan Airport Liner about 65 minutes. The page's summary and detail sections give slightly different times.
- **Kawayu** — There is a JR Kawayu Onsen station on the Senmo line with buses to the town. The association gives no journey time.
- **Tokachigawa** — From JR Obihiro station by Tokachi bus route 45 about 28 minutes; some runs are weekdays only and need booking. There is no direct bus from Sapporo.

## What people get wrong

- **"All Hokkaido onsen are volcanic."** Some towns tell their story through a volcano. Jozankei does not, and I found no official source for the general claim.
- **"Most onsen areas means the best onsen."** The count is of places with somewhere to stay. On springs Hokkaido is third.
- **"Noboribetsu has all ten official spring types."** It has ten names on the association's list, and they are not the ministry's ten.
- **"Toya's springs are ancient."** The association dates them to an eruption in 1910.
- **"A moor spring is an official category."** It is how Tokachigawa describes its water. I could not find it in the official classification.
- **"The water will treat a condition."** The ministry's rules list indications by spring type and say treatment at a spa is best done with a doctor's guidance. This page does not claim any town's water cures anything.
