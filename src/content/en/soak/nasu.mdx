Nasu Onsen, in the town of Nasu in northern Tochigi, is not one spring but a group scattered over the slopes of Chausudake, an active volcano. Its oldest bath, Shika no Yu, still has people bathing in very hot water by a set routine, and a short walk away is Sesshōseki, the "killing stone" of a fox legend. This page explains how it works using the town, Tochigi prefecture, the tourism association, Shika no Yu’s own site, the weather agency, the national geological survey and JNTO. Where sources disagree, each is given with its source. It does not rate any bath or inn.

Nasu has no environment ministry plan as a national health resort onsen. The ministry’s list shows only two plans for Tochigi, Okunikkō-Yumoto and Itamuro. So there are fewer official figures for Nasu than for a town like [Zao](/en/soak/zao/) or [Naruko](/en/soak/naruko/). For what the spring types mean, see the guide to [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## Where it came from

### The white deer

The founding story is told by several sources, with small differences.

- **Nasu town** says that, according to legend, in the time of Emperor Jomei (629) Kano Saburō Yukihiro, who lived at the foot of the mountain, shot a white deer and followed it. In a valley at the foot of Chausudake he found the deer healing its arrow wound in a spring welling out of the ground. "This is said to be the first discovery of the spring."
- **The tourism association** puts it around 630. In its version Saburō, following the deer he had failed to kill into a misty valley near today’s Shika no Yu, met an old white-haired man who said he was the god of the spring and told him where to look. Saburō named the spring Shika no Yu, "deer’s bath", built a shrine to the spring, now Nasu Onsen Shrine, and offered the deer’s antlers. The association says the shrine is said to have been founded in 630.
- **Shika no Yu’s own site** says the spring is said to have opened in Jomei’s reign, in the first half of the seventh century, about 1,300 years ago.
- **Tochigi prefecture** says it is said to have been found nearly 1,400 years ago because a deer wounded in a hunt was healing itself there. Its page of local legends tells the same story of the hunter and the white deer.

629 and "around 630" are the same legend written two ways, and "about 1,300" and "nearly 1,400 years" are two ways of counting from it. None of it is a record.

### The first record

The town says the name Nasu Onsen appears in the *Suruga no Kuni Shōzeichō*, a provincial tax ledger of 738, so the spring was already well known in the Nara period. The tourism association calls the same Shōsōin document the first written mention. Shika no Yu’s site puts it most plainly. Officially, it says, the record of Nasu Onsen is in a Shōsōin document of 738, in the reign of Emperor Shōmu. So: a legend dated 629 or 630, and a record from 738.

### The move to Yumoto

The town and the tourism association both say that on 14 June 1858 (Ansei 5) heavy rain set off a debris flow that struck the settlement along the Yukawa stream, and the hot spring town moved up to the higher ground where Yumoto stands today.

### Sesshōseki

Sesshōseki, the "killing stone", is a historic site, not a spring. The town calls it a famous place tied to the legend of a golden nine-tailed fox with a white face. The story is set in the Heian period, but the town says it first appears in writing in the *Kagakushū* of 1445. In the legend the fox turned into a stone, and when the monk Gennō rebuked it, the stone split in three. The poet Matsuo Bashō visited and wrote of it in *Oku no Hosomichi*. The town’s two pages tell the details a little differently, and this page treats it as a legend, not history.

On 18 March 2014 Sesshōseki was designated a national Place of Scenic Beauty as one of the landscapes of *Oku no Hosomichi*. The town says a distinctive smell of sulphur still hangs there. It lies 200 m north of the Onsen Shrine.

### The volcano

The Japan Meteorological Agency lists Nasudake, also called Chausudake, 1,915 m, as a volcano under constant observation. It is one of a group of stratovolcanoes lined up north to south along the Sekiya fault. The agency says Chausudake began to form about 16,000 years ago, has many fumaroles in and around the central crater of its lava dome, with active steaming in two explosion craters on the west slope, and that every eruption on record has been explosive and prone to mudflows. The national geological survey, AIST, says the eruption of 1410 is said to have killed 180 people, and notes that hot spring resorts, golf courses and ski slopes lie around the volcano.

Neither says the springs’ water comes from the volcano, and no official source says so. This page calls Nasu a group of springs at the foot of an active volcano and no more.

## The water

### No total figure

Official sources do not give the number of sources, the total flow or the overall pH for Nasu. Without a ministry plan, the figures available are partial.

- **Nasu town** gives the Yumoto source, about 100 m south of Sesshōseki along the Yukawa, as 800 litres a minute at 68°C, with no year. It calls it the birthplace of today’s Nasu Onsen and says it is designated a natural monument.
- **The tourism association** says Nasu’s source temperatures are high, 63 to 80 degrees, and gives the water as sulphur. It also mentions private wells drilled deep underground at individual facilities, which this page does not use as figures for the whole area.

### Spring types by district

The tourism association gives a spring type for each district. Several are simple springs, low in minerals, which the [page on simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) explains.

- **Yumoto (Shika no Yu)** — sulphur.
- **Ōmaru, Kita, Benten, Yahata** — simple.
- **Shin-Nasu** — simple, source at 70°C.
- **Takao** — sulphate-bearing hydrogen sulphide spring, 40°C (an older style of name).
- **Sandogoya** — alkaline simple.
- **Ashino** — alkaline simple, pH 9.85.

The association gives no years for these.

### Shika no Yu’s label

Shika no Yu’s own site names its water a simple acidic sulphur spring (hydrogen sulphide type), acidic, hypotonic, high-temperature. It explains that "acidic" means pH under 3, which changes with the pH at the moment the water comes out, and "high-temperature" means 42°C or more. It gives no pH figure. The tourism association simply says sulphur. How acidic and sulphur springs are classed is in the page on [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/).

## The seven springs, three ways

"Nasu’s seven springs" (*Nasu nanayu*) is a common phrase, but three official sources list different seven.

- **Nasu town** — Moto-yu (Shika no Yu), Ōmaru, Kita, Benten, Takao, Sandogoya and Itamuro. It says Asahi Onsen opened in the Meiji era and Shin-Nasu, Yahata, Iimori and Kakkō were added from the Taishō era on, making the "eleven springs of Nasu".
- **The tourism association** — the seven found by the end of the Edo period were Shika no Yu, Itamuro, Sandogoya, Ōmaru, Kita, Benten and Takao. Yahata followed in Meiji, Asahi, Iimori and Kakkō in Taishō, then Shin-Nasu, and the group was sometimes called the "twelve springs". Today, it says, leaving out Itamuro, which is far away, "eleven springs" is the usual name. Asahi, Iimori and Kakkō now have no inns or facilities and are only sites. So some people call Shika no Yu, Sandogoya, Ōmaru, Kita, Benten, Takao and Yahata the seven, and add Shin-Nasu to make eight.
- **Tochigi prefecture** — Shika no Yu was found in the seventh century, and with Yumoto, Ōmaru, Kita and Takao, plus Itamuro and Sandogoya in Nasushiobara city, they came to be called the seven. Yahata, Shin-Nasu and others were developed from the Meiji era on. Benten is not on its list, and it reaches seven only by counting Shika no Yu and Yumoto separately.

No official body is named as having chosen the seven. "Sesshō" is not one of them in any of these sources. It is the stone.

## How the town works

### Yumoto

The prefecture describes Yumoto, on the southern foot of Chausudake, as the centre of Nasu Onsen, where modern hotels and old *tōji* inns (for people staying days or weeks to bathe) stand side by side. Shika no Yu is its shared bathhouse, a single-storey wooden building. Its own site says it was rebuilt in 1941 and keeps that form. The prefecture says scenes of *tōji* bathing, stirring the water and *jikan-yu* (timed bathing), can still be seen there.

### Shika no Yu

From Shika no Yu’s own site, as checked on 11 October 2026:

- **Hours** — 8:00 to 18:00, last entry 17:30. The tourism association’s list gives 8:00 to 17:30, perhaps giving last entry as closing time, though that is not confirmed. This page uses the bathhouse’s own figures.
- **Closing days** — open all year in principle, but closed on irregular days for maintenance. A notice dated 12 September 2026 is headed "closing days for October 2026". Its content is not confirmed here, so check before you go. An old closure notice from about 2022 is still on the home page; it does not describe current hours.
- **Payment** — cash only. No credit cards.
- **Parking** — about 50 cars.
- **Soap and shampoo** — not allowed. The bathhouse says they lather poorly in sulphur water and make the floor slippery.

The site lists fees, but they are not given here, because it does not say whether they include tax. The same goes for its coupon books and lockers.

### Bathing by the clock

Shika no Yu explains its routine in Japanese. In summary:

- There are baths at 41, 42, 43, 44, 46 and 48°C. The women’s side has no 48°C bath.
- Before getting in, pour water over your head with a ladle, about 200 times for adults and about 100 for children. Bring your knees close to the edge, lower your head, and pour quietly without splashing water out of the bath.
- The first time, go in only to the chest, for two or three minutes, then get out and rest. From the second time, go in fully for two or three minutes, and repeat.
- One session is about 15 minutes in all, and up to four sessions a day is the guide.
- Afterwards, do not rinse off, and keep from getting cold.

It lends hourglasses free of charge. JNTO’s English page warns that Shika no Yu is piping hot. The bathhouse also says what it believes the water helps with, which this page does not repeat.

### Other baths

The tourism association has a search of day-bathing places by district (Yumoto, Oku-Nasu, Takao, Shin-Nasu, Kōgen and Ashino) and by spring type, with each place’s hours. This page does not list them.

On shared baths, the association says that after rebuilding in the Edo period five bathhouses were used as shared baths. Once inns with their own baths appeared in the Taishō era they became the norm, and the shared baths moved and stayed on for local people. Official sources do not give how many there are now or what they charge.

## Bathing there

The tourism association’s pages, Shika no Yu’s site and JNTO’s Nasu page give no tattoo guidance for Nasu, and no official Nasu source compares its water with baths in the cities.

- **Language.** An English version of the tourism association’s site is not confirmed, and Shika no Yu explains its routine only in Japanese.
- **Tattoos.** The general picture is in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn or bath.
- **Compared with city baths.** What Shika no Yu does set out, officially, is a bathing method from the *tōji* tradition, with six temperatures and timed sessions.

## What it is eaten with

No dish is tied to Nasu Onsen by both the farm ministry and the hot spring side.

- **Dairy.** Tochigi prefecture calls areas such as Nasu a "dairy kingdom", with volcanic ash soil and a cool climate suited to dairy farming, and says Tochigi has been second only to Hokkaido in raw milk production since 1999. But the tourism association’s pages on the hot springs and on Nasu’s attractions do not mention dairy, and no official page puts the two together.
- **Shimotsukare**, a Tochigi dish, is on the farm ministry’s list, but its page does not mention Nasu.
- **Battō-jiru and dango-jiru**, flour dumplings in soup, are on the ministry’s list as names used in the former Nishinasuno area of Nasushiobara city, and the ministry says Nasu town’s chamber of commerce made a leaflet of places serving them. The page does not tie them to the hot springs.

## Getting there

As checked on 11 October 2026. Fares are not on this page.

- **Shinkansen.** The tourism association gives 75 minutes from Tokyo to Nasushiobara by the *Nasuno*, and 75 minutes from Sendai by the *Yamabiko*. JNTO also gives about 75 minutes from Tokyo.
- **Bus to the springs.** From Nasushiobara, the association names the Kantō Jidōsha bus (formerly Tōya Kōtsū) to Nasu-Yumoto. JNTO says the bus towards the Nasu Ropeway passes through the hot spring area and takes 40 minutes to an hour, depending on where you get off. The current timetable is not confirmed on the bus company’s site.
- **Local trains.** JNTO gives about 3 hours from Ueno to Nasushiobara on the Utsunomiya line. Official sources give no time from Utsunomiya.
- **Car.** The association gives 152 km and 1 hour 37 minutes from Kawaguchi junction, and 179 km and 1 hour 55 minutes from Sendai-Miyagi interchange.

JNTO’s Nasu page carries a COVID-19 note and is evidently not recent, and these times have not been checked against JR East’s own pages.

## What people get wrong

- **"Sesshō is one of the springs."** Sesshōseki is a stone and a historic site, not a spring.
- **"The seven springs are a fixed list."** The town, the tourism association and the prefecture list different seven, and no body is named as having chosen them.
- **"Nasu was founded in 629."** That is the legend’s date, given as 629 by the town and around 630 by the association. The first record is from 738.
- **"The water comes from the volcano."** It may, but no official source says so.
- **"All of Nasu is sulphur water."** Yumoto is sulphur; the association lists several districts as simple or alkaline simple springs.
- **"Shika no Yu opens every day."** It closes on irregular days for maintenance, and there is a notice of closing days for October 2026.
- **"The water cures things."** This page does not say so. The official list of indications for each spring type is in the water types guide, along with the environment ministry’s note that spa therapy is best done under a doctor’s guidance.
