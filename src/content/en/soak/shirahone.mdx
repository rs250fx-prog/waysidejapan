Shirahone Onsen is a small cluster of inns in a valley in western Matsumoto, Nagano, about 1,400 m up on the flank of **Mt Norikura**. Its water comes out clear and turns milky white. This page uses the environment ministry's plan for the spa, Matsumoto city, the Japan Meteorological Agency, the Shirahone Onsen website (the site does not say who runs it), Nagano's official tourism guide, government papers from 2004 and the bus timetables. Where they disagree, both are given. It does not rate any bath or inn.

For the spring types named below, see [hydrogen-carbonate, sulphate, iodine and simple springs](/en/soak/hydrogen-carbonate-sulphate-iodine-simple-springs/) and [acidic, sulphur and radioactive springs](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/). For reading the sign at any bath, see [onsen water types](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/).

## The water

### A national health resort

Shirahone is one of the environment ministry's **national health resort hot springs** (*kokumin hoyō onsenchi*). The ministry's list, as of October 2022, gives the designation date as **30 March 1974** and the name simply as "Shirahone Onsen"; the Norikura Kōgen plateau nearby is not on the list. The ministry's plan for Shirahone, revised in September 2020, places it on the Yu river at about 1,400 m, at the southern end of the Hida mountains, inside the Chūbu-Sangaku National Park.

### Fourteen sources

The plan says **14 sources** are in use, most of them flowing naturally, with a total of **2,500 to 2,600 litres a minute**, shared by 11 inns and two day-bathing facilities. It names the spring types as **calcium-magnesium hydrogen-carbonate spring, sodium hydrogen-carbonate spring, and sulphur spring (hydrogen sulphide type)**.

Its table of sources has 15 rows, one marked as not in use, and gives:

| Item | Range |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 31.0 to 49.7°C |
| Flow per source | 4.0 to 1,730 litres a minute |
| pH | not given |
| Year | not given (owners measure temperature and flow once a year) |

Official sources do not give a pH. The Shirahone site says milky springs are usually strongly acidic, but that Shirahone's is weakly acidic, close to neutral; it gives no figure.

### Why it turns white

Three sources explain the colour, in slightly different terms:

- **The ministry's plan**: the water is clear when it comes out and gradually turns milky white; it is rich in calcium carbonate.
- **Matsumoto city**: the white is calcium carbonate, and the water is clear just after it emerges.
- **The Shirahone site**: on contact with air, hydrogen sulphide becomes fine particles of sulphur and calcium bicarbonate becomes fine particles of calcium carbonate.

Matsumoto city also says three conditions overlap here: a heat source to warm groundwater, such as **geothermal heat from volcanic activity**; calcium carbonate dissolved from limestone; and a fault fracture zone. It says the travertine deposits are up to 30 m thick, cover 14.7 hectares and were laid down over roughly the last **8,000 years**. The Shirahone site speaks of signs that water was flowing about 30,000 years ago, without saying what that is based on.

### Mt Norikura

The Meteorological Agency keeps **Mt Norikura** (3,026 m at Kengamine) under constant observation. Its summary describes a compound stratovolcano that has erupted at least 14 times in the last 10,000 years, most recently about 500 years ago, and says there is **no fumarolic area at the summit**. On 13 October 2026 its status page showed a **volcanic forecast at alert level 1**, with the note to remember it is an active volcano. The alert-level system for Norikura began on 18 March 2019 and covers Matsumoto and Takayama.

The ministry's plan places Shirahone "in the lap of Mt Norikura", and Matsumoto city gives volcanic heat as one example of a heat source. Neither says that Norikura's activity heats Shirahone's water, and the agency's summary of Norikura does not mention the spa. This page does not join them.

## Where it came from

### Two accounts of the early days

The ministry's plan and the Shirahone site agree that the water was already flowing when the **Kamakura Ōkan** road was opened in the Kamakura period, and that nobody knows who found it. After that they differ:

- **The ministry's plan**: in the Edo period, miners from the Ōoke silver mine who suffered from mine poisoning are said to have bathed here; by the mid-18th century domain samurai came too, and it flourished as a place to treat stomach complaints. The first inn is said to have been opened in the Genroku era (1688–1704) by Saitō Magozaemon, from Shinano.
- **The Shirahone site**: in the Warring States period, **Takeda Shingen** opened a silver mine at the foot of Mt Norikura, and the spring was used as a "hidden spring" (*kakushi-yu*) to heal wounded soldiers and mountain workers.

So the mine story is Edo-period miners in the ministry's plan and Shingen's mine in the site's account. "Hidden spring" is the site's phrase; the plan does not use it. This page gives both and chooses neither.

### The name

The ministry's plan gives **several theories**: one says the name was once *Shirafune*, "white boat", after a bath carved from a great tree that turned white and boat-shaped with deposits; another says branches coated in lime looked like bones (*hone*). It says the present name, Shirahone, became known across Japan after the spa appeared in **Nakazato Kaizan's** popular novel *Daibosatsu Tōge*, whose "Shirahone" chapter praised the scenery. The Shirahone site says that in the Meiji period the reading was still unsettled between Shirahone and Shirafune, and that Kaizan's use of Shirahone in the Taishō period fixed it. Official sources do not give the chapter's publication year.

### One year or three

A saying goes that three days in the water keeps colds away. The ministry's plan, citing a travel essay by the poet **Wakayama Bokusui**, gives "for **one year**". The Shirahone site gives "for **three years**". This page records the saying, not its effect.

### The 2004 bath-additive case

Government papers record the following, and this page gives only their dates and the measures taken:

- **Mid-July 2004**: news reports began. The environment ministry learned of them on 12 July, asked Nagano prefecture's pharmaceutical affairs section to check, and received its report on 14 July (government written answer to the House of Councillors, 10 August 2004). The place was then Azumi village, Minami-Azumi district.
- **19 August 2004**: Nagano prefecture issued a **written caution** to four businesses, the Shirahone Onsen ryokan association and three inn operators, on the grounds that their pamphlets and websites showed photos and wording of spring water coloured with a bath additive, which could breach Article 4 of the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations (misleading as to quality). It asked for a reply on improvements by 31 August (Fair Trade Commission summary in a ministry committee paper, 22 November 2004).
- The same written answer said the Hot Springs Act does not directly regulate adding bath additives, that additive components cannot be called components of the spring, and that the government did not consider an immediate change to the Act necessary.
- **24 May 2005**: an amended ministerial ordinance (promulgated 24 February 2005) began requiring baths to post whether, and why, they add water, heat it, recirculate it, add bath additives or disinfect it. The ministry's leaflet says the change followed cases the previous year of bath additives being used without notice; it does not name Shirahone.

The ministry's 2020 plan and the Shirahone site's pages on the spring and its history do not mention the case.

## How the spa works

### A valley of inns

The ministry's plan describes a hollow about 1.5 km north to south and 700 m east to west, holding **11 inns**, day-bathing facilities (one of them public), two drinking fountains and a tourist information point. Each inn has a *yugō*, a nickname describing its water. Nagano's official tourism guide calls Shirahone a handful of ryokan with no central townscape.

### The public open-air bath

The Shirahone site's page for the **Shirahone Onsen public open-air bath** (*kōkyō noten-buro*) gives, as checked on 13 October 2026:

- **Price, tax included.** Adults 700 yen; children of primary-school age and under 400 yen.
- **Hours.** Day bathing 10:00 to 16:00 on weekdays and 9:00 to 17:00 at weekends and on public holidays. The same page also shows "10:00 to 16:00" on its own, so check before you go.
- **Closed.** Thursdays, and for the winter from 25 November to mid-April.
- **The bath.** Separate men's and women's open-air baths, free-flowing.

Some inns and eating places also take day bathers, each with its own hours and closing days; their prices are not given here because they do not say whether tax is included.

**Mixed bathing.** Nagano's official tourism guide describes the bath at Awanoyu as one where men and women can bathe together. The public open-air bath is separated by sex.

### The spring mounds

Matsumoto city says the spa's **spring mounds and spherical limestone** became a national natural monument in 1922 and a **special natural monument** on 29 March 1952, with an addition on 10 March 2020. An observation deck and path were built in 2023. The ministry's plan also names nearby gorges and the Ryūjin falls among the sights.

### Through the year

- **Fireworks.** The ministry's plan gives 8 August every year; the Shirahone site announced the 2026 display for Saturday 8 August at 20:00.
- **Winter.** The site says the road is open to cars all year. The public open-air bath closes for winter, and the Alpico bus switches to a winter timetable from 16 November 2026, which is not covered here.

## Bathing there

### How the Shirahone site says to bathe

The English page on the spring gives a bathing method: pour water over yourself first, legs, then arms, then belly; stay about 15 minutes in a lukewarm bath and about 5 in a hot one; **do not rinse off with fresh water afterwards**; rest for 30 minutes; bathe no more than three times a day. The Japanese page presents this as a method for health; this page does not repeat claims about effects. The environment ministry's general guidance on rinsing, including its note for sensitive skin after sulphur springs, is in the [sulphur springs guide](/en/soak/acidic-sulphur-radioactive-springs/).

### Tattoos and English

The Shirahone site has English pages, among them the front page, the spring and access. Neither its Japanese nor its English pages give a tattoo rule. The ministry's plan lists improving how inns receive overseas visitors, and multilingual signs, as work to do.

Nagano's official tourism guide, writing about the whole prefecture, says tattoos have long been banned, that about 30% of facility and hotel managers do not refuse them according to the Japan Tourism Agency, and suggests covering a small tattoo with tape or using an inn's private bath for a large one. More in the tattoos section of the [water types guide](/en/soak/onsen-water-types/). Ask each inn before you book.

### Compared with city baths

No Shirahone source compares its water with baths elsewhere. The 2005 posting rule applies everywhere, in spa towns and cities alike: any bath that adds water, heats, recirculates, uses bath additives or disinfects must say so on its notice. For what to look for, see [*kakenagashi*](/en/soak/kakenagashi-explained/).

## Food

**Onsen-gayu.** Both the ministry's plan and the Shirahone site name rice porridge cooked in the spa's water. The plan says inns often serve it at breakfast. The site calls it Shirahone's speciality: originally a home dish, it became known after inns began serving it to guests with weak stomachs. It is not among the 29 Nagano dishes on the farm ministry's list of local dishes.

**Soba.** An eating place on the Shirahone site lists *tōji soba*, and the farm ministry describes *tōji soba* from Nagawa under hand-made soba, a dish of the whole prefecture. The ministry's page does not mention Shirahone, so this page goes no further than the menu. For Nagano's dishes, see the [Chūbu local food guide](/en/eat/kyodo-ryori-chubu/).

## Getting there

As checked on 13 October 2026. Fares are not on this page, because the timetables do not say whether they include tax.

- **By car.** The Shirahone site gives about an hour from the Matsumoto interchange, and says you can come from Matsumoto or Takayama.
- **By train and bus from Matsumoto.** On Alpico's timetable for **17 April to 15 November 2026**, the 7:15 train from Matsumoto reaches Shin-Shimashima at 7:49; the 8:00 bus reaches the Sawando bus terminal at 8:38; the 8:55 Shirahone bus arrives at Shirahone Onsen at 9:10 and Awanoyu at 9:15. That is about 1 hour 55 minutes with two changes. The 13:29 and 14:47 departures arrive at 15:10 and 16:30, about 1 hour 40 minutes.
- **Back.** The 9:25 from Awanoyu reaches Sawando at 9:44, Shin-Shimashima at 10:31 and Matsumoto at 11:22.
- **From Takayama or Hirayu.** Nōhi Bus's Matsumoto–Takayama line, run with Alpico, leaves Matsumoto bus terminal at 7:40 and reaches Sawando at 8:46 and Hirayu Onsen at 9:08. The times fit the 8:55 Shirahone bus at Sawando; official sources do not say whether the connection is guaranteed.
- **From Tokyo.** JNTO gives 2 hours 40 minutes from Shinjuku to Matsumoto.

Nagano's official tourism guide says Shirahone is an hour by car or bus from Takayama or Matsumoto. The bus timetables show 1 hour 40 minutes to 2 hours from Matsumoto with changes, so use the timetable.

For a neighbouring spa on the Gifu side of the same mountains, see [Okuhida Onsen-gō](/en/soak/okuhida/); elsewhere in Nagano, see [Togura-Kamiyamada](/en/soak/togura-kamiyamada/), [Yudanaka-Shibu](/en/soak/yudanaka-shibu/) and [Nozawa](/en/soak/nozawa/).

## What people get wrong

- **"The water comes out white."** Matsumoto city and the ministry's plan both say it is clear at first.
- **"Mt Norikura heats the water."** The plan places Shirahone on Norikura's flank and Matsumoto city names volcanic heat as one possible heat source, but no official source says Norikura heats Shirahone.
- **"It is Norikura Kōgen."** The ministry's health resort list names Shirahone Onsen; Norikura Kōgen is not on it.
- **"Three days keeps colds away for three years."** That is the Shirahone site's version; the ministry's plan, citing Bokusui, says one year. Neither is a medical finding.
- **"It was Shingen's hidden spring."** That is the Shirahone site's account. The ministry's plan ties the mine story to Edo-period miners.
